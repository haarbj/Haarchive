### Extracted bullets

- **[OVERLAP — Livingston/HITSYSTEM training-adaptation graphic]** Costill (*Distance Running*, TAFNEWS, 1979) chart, credited "image courtesy Keith Livingston" and bearing the HITSYSTEM logo: an aerobically-trained athlete can run on fat fuel at 80% of capacity — capacity that itself has risen — versus roughly 50% of capacity pre-training, without depleting carbohydrate stores. This is the same "trained aerobic base shifts fuel mix toward fat, sparing glycogen" logic already covered via the acid/alkali buffering and Steady State material, but adds a specific 50%→80% capacity figure and is the one slide in this range explicitly sourced to Livingston/HITSYSTEM (both flagged in existing-coverage.md as not-yet-covered attribution categories).

- **[NOVEL]** Nancy Clark, RD (Boston Marathon Sports Med conference, 2017), cited by Cucuzzella: Kenyan elite runners' diet is roughly 75% carbohydrate (including ~20% as white sugar in tea) and 10% protein — offered as a rebuttal to "carbs are evil."

- **[NOVEL]** Carbohydrate intake table (IOC/AND Evidence Analysis Library, via Clark): moderate exercise (~1 hr/day) 2.5–3 g carb/lb bodyweight (5–7 g/kg); endurance exercise (1–3 hr/day) 2.5–4.5 g/lb (6–10 g/kg); extreme exercise (>4–5 hr/day) 3.5–5.5 g/lb (8–12 g/kg). For a 150 lb athlete that's 375–975 g carbs/day (1,400–3,800 carb-calories/day). This is a different, more granular sourcing of the same carb-guidance ballpark the site already cites from Thomas et al. 2016 (8–12 g/kg/day) — same number range, new citation trail (IOC/AND table via Nancy Clark) and new sub-breakdown by exercise duration.

- **[NOVEL]** Dr. Richard Wood's "alternative hypothesis": muscle-glycogen depletion drives fatigue via two independent levers — (1) prevent glycogen from getting too low (creates carbohydrate dependence) vs. (2) reduce reliance on glycogen altogether. Paired stat: available stored energy from carbohydrate is ~1,800 kcal vs. 40,000+ kcal from fat.

- **[NOVEL]** Historical anecdote: 1960s cyclist Jacques Anquetil (5-time Tour de France winner) reportedly fueled on steak, lobster, and champagne — offered as a pre-modern counterexample to carb-loading orthodoxy.

- **[NOVEL]** Chris Froome (4-time Tour de France winner) publicly credited a low-carb approach — training "low" (glycogen-depleted) twice a week over winter — for building stamina and fat-burning capacity (Telegraph, Mar. 19, 2019).

- **[NOVEL]** FASTER study citation subjects: Tim Olsen (Western States 100 course record), Zach Bitter (American 100-mile record, 11:47:21), Mike Morton (American 24-hour distance record, 172.5 miles) — presented as elite fat-adapted ultrarunners.

- **[NOVEL]** Migratory-bird anecdote: bar-tailed godwits fly 7,000+ miles nonstop (Alaska to New Zealand in 8 days per a 2008 Washington Post report), weighing under 1.5 lbs at departure, half of which is fat completely burned off during the flight — used as a fat-as-endurance-fuel metaphor.

- **[NOVEL]** Sled-dog physiology comparison: dogs run 100 miles at ~8 min/mile pace repeatedly; have ~70% more mitochondria per cell than humans (each human muscle cell holds up to ~2,500 mitochondria); cited VO2max ~300 ml/kg/min — offered as an extreme benchmark for fat-fueled aerobic capacity.

- **[NOVEL]** "Crossover point" concept (~ventilatory threshold): as %VO2max rises, carbohydrate contribution to energy rises and fat's falls, crossing over near the ventilatory/anaerobic threshold.

- **[NOVEL]** Peak fat oxidation (Achten et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 34:92-97, 2002): consensus peak fat-burning rate is ~0.5–0.6 g/min, occurring around 60–65% VO2max, with a bell-curve decline on either side.

- **[NOVEL]** "Old school" ceiling data (Venables et al., J Appl Physiol 98:160-7, 2005): peak fat oxidation across a large sample tops out near 1.0 g/min regardless of VO2max, contradicted later by the low-carb-diet athletes described below.

- **[NOVEL]** Training-adaptation fuel-mix shift (Martin et al., 1993): at a fixed submaximal intensity, pre- vs. post-training energy contribution shifts from ~40%/~38%/~21% (carb/muscle-triglyceride/plasma-FFA) to ~60%/~27%/~13% — i.e., training increases carbohydrate's *share* at that fixed intensity even while overall capacity rises, with intramuscular triglycerides (not plasma free fatty acids) supplying more of the fat portion. A genuinely different, more nuanced take than the "training just shifts you toward fat" simplification.

- **[NOVEL]** Direct quote, attributed to Dr. Petro Dobromylskyj: "The function of insulin is the inhibition of lipolysis." Paired with a chart showing fat oxidation dropping sharply as blood insulin concentration rises from 0 toward ~100 pmol/L.

- **[NOVEL]** FASTER study (Volek, Freidenreich, Saenz, et al., *Metabolism* 65 (2016) 100-110, "Metabolic characteristics of keto-adapted ultra-endurance runners"): peak fat oxidation averaged 1.54 g/min in a low-carb-diet (LCD) group vs. 0.67 g/min in a high-carb-diet (HCD) group — roughly double. Habitual diets compared: HCD ~60% carb/25% fat/15% protein vs. LCD ~70% fat/20% protein/10-12% carb. During a 3-hour submax effort at 64% VO2max, the LCD group derived ~85-90% of energy from fat throughout, versus the HCD group's fat contribution declining from ~55% down toward ~40%.

- **[NOVEL]** Same FASTER study: insulin-resistance (LP-IR) scores were markedly lower/more insulin-sensitive in the LCD group (mean ≈ 6) than the HCD group (mean ≈ 27), on a 0-100 scale where higher = more insulin resistant.

- **[NOVEL]** Cucuzzella's own self-quantification anecdote: repeat VO2max/metabolic testing on himself (age 49-50, ~140 lb, 5'8") showed peak fat oxidation rising from ~1.1 g/min ("Good Butter Burner," Jan. 2016) to ~1.9 g/min ("Butter Burning Beast," Feb. 2017) at a VO2max of 64.6-64.8 ml/kg/min, framed as a personal N=1 case for fat-adaptation training.

- **[NOVEL]** Peter Defty (low-carb athlete/coach) framework on reframing recovery from fat-adapted training: not necessarily *faster* recovery, but *less* muscle damage, improved hormonal balance, endogenous glycogen synthesis, and an improved hormetic response.

- **[NOVEL]** Peter Defty's "what you notice is what you don't notice" framing: benefits attributed to fat-adapted athletes include fewer energy ups-and-downs, less mental fog, absence of bonking/cravings, better mental focus and motor skills, lower perceived effort, and improved recovery — a named coaching heuristic for the felt experience of fat-adaptation.

- **[NOVEL]** Anecdote: "Tracy A.," a firefighter and the oldest female to complete her fire academy, cited as a case of combining HIIT training with a low-carb, high-fat (LCHF) diet.

- **[NOVEL]** Study citation (Wilson, Lowery, Roberts, et al., *J Strength Cond Res*, in press): ketogenic dieting combined with resistance training produced favorable changes in body composition, strength, power, and hormonal profiles in resistance-trained males.

- **[NOVEL]** Study citation (Francois, Gillen, Little, *Frontiers in Nutrition*, Oct. 2017): proposes carbohydrate restriction combined with high-intensity interval training as an optimal combination for treating metabolic disease.

- **[NOVEL]** Misconception addressed: the "Female Athlete Triad" (amenorrhea, anorexia nervosa, osteoporosis) is explicitly flagged "***outdated***" in favor of the broader **RED-S** (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport) framework (Mountjoy et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2014;48:491-497), which expands the triad to gastrointestinal, immunological, cardiovascular, psychological, endocrine, metabolic, hematological, and growth/development effects, and extends applicability to male athletes.

- **[NOVEL]** RED-S energy-availability formula, described as the "cornerstone" of the diagnosis: (Energy intake − Energy expenditure) / Lean mass ≥ 30 kcal/kg is the threshold for adequate energy availability; falling below it triggers the RED-S cascade.

- **[NOVEL]** RED-S recovery timeline (2014 Female Athlete Triad Coalition consensus statement): energy status recovers within days to weeks of increased intake/decreased expenditure; menstrual status recovers over months; bone mineral density recovery can take years and may lag behind both.

- **[NOVEL]** Anecdote: elite runner Tina Muir went 9 years without a menstrual period, stopped running to recover, and wrote *Overcoming Amenorrhea* — used as a cautionary case for RED-S/energy deficiency.
