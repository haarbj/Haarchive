# Batch 12 digest

## Fat Adapted Training.pdf (22 pages)

**Identified author:** Keith Livingstone — chiropractor, coach, and author of *Healthy Intelligent Training* (the site's existing second major Lydiard source). Internal evidence: every original diagram in the deck carries the HITSYSTEM ("Healthy Intelligent Training — HIT Your Goals") logo; the case-study athlete "John Meagher," described as coached by the author since 1987, matches published reporting that Meagher trains under Livingstone/HITSYSTEM and broke the Australian M55+ marathon best in the same Melbourne Marathon described here; the author's personal details (chiropractic background, brain-tumor-related seizure history, daughter "Annabel" at Melbourne University's Janet Clarke Hall) are consistent with Livingstone's own biography. This is **not** the *Healthy Intelligent Training* book — it reads as a separate personal chapter/blog piece — so per the citation convention it should be cited distinctly, e.g. `(Livingstone, Fat Adapted Training)`, not `(Livingstone, Healthy Intelligent Training)`.

**Note on scope:** Roughly the first half is genuine fat-adaptation/endurance-training material with a real Lydiard tie-in (long steady aerobic running as the mechanism for fat-adaptation). The back half drifts into general health/nutrition territory only loosely connected to running training — omega-3:omega-6 ratios, cholesterol/statin skepticism, vitamin D, "earthing," the author's own anti-seizure medication regimen, and telomere biology. These are extracted below but flagged as tangential; a synthesis pass should weigh how much (if any) belongs on a running-coaching site.

### Extracted bullets — fat-adaptation / training core

- **[OVERLAP — capillary-density study]** Mechanistic elaboration on *why* sustained low-intensity long runs build capillary density: "sustained low aerobic pressure on the capillary system invokes angiogenesis" — new capillary networks form in both the working leg muscles and the heart muscle, letting more blood reach the muscle at lower pressure and clearing CO2/lactic acid faster. Existing coverage has the Swedish-vs-Kenyan capillary-density *study result*; this adds the underlying angiogenesis mechanism explanation, not previously covered.

- **[NOVEL]** Named "Major Problem Number One: the tiny fuel tank" — glycogen depletion at roughly the two-hour mark as the physiological cause of "the wall," framed as common to marathoners, cyclists, kayakers, and triathletes alike.

- **[NOVEL]** Claim: prolonged, unsupplemented long runs (Lydiard-style, past 2 hours, no in-run carb intake) eventually let an athlete reach "near-tirelessness" at all aerobic intensities below anaerobic threshold, provided hydration/electrolytes are maintained — framed as a trainable end-state, not just a fueling tactic.

- **[NOVEL]** Costill chart (from *Distance Running*, TAFNEWS, 1979, credited in-image) that already appears in batch-11's Cucuzzella deck: untrained athletes run on ~50% sugar/50% fat at steady aerobic pace; trained athletes shift to ~20% sugar/80% fat at steady state, and the old 100%-VO2max effort becomes a fat-burning ~80% of the newly-raised trained VO2max (i.e., ~125% of the former capacity). Same chart/citation as batch-11's version, reinforcing it's a stock HITSYSTEM-circulated graphic.

- **[NOVEL]** Case study: Irish coach René Borg's own multi-year (2008-2012) steady-state pace data, shown as a bar chart of "steady state pace (kph)" rising year over year — offered as real-world confirmation of the gradual aerobic-capacity-building progression, but without the underlying numbers transcribed in the text.

- **[NOVEL]** Caveat/limit on the fat-adaptation ceiling: even well-trained, gradually-built aerobic athletes still plateau at sustaining fat-fueled efforts at only about 80% of maximal intensity in repeated lab testing — a few percentage points short of the glycogen-burning anaerobic threshold.

- **[NOVEL]** FASTER study (2015, "Fat-Adapted Substrate Oxidation in Trained Elite Runners") figures: mean fat-oxidation rate of 1.54 g/min among fat-adapted athletes and a max of 1.8 g/min in one athlete — nearly double the previously accepted "absolute human limit" of ~1.0 g/min (per Venables et al. 2005, cited as 0.45-0.75 g/min for most trained athletes). Even the *lowest* fat-adapted athlete tested (1.1 g/min) exceeded the old ceiling by ~10%. (Same FASTER study appears with different specific figures in batch-11's version — this document's numbers, framing, and study title differ slightly from Cucuzzella's citation, worth reconciling in synthesis.)

- **[NOVEL]** RQ (Respiratory Quotient) explainer as a coaching/diagnostic concept: RQ near 1.0 = pure carbohydrate metabolism (a "sugar-burner," typically depleting glycogen even at 35-50% VO2max); RQ near 0.7 = an athlete burning mostly fat at higher aerobic intensities, conserving glycogen for late-race use.

- **[NOVEL]** Case study (via Mark Sisson's *Primal Endurance*): American 100-mile record-holder Zach Bitter, lab-tested on a treadmill at 7:00/mile pace, showed 98% fat-burning while running at 75% of his Max VO2.

- **[NOVEL]** Case study: Dr. Peter Attia (physician) pre-keto burned 95% carbohydrate/5% fat at an all-day comfortable pace (HR 104 bpm); after 12 weeks of strict ketogenic eating, at the same pace he burned 22% carbohydrate/78% fat, and his 50/50 carb-fat crossover heart rate rose to 162 bpm. At his red-line anaerobic threshold, he went from 100% glycogen-fueled (pre-keto) to 70% glycogen/30% fat (post-keto), reaching threshold at a *higher* wattage than before — though his absolute all-out VO2max effort saw a slight performance decline while ketogenic.

- **[NOVEL]** Case study: amateur 70.3-triathlon world champion Sami Inkinen, after 3 months of extreme low-carb eating, shifted his fuel mix at a fixed 300-watt output (~25 mph/40+ kmh flat-ground cycling) from 95% glucose to a 50/50 glucose-fat split, doubling his fat-oxidation rate to 400 cal/hr. His calculated "time to bonk" (zero blood glucose) at 200 watts went from 5.6 hours as a sugar-burner to 87 hours after five years of fat-adapted eating and training (2014 re-test).

- **[NOVEL]** Coaching case history: John Meagher (Australian age-group runner/coach, debut marathon 2:16 in 1989, coached by the author since 1987) developed classic sugar-burner symptoms in his 50s despite decades of high-volume aerobic training — post-work fatigue, light-headedness/dizziness on long hilly runs, loss of enjoyment in long runs, suspected insulin resistance/early Type II diabetes. Diet audit found he was carb-heavy (bread, toast, cereal) with minimal vegetables. Re-adapted to fat-burning via diet: cut carbs, added coconut oil, eggs, non-nitrite bacon, and vegetables cooked in saturated fat (for fat-soluble vitamin absorption). Result: broke the Australian M55+ best marathon time by 11 seconds on October 14 at the Melbourne Marathon, 2:37:16, in warm/windy conditions with a hard final kilometer, and recovered quickly afterward.

- **[NOVEL]** Race-day fueling protocol discussed for Meagher's marathon buildup: high-carb/high-fat meal the night before racing (example given: baked potato with cream cheese or sour cream); very slow, fat-burning-intensity aerobic running maintained right up to race eve; long-weekend-run duration deliberately cut by 20% in each of the final two weeks before the race.

- **[NOVEL]** Named timeline: full dietary fat-adaptation (shifting a "sugar-burning" athlete to a fat-burning one) takes about three weeks of gradually increasing dietary fat while cutting carbohydrate.

- **[OVERLAP — Steady State/oxygen-debt physiology]** General framing that well-trained endurance athletes can hold near-anaerobic-threshold paces for "nearly two hours" before glycogen depletion becomes limiting — a different framing of the same oxygen-debt/threshold territory already covered, with the fat-adaptation angle as the new element rather than the threshold concept itself.

### Extracted bullets — tangential health/nutrition material (weak Lydiard connection)

- **[NOVEL]** Omega-3:omega-6 fatty-acid ratio claims: ideal ratio described as 1:1; modern Western/Standard-American-Diet levels commonly run 1:15 to 1:30, with one measured seminar participant (a health-conscious undergraduate) at 1:70. Attributed to a 2016 Melbourne seminar by Dr. Dan Murphy (chiropractic researcher, Life Chiropractic College West).

- **[NOVEL]** Coconut oil/MCT physiology: medium-chain triglycerides convert rapidly to ketones in the liver; ketogenic eating has reportedly been used for 90+ years in epilepsy treatment; claim that the brain can derive up to 15% more energy from ketones than from glucose.

- **[NOVEL]** Personal anecdote: author's mother-in-law "Lalli," an active post-polio survivor in her 70s, was harmed by a statin (Lipitor) prescription, becoming too weak to climb stairs; recovered over months of CoQ10 supplementation and chiropractic care.

- **[NOVEL]** Anecdote: Dr. Tim Noakes (South African sports scientist, formerly a leading pro-carbohydrate researcher) developed Type II diabetes, reversed it via a ketogenic diet and lost 44 lbs (20 kg), then publicly reversed his own carb-loading position — triggering a professional deregistration complaint in South Africa that he ultimately won.

- **[NOVEL]** Telomere-health material (secondary source: *The Telomere Effect*, Blackburn & Epel), presented as five research-backed recommendations: (1) aerobic exercise — a German study found both moderate exercise (3x/week, 45 min) and HIIT (3x/week: 10-min warm-up, 4x3-min hard/easy intervals, 10-min cooldown) doubled telomerase activity over 6 months, while resistance training alone had little telomerase effect; (2) excess sugar shortens telomeres — a 2014 study of 5,000 Americans found 590 mL/day of sugary soda linked to 4.6 extra years of biological aging, 237 mL/day linked to 2 extra years; (3) omega-3s (fish/seaweed/flaxseed) linked to longer telomeres — a 2010 UCSF study of 608 heart-disease patients found higher blood omega-3 levels predicted less telomere shortening over 5 years, and telomere-shortening patients had 39% mortality over the next 4 years vs. 12% for telomere-lengthening patients; (4) depression/anxiety/chronic stress shortens telomeres (cited: 2015 study of ~12,000 Chinese women, 2014 study of ~3,000 Dutch people); (5) avoid yo-yo dieting/excess sugar.

- **[DISCARDED — tangential/no running relevance]** "Earthing"/grounding claims (barefoot contact with earth as an antioxidant-electron source), vitamin D3/K1/K2 sourcing advice, and the author's personal anti-seizure medication (Dilantin/phenytoin) history and supplement regimen — health-memoir material with no training-methodology content.

- **[DISCARDED — boilerplate]** Closing "Further Suggested Reading" book list (Perlmutter, Teicholz, Sisson, Moore, Cuddy, Frankl, Blackburn/Epel, etc.) — bibliography, no extractable claims.

## Jogging Basics by George Sheehan.pdf (6 pages)

**Identified author:** Dr. George Sheehan — cardiologist, running writer, and "philosopher-king" of the 1970s jogging boom. Title page: "The Basics of Jogging, Dr. George Sheehan, Published 1974, Copyright The George Sheehan Trust." A wholly distinct author from Lydiard, confirmed by existing-coverage.md as a gap category (zero site coverage).

- **[NOVEL]** Central framework: reduces training prescription to three questions — "How fast? How far? How often?" — presented as "the fitness equation."

- **[NOVEL]** Coaching cue/rule: "Train, don't strain." Effort, not speed, should be the measure; the body (not the stopwatch) sets the proper pace.

- **[NOVEL]** Distinguishes "pace" (a feeling-based, comfortable effort) from "speed" (a numeric, stopwatch-based measure) — recommends training by the former.

- **[NOVEL]** Cites Gunnar Borg's 1960 theory of perceived exertion: perceived effort is "almost identical" to machine-measured physiological effort, and Borg found perceived exertion superior to any single physiological measurement as a training guide. Reproduces the Borg 6-20 RPE scale with associated pulse ranges (e.g., 12-13 "somewhat hard" ≈ pulse 120-130; add a zero to the rating for approximate pulse).

- **[NOVEL]** Names the target zone "comfortable" — the mid-range between "light" and "hard" on the Borg scale — and ties it to Bill Bowerman's "talk test" (able to hold a conversation while running).

- **[NOVEL]** Beginner progression cue: "scout pace" — alternating 50 steps walking with 50 steps running — as a bridge to continuous jogging.

- **[OVERLAP — warm-up protocol]** Prescribes the first 6-10 minutes of *every* run, even for experienced runners, be done "very slowly" to let circulation/temperature adapt and bring on the "second wind." This is the same warm-up-before-hard-effort logic as Lydiard's 5-minute warm-up rule already covered, but a different source, a different number (6-10 min vs. 5 min), and framed as pre-run adaptation generally rather than pre-hard-effort specifically.

- **[NOVEL]** Notes that ground-level pace (minutes/mile) varies with time of day (early-morning runs run ~1 minute/mile slower), heat/humidity, headwinds, hills, or psychological stress, but perceived effort stays constant — and argues physiological benefit tracks effort, not clock pace.

- **[NOVEL]** "How far" prescription: no distance target — the goal is 30 continuous minutes at a comfortable pace, run to "this side of fatigue." Claims 30 minutes puts someone in the 95th percentile for cardiopulmonary endurance, and at "12 calories per minute" contributes to weight loss, lowers pulse and blood pressure.

- **[NOVEL]** Anecdote: a 30-year-old housewife went from no running to a continuous 30-minute run within one month of starting, and completed a 5-mile race within 10 weeks of buying running shoes.

- **[NOVEL]** Names a "third wind" — distinct from the physiological "second wind" — as a psychological/flow state Sheehan says he experienced around 35 minutes into a run on a hilly stretch he calls "the river road": "a feeling of wholeness and peace and contentment... I loved myself and the world and everyone in it... The road seemed to be running me."

- **[NOVEL]** "How often" prescription: cites exercise-physiology consensus of four 30-minute sessions/week (~2 hours/week total) as sufficient for baseline fitness, while noting the field hadn't adequately studied de-training rates, so concentrating the same weekly volume differently might be equally valid.

- **[NOVEL]** Anecdote: a colleague who runs only ~2+ hours on Saturday plus a Sunday race (no other training) has broken 3 hours in the marathon and often beats Sheehan at shorter distances — offered as evidence that weekly volume distribution may matter less than physiologists assume.

- **[NOVEL]** General training-stress definition: "Training is after all simply a matter of applying stress, allowing the body to recover, and then applying stress again," with appropriate stress/recovery time varying by individual.

- **[OVERLAP — overtraining symptom clusters]** Self-monitoring heuristic distinct from but adjacent to the site's existing overtraining-symptom coverage: uses the presence/absence of the "second wind" at the 6-minute mark to distinguish laziness (usual warm sweat/energy still arrives — push through) from real physical exhaustion (cold, clammy sweat and weakness — stop and go home). Separately, tracks morning resting pulse; when it's 10 beats above his personal basal pulse of 48, treats that as an overtraining signal and substitutes a nap for the workout — a new, concrete self-quantification number not in the existing overtraining material.

- **[NOVEL]** Memorable quotes (Sheehan quoting others, in service of his "don't rush, don't strain" philosophy): "Only the sick and the ambitious are in a hurry" (Ortega); "I have never been harmed by anything that was a real pleasure" (Montaigne). And Sheehan's own closing line: "Fitness can change your body. But the third wind can change your life."

## Lydiard Marathons for Milers.pdf (5 pages)

**Identified author:** Arthur Lydiard himself — byline "by ARTHUR LYDIARD" on a *Sports Illustrated* article titled "Why I Prescribe Marathons for Milers" (internal evidence dates it to 1962: references "last summer's" European trip, predicts Peter Snell will run 3:48 "within two years," and the accompanying photo caption calls Lydiard's Ford-wagon-ad-adjacent layout "'62 FORD WAGONS"). This is Lydiard's own words, but from a magazine article distinct from *Running to the Top* — cite as `(Lydiard, "Why I Prescribe Marathons for Milers," Sports Illustrated, 1962)`, not the `(Lydiard, Running to the Top)` pattern used for the book. The piece also includes a sidebar, "Other Views of the Lydiard Way," with solicited quotes from ten other named coaches/athletes commenting on Lydiard — each attributed to its own speaker, not Lydiard.

### Lydiard's own article

- **[NOVEL]** Framing thesis: "My system is as simple as it is effective: build up your endurance through marathon running." Explicitly states there's "no psychological magic and no pain barrier involved. It is merely a process of gradual conditioning" — an implicit rebuttal of the mystique around his "hardest training regimen" reputation.

- **[NOVEL]** States the method is "designed solely for middle-distance and distance runners, not for sprinters."

- **[NOVEL]** Historical/statistical framing: in the two years before this article, New Zealand runners broke 8 world records; 12 of Lydiard's athletes had represented New Zealand internationally, two (Murray Halberg and Peter Snell) were Olympic champions, and Snell broke 4 world records (mile, half-mile, 800m, 1000 yards) within a 3-week span.

- **[NOVEL]** Direct critique of pure repetition/interval training as the dominant method among world coaches at the time: it builds speed but not stamina, which he calls "far more important." His squad instead runs "on the roads, over the hills for 20 or 30 miles," making a mile or two-mile race afterward "a lark."

- **[NOVEL]** Anecdote/contrast: Snell (marathon-conditioned) "can turn on all his speed" in a finishing kick; George Kerr, "much faster" in raw speed than Snell, "doesn't have the stamina to use his speed when he needs it most."

- **[NOVEL]** Peaking philosophy, direct quote: "There is an art to this business, this is it: to be in peak form on the big day. We let the mugs have the unimportant races. We point for the big ones." Cites Gordon Pirie (England) as a cautionary example of a repetition-trained athlete who could never reliably predict his own peak form.

- **[NOVEL]** Anecdote: cites Emil Zatopek, as national coach of Czechoslovakia relying on pure repetition training, whose team "didn't win a thing" at the Rome Olympics despite Zatopek's own legendary individual talent — used as evidence against reliance on repetition training at a program level.

- **[NOVEL]** Direct quote: "A good many fine prospects have been ruined by excessive speed work and by trying for quick results. That is a shame, but the coaches' stubbornness doesn't bother me. I couldn't care less what they think. I'll let the results speak for themselves."

- **[NOVEL]** Anecdote: before Christmas the prior year, Snell and Halberg entered a regular marathon race (Halberg finished 2nd; Snell "took it pretty easy" but ran a good time); within eight weeks of that race, Snell broke the world mile record — offered as direct evidence that marathon-distance racing built the stamina behind the record.

- **[OVERLAP — beginner walk/jog progression]** Beginner prescription: a new runner is sent out for 5-10 minutes, then told to turn around and come back; running time is then increased gradually (not daily) until the person "can go an hour out and an hour back." More experienced runners start at higher volume and progress faster. This predates and roughly matches the existing-covered Lydiard Foundation walk/jog beginner progression — same underlying philosophy from an earlier (1962), non-Foundation primary source.

- **[NOVEL]** Peak elite prescription: "At their peak the boys do 100 miles a week and they run every day of the year."

- **[OVERLAP — three hill-exercise types]** Generic description of hill work ("We spring up one side of the hill and stride out down the other, and the miles roll by") — reinforces the existing hill-training coverage but without the specific three-technique (steep hill running/bounding/springing) breakdown already documented; a looser, earlier framing of the same practice.

- **[NOVEL]** Terrain-variety rationale: trains on road, firm, and soft surfaces to keep legs flexible and avoid shin/calf injuries; in New Zealand's wet autumn/winter, trains on cross-country courses and horse-racing tracks — the sliding/slipping on soft horse-track ground is credited with strengthening calf muscles specifically.

- **[OVERLAP — interval-pace-vs-fitness matching / taper structure]** Concrete late-stage sharpening prescription: 4-6 weeks before a big race, the squad moves onto a track, using repetition training only "to add the finishing touches to stamina already built by marathon running" — not as the primary training method. Specific numbers given: 220-yard repeats starting around 30 seconds, progressing down to 25 seconds, alternating short and medium distances. Taper: begins about 10 days before the race; the day before the race is an easy 3-mile jog. New concrete numbers (10-day taper, 3-mile pre-race jog, 220s at 30→25 sec) not previously captured.

- **[NOVEL]** No fixed food/sleep regimen imposed on athletes ("Fetishes are out" — sleep is left to the individual, since training-induced tiredness handles it "subconsciously"). Diet described as heavy in eggs and fresh meat, "a lot of ice cream," an occasional glass of ale, never hard liquor, rarely pastry.

- **[NOVEL]** Coaching philosophy, direct quote on athlete self-selection: "I don't want fellows who have to be coaxed along. That is why I never go after a runner. I make him come to me and ask for help. Then I have got the upper hand." Beyond initially helping a new athlete past the mental barrier of a 20-mile run, "I don't worry about any of these things... the rest is pretty much up to him." Also: "I don't use much psychology, either."

- **[OVERLAP — historical jogging-movement material]** Community-outreach anecdote predating/paralleling the existing 1961 Auckland jogging-club material: Lydiard describes touring New Zealand men's clubs promoting running to sedentary middle-aged men, claiming a typical unfit man's resting pulse could drop from ~70 to ~45 within about six months of taking up running; separately notes local teenage boys were already imitating his athletes by running 20 miles themselves "thinking nothing of it." New concrete resting-pulse figures (70→45 in six months) not previously captured.

- **[OVERLAP — strength-training frequency data]** Weight-training skepticism, direct and distinct from the existing heavy-low-rep-lifting coverage: "for distance runners, weight lifting is not an efficient means of training." Squad does some calisthenics, "particularly the high-kicking type favored by ballet dancers," but nothing systematic — states he'd bet Halberg does none at all. A skeptical counterpoint to the site's existing (favorable) strength-training material, worth flagging as a genuinely different take rather than pure reinforcement.

- **[NOVEL]** Prediction (made in this 1962 piece): Lydiard states he had already predicted Peter Snell would run a 3:48 mile within two years.

- **[OVERLAP — team-coaching habits, Barry Magee]** States he had shifted into an administrator role, no longer training with the squad daily, delegating day-to-day subcoaching to Halberg, Snell, and Barry Magee — reinforces the existing Barry Magee subcoaching detail, adding Halberg and Snell as co-subcoaches (a new specific not previously captured).

- **[NOVEL]** Closing ambition/speculation: believes New Zealand has never produced significant sprint talent, and that with the athletic talent pool in the United States, he could put American milers and distance runners "right up top" internationally within two or three years, given a full-time coaching post.

### "Other Views of the Lydiard Way" sidebar (Sports Illustrated-solicited reactions, each its own speaker/source)

- **[NOVEL]** Herb Elliott (Australia, former world mile record holder): dismisses the "endurance barrier" as "mythical," comparing it to the four-minute-mile myth; credits Snell's results to Snell's own limit-pushing, not a special method — "there is no secret."

- **[NOVEL]** Percy Cerutty (Australia, coach of Herb Elliott): claims Lydiard is "using the same theory as my own... breaking through the pain barrier," while distinguishing his own coaching style as "much less agreeable" than Lydiard's, noting his runners nonetheless show "tremendous loyalty."

- **[NOVEL]** Mihaly Igloi (US, Hungarian-born coach of Jim Beatty and Jim Grelle): skeptical of road/marathon-based training, favors track training; emphasizes individualized programming — "You find out by watching each runner."

- **[NOVEL]** Thomas Cureton (US, director of the physical fitness research lab, University of Illinois): endorses all-out training even for middle-aged men; frames Snell's edge as psychological/physiological tolerance to lactic acid built through his training, which "the coaches are scared of."

- **[NOVEL]** Jim Beatty (US, world indoor mile record holder), direct quote: "There's no magic formula. You can't say, 'Wow, all of a sudden Snell is great.'"

- **[NOVEL]** Franz Stampfl (Austrian-born coach of Roger Bannister, based in Australia): critiques Lydiard's volume as risky for "tired, exhausted athletes," contrasting his own more measured, intensity-over-volume method with defined work limits.

- **[NOVEL]** Dr. Roger Bannister (England, first sub-4-minute miler): calls Lydiard's methods "not an absolute departure," crediting Lydiard's luck in having "a most accomplished athlete" in Snell, and questioning whether Lydiard fully appreciates the psychological barriers his training approach requires athletes to overcome.

- **[NOVEL]** Jim Elliott (US, Villanova track coach): notes a college coach must prioritize "scholastic progress... first, track second," unlike Lydiard's singular focus, but says he'd have "no quarrel" with Lydiard's methods otherwise, and that Ron Delany could match Snell under similar training.

- **[OVERLAP — historical jogging-movement material]** Bill Bowerman (US, Oregon track coach), direct 1962 quote: "Lydiard is reasonable, and he plans his workouts carefully. He studies the man and arrives at the volume of work he can do according to this knowledge." A contemporaneous (same year as Bowerman's documented 1962 New Zealand visit) primary-source Bowerman quote about Lydiard — adds a direct quote to the existing Bowerman/1962-visit historical material.

- **[NOVEL]** Ted Haydon (US, University of Chicago track coach): questions whether Snell's toughness came from Lydiard's workouts or was innate, contrasting Bannister's near-collapse after his Vancouver mile with John Landy's composure despite running nearly as fast in the same race.
