## Lydiard Part 4: Fast Twitch Systems (22 pages) — Keith Livingstone / HITSYSTEM

- **[OVERLAP]** — *Muscle fiber types / size principle of recruitment* (already
  covered). Adds firing-order framing: Type I fires first until fatigued (small
  incoming nerve axons), Type IIA fires second to maintain tension (big axons),
  Type IIB fires last, only if forced (massive axons).
- **[NOVEL]** Plyometrics (skipping, hill bounding) are framed explicitly as a
  way to "safely reverse the size principle" — forcing early recruitment of IIB
  fibers that would normally fire last. Lydiard athletes reportedly used these
  before physiologists had described the underlying neuromotor concept. Also
  named: "running tall" exercises, depth-jumps, and medicine-ball catches as
  other "sudden-stretch" exercises — none of these three are in the existing
  hill-exercise coverage.
- **[NOVEL]** A third mechanism for training fast-twitch IIB fibers beyond
  sprinting/lifting/plyometrics: running steadily and slowly to near-exhaustion
  in a long run, forcing the body to eventually recruit fast-twitch fibers to
  access their glycogen storage (and potentially exhaust hoarded creatine
  phosphate stores), creating a strong adaptive stimulus. This is a distinct
  mechanistic claim not present in the existing fiber-type/buffering coverage.
- **[OVERLAP]** — *Heavy low-rep lifting protocol* (already covered). Adds
  specific numbers: best training for Type IIB/alactic system uses near-maximal
  loads within a 10-second "load time," best above 85% 1RM, yielding only 5-6
  reps; lifts should be "closed-chain"/real-world movements; warm up for
  weights with a "dynamic" routine (mimic the actual exercise, lighter load,
  more reps). Can be done safely year-round because it produces no acidosis.
- **[OVERLAP]** — *Strength-training frequency/rep-scheme physiology* (already
  covered). Adds a full rep-to-%1RM-to-fiber-type table (credited to
  SpeedEndurance.com, not Lydiard): 1 rep=100%, 5 reps=87%, 6 reps=85% (Type
  IIB alactic/strength/functional hypertrophy); 7-12 reps=83%-67% (Type IIA
  lactic/strength-endurance/non-functional hypertrophy); 15-100 reps=65%-30%
  (Type I aerobic/endurance).
- **[NOVEL]** Historical anecdote: UCLA coaching great Jim Bush used Lydiard's
  hill exercises in training his track athletes, including 1992 Olympic 400m
  gold medallist and world record-holder (43.50s) Quincy Watts, and 1984
  Olympic 110m hurdles silver medallist Greg Foster. Slide notes Lydiard
  devised the exercises in the 1950s.
- **[NOVEL]** Direct Lydiard quotes on hill-running technique: "A lot of people
  run like they're sitting in a bucket... When their knees are bent, they can't
  get power and stride." Lydiard cited 1972 Olympic 1500m champion Pekka
  Vasala of Finland as his model example, reportedly doing hill bounding on a
  100-200m hill.
- **[NOVEL]** Direct Lydiard quote on ankle mechanics: "Increase in speed comes
  from flicking of the ankles... If you want speed, you don't need to be like a
  body-builder. You need to be like a ballet dancer, with springy and bouncy
  ankles" (said in the 1960s, per the slide unchanged since). Slide cites Seb
  Coe and Haile Gebrselassie as examples of visible ankle "suppleness and
  spring."
- **[NOVEL]** Biomechanics citation: a 1987 *Journal of Biomechanics* study
  found the horizontal force used during constant-speed running was **one-tenth**
  the vertical force used — framed as support for Lydiard's vertical-thrust,
  hill-spring emphasis over stride-length chasing.
- **[NOVEL]** Named coaching cue/misconception flag: "CAUTION!!! SUSTAINED SPEED
  KILLS!" — warning against over-relying on sustained glycolytic (Type IIA)
  training.
- **[NOVEL]** Direct quote from Wilf Paish (former Great Britain Olympic team
  coach, 1978), presented to corroborate the low-rep/heavy-weight strength
  philosophy: "I am convinced all middle distance runners should train with
  weights using the accepted strength training techniques of low repetitions
  and heavy weights. The pyramid system of the three sets of not more than 6
  repetitions should prove ideal for the middle distance runner." Slide notes
  Paish coached 1:42.97 800m runner Peter Elliott, a multiple major-championship
  medallist (1986-1990). This is Paish's own quote, not Lydiard's — cite as
  `(Paish, quoted in Livingstone, HITSYSTEM Clinic Slides)`.
- **[OVERLAP]** — *Stride-length/economy math* (already covered). Adds a named
  academic citation and table: Paavolainen et al., "Explosive-strength training
  improves 5-km running time by improving running economy and muscle power," *J
  Appl Physiol* Vol. 86, Issue 5, 1527-1533, May 1999 — experienced orienteers
  who did low-rep/heavy-weight training and cut aerobic volume by 35% still
  improved 5000m time via stride-length gains, despite no significant VO2max or
  AT change. Slide's own theoretical table: a 0.5%-2.5% stride-length increase
  maps to 5000m improving from 14:00 to 13:39.0, 800m from 1:50 to 1:47.3, and
  1500m from 3:40 to 3:34.5.
- **[OVERLAP]** — *Fast-twitch/slow-twitch fiber types* (already covered). Adds
  a much more granular physiological comparison table (Type I/IIA/IIB) covering
  contraction time, motor-axon size, fatigue resistance, force production,
  mitochondrial density, capillary density, oxidative/glycolytic capacity,
  storage fuel, and myoglobin content — credited to Karp, J.R., "Motor unit
  recruitment strategy in muscle during eccentric contractions," unpublished
  master's thesis, University of Calgary, 1997.
- **[NOVEL]** VO2 max is defined as very close to the maximum pace a good
  athlete can sustain for **8-11 minutes** — a specific duration figure not
  present in the existing steady-state/oxygen-debt coverage.
- **[NOVEL]** Named terminology distinction for anaerobic training: "systemic
  acidosis" (Stage 1 — long VO2max-pace intervals with equal/shorter recovery,
  builds general circulating acidity, detectable via earlobe blood-lactate
  test) vs. "local acidosis" (Stage 2 — anaerobic glycolytic reps at 800m/1500m
  race-specific power output, floods the running muscles with acid so
  localized it hasn't reached general circulation and often won't show on an
  earlobe lactate test; requires long recoveries).
- **[NOVEL]** Specific VO2max training-pace guidance: VO2max work occurs
  between 3k and 5k race pace; safest trained at 5000m pace/best 15-minute pace
  (96% VO2max), progressing to 3000m pace/best 8-minute pace (100% VO2max) as
  fitness improves.
- **[NOVEL]** Concrete VO2max workout prescription: 5 x 1000m at best 5000m
  pace with equal jog recovery (roughly 2.5 laps hard / 1.5 laps slow), one
  rep every 6:00; work intervals generally 2-3 minutes with equal recovery.
- **[NOVEL]** Lactate-tolerance (above-VO2max) training guidance: best trained
  at 400-600m race pace, or the fastest pace sustainable for 45-90 seconds,
  with complete recovery between reps; heart rate often peaks during recovery,
  not during the effort itself.
- **[NOVEL]** 800m/1500m-specific anaerobic rep prescription: run at current
  best 800m-1500m pace, aiming for 1200m-2000m of total hard running via very
  long slow-jog or full-rest recoveries between reps. Coaching cue: "QUIT
  SESSION IF TIRED OR UNABLE TO CONTINUE WITH EFFECTIVE TECHNIQUE. YOU DON'T
  WANT TO TRAIN FAILURE AT RACE SPEEDS."
- **[NOVEL]** Biochemistry/RER detail (Respiratory Exchange Ratio via gas
  calorimetry): aerobic glucose respiration RER = 1.0; aerobic fat (palmitic
  acid) respiration RER = 0.696; aerobic protein (albumin) respiration RER =
  0.8; anaerobic glycolysis RER = indefinable (no CO2 produced, no O2 used).
  Full balanced chemical equations given for each.
- **[NOVEL]** Practical lactate-testing nuance: an earlobe pin-prick blood
  lactate test reliably reflects "systemic" acidosis buildup (Stage 1 style
  training) but often fails to detect "local" acidosis from short, hard,
  fully-recovered reps (Stage 2 style), because the localized muscle acid is
  many times more concentrated than what reaches general circulation and the
  liver hasn't yet re-metabolized it.

## Lydiard Part 5: Intensities & Applications (25 pages) — Keith Livingstone / HITSYSTEM

- **[NOVEL]** Core terminology: the "Deflection Point" is where heart rate stops
  rising proportionally with speed/effort; the speed at that point is "Threshold
  Speed" and the heart rate there is "Threshold Heart Rate." Work below it is
  "sub-maximal"/aerobic/endurance work; work above it is "maximal"/anaerobic
  work.
- **[NOVEL]** Lactate rises slowly until threshold speed, then increases
  exponentially; threshold occurs at a lactate concentration of approximately
  **4 mmol/litre**, though individual variation is significant. This numeric
  threshold isn't in the existing coverage (which covers oxygen-debt ceiling
  numbers but not a lactate-concentration threshold figure).
- **[NOVEL]** "Heart Rate Reserve" (HRR) defined as the difference between
  resting HR and maximal HR — the athlete's usable "work capacity."
- **[NOVEL]** Explicit misconception callout: a chart splitting VO2max
  performance into a "true aerobic mountain" (0-~80% HRR) topped by a small
  "anaerobic peak" (Type IIA, ~85-100%) is captioned "NB: This is where all the
  'High Intensity' guys get it COMPLETELY wrong!" — implying most of the VO2max
  ceiling is built aerobically, not anaerobically.
- **[NOVEL]** Karvonen method spelled out as the recommended (most accurate)
  intensity-calculation formula: Target HR = Resting HR + (Intensity% x HRR).
  Worked example (resting HR 45, max HR 195, HRR 150) with a full zone table:
  60% = 135 ("most effective aerobic zone starts here: recovery running"), 65%
  = 142.5 ("Steady Aerobic Zone"), 70% = 150 ("Sub-Threshold Zone"), 75% =
  157.5 ("Anaerobic Threshold intensities start above here"), 80% = 165, 85% =
  172.5 ("upper end of anaerobic threshold"), 90% = 180 ("too hard for AT, too
  easy for VO2max, but tiring nevertheless").
- **[NOVEL]** Adaptation-timeline claim: the anaerobic system "takes weeks to
  develop" (stroke volume and biochemistry adapt quickly, recruits Type IIA
  fibers), while the aerobic system "takes years to develop" (capillarization
  of muscle, recruits slow-twitch fibers) — framed as "VO2 Max Made 'Simplish'."
- **[NOVEL]** Coaching metaphor: lowering the "aerobic end of the clothes-line"
  (i.e., extending endurance at slower VDOT paces) shifts the whole lactate
  curve rightward over time, requiring patience while "a substantial base and
  fat-burning engine is created over several seasons."
- **[NOVEL]** Substantial anecdote: triathlete Mark Allen (deck labels him "7x
  Ironman World Champion," though body text says "six Hawaii Ironman titles" —
  worth flagging as an internal inconsistency in the source) came from a
  high-intensity interval swimming background and trained hard across all three
  disciplines weekly, with inconsistent results and frequent injury. Under
  instruction from chiropractor Phil Maffetone (interested in the heart
  rate/acidosis relationship), Allen was capped at a max HR of 154 for all
  steady endurance work. He felt he was "plodding" for weeks (miles well over
  8:00/mile), but soreness resolved and, after months at the same low-effort
  HR, his pace at that same effort dropped into the high 5:00s/mile — he went
  on to win multiple Hawaii Ironman titles, the last at age 37. Prescriptive
  takeaway given to the audience: keep heart rate "way under" current
  anaerobic threshold — even jogging behind a nursing-mothers' stroller group
  if needed — and within about 12 weeks at a very low effort (HR as low as
  120), the aerobic "fat-burning engine" rebuilds.
- **[NOVEL]** Named coaching concept: "Hop off the 'Intensity Escalator' at or
  just above the level you're training for" — a pyramid of per-event intensity
  caps (e.g., 1500m specialists shouldn't train above 1500m pace except
  occasionally; marathoners need little pace-work faster than 15k pace, maybe
  1-2 10k-pace sessions at most). Explicit tradeoff principle: training much
  above anaerobic threshold pits two muscle-fiber types/energy systems against
  each other, and over-training one will "de-train" the other; perceived speed
  deficits should instead be addressed with leg-speed drills and fartlek,
  year-round.
- **[NOVEL]** Cross-discipline application: a cycling heart-rate zone table
  (VO2 Max Boosting 92-100%, Anaerobic Threshold Endurance 85-91%, General
  Aerobic Endurance 75-84%, Base Aerobic Endurance 65-74%, Recovery 50-64%,
  each with a talk-test descriptor) is followed by the same misconception
  prompt: "SPOT THE COMMON MISCONCEPTION AGAIN? WHICH ZONES WILL HAVE A MUCH
  BIGGER AND SAFER 'BOOST' OF VO2 MAX, 'PUSHING UP' FROM BELOW?"
- **[NOVEL]** Real-world case study: Marcellin College (Melbourne) is credited
  with "multiple Australian schoolboys cross-country team titles," presented as
  the deck's proof-of-concept, with full weekly training templates:
  - *Winter*: Mon/Fri easy recovery (~30 min); Tue tough cross-country
    intervals at high effort/short recovery ("the closest the squad will get to
    true extended anaerobic work for months"); Wed steady run up to 90 min for
    seniors; Thu alactic leg-speed day — short fast relaxed sprints then strong
    fartleks done "the 'Roger Robinson' way" (slowest athlete on the inside of
    the circuit) — a named coaching-cue attribution not in existing coverage;
    Sat strong cross-country effort/race, often rolling 5000m efforts at
    sub-threshold pace; Sun always long and very easy over hilly circuits
    (juniors 1hr+, seniors up to 2 hrs). Predominantly aerobic; young athletes
    mentored by senior boys; weekly long-run attendance non-negotiable for team
    eligibility.
  - *Early summer*: same structure but Tuesday shifts to VO2max track
    intervals with proper-form emphasis, later transitioning to goal-race-pace
    reps as major races approach; Sunday drops to 90 min max for seniors.
    Explicit principle: "Tuesday anaerobic work is always set to current
    realistic goal-race pace, and never faster."
  - *Late-summer 1500m tuning* (for a 4:00/1500m athlete, 64s/lap): Tue
    warm-up of 6-10x alactic strides, then 2000m @ 6:15 and 6x400m @ 64s with
    full recovery; Sat is race or time trial ("training to race, not racing to
    train... always leave something in the tank") — a 4:00 1500m runner should
    be able to solidly time-trial 1200m at 3:15. Warm-up sequencing principle:
    60m strideouts to warm up fast-twitch IIB fibers, then 2000m steady @ AT
    pace to warm up Type IIA/glycolytic systems "without blowing the barn doors
    off," then the prescribed anaerobic session.
  - *Late-summer 800m tuning* (for a 1:56/800m athlete, 58s/lap): Tue 2x400m @
    58s full recovery, then 4x200m @ 28-29s with 200m jog recovery, total hard
    volume usually capped at 1200-1600m at 800m pace; Sat long uphill intervals
    of 300m @ 5000m pace with generous recovery. Note: recoveries at 800m pace
    "may be a lot more" than for 1500m pace work.
- **[NOVEL]** (Reinforces a claim already made in Part 4 of this same batch —
  noted once, not double-counted against site coverage.) A weekly track-spikes
  session of relaxed sprints under 10 seconds, with easy jogging between reps,
  is recommended because "the lactic acid system is not even woken up until
  about 10 seconds of fast running" (energy instead drawn from the creatine
  phosphate system in IIB fibers); framed as good for technique/economy at all
  speeds without accumulating fatigue.
