# Batch 07 digest

## Vienna 2017 Session 2 Int Distribution.pdf — "Session 2: Training intensity distribution for endurance athletes: From polarized to optimized? Moving towards 2025"

**Identified author:** Stephen Seiler (exercise physiologist, University of Agder, Kristiansand,
Norway; title slide byline, ResearchGate cover page metadata — "Presentation, October 2017,
1 author: Stephen Seiler" — and repeated self-citation/attribution throughout confirm sole
authorship). This is **not** Lydiard's own material and never mentions Lydiard or his method by
name anywhere in the deck's visible text. It is the companion "Session 2" to the
`Vienna 2017 Training Monitoring Basics.pdf` deck already digested in batch-09 (also Seiler,
also no Lydiard mentions) — both appear to have been delivered as guest lectures at the
Lydiard Foundation's Vienna 2017 clinic. Recommended citation: `(Seiler, Vienna 2017 Clinic)`.
29 slides total; discarded ~9 pure chart-without-claim/photo/decoration/agenda slides
("and then we and others did lots more studies" bibliography list, a butterfly-collage
periodization slide, a black tug-of-war stock image, bare brain/heart/muscle icon slide).

- **[NOVEL]** Central framing concept: a "toxic" training effect is not a property of any single
  session in isolation ("In isolation, there are no 'toxic' training intensities, or session
  compositions") — toxicity is a property of the **weekly/chronic distribution and dose**, not
  any one workout. Explicit slide title: "Because... there are potentially 'toxic' training
  intensity distributions!"
- **[NOVEL]** Dose-response framing: there is a training-load range with maximal positive/minimal
  negative effect, and a separate, higher-load range needed for elite athletes to actually win —
  these two ranges partially overlap in a "toxic side effects" zone, meaning top performers
  necessarily accept some elevated injury/illness risk that recreational-optimal dosing avoids.
- **[NOVEL]** "Chronic dose-response risk/benefit ≈ Training Volume × Intensity Distribution" —
  positioned as the key controllable variable, alongside two illustrative sample weekly patterns:
  a block/clustered pattern (LI, LI, Rest, LI, HI, Threshold, Rest → 3 HIT / 1 Threshold / 7 LI
  sessions a week) vs. an alternating pattern (HI, LI, HI, LI, Rest, LI, LI).
- **[OVERLAP]** — *Steady State / oxygen-debt physiology*: reinforces the existing coverage with
  a different, modern lactate/HR-based framing rather than Lydiard's own terms. Presents a
  standard 5-zone lactate/%HRpeak scheme (VT1 ≈ 2 mmol/L lactate ≈ 78–80% HRpeak; VT2 ≈ 4 mmol/L
  ≈ 86–87% HRpeak; VO2max/HRmax = 100%; zones running 55/72/80/87/93/100% HRpeak breakpoints) and
  a simplified 3-zone collapse of the same curve. New numeric anchors, not present in the site's
  existing Lydiard/Livingstone material.
- **[NOVEL]** Two competing historical models of intensity distribution, presented as directly
  opposed shapes: the "Lactate Threshold Training Model" (a bell curve — most training clustered
  *at* threshold intensity) vs. the "Polarized Training Model" (a U-shape — most volume low,
  a meaningful chunk high, and a *trough*, deliberately avoided, at threshold). This
  threshold-vs-polarized dichotomy is the deck's organizing thesis and isn't framed anywhere in
  the existing site content.
- **[NOVEL]** "Two intensity poles" metaphor: polarized training clusters around two poles, roughly
  ~65% VO2max (low) and ~90% VO2max (high), with the lactate-threshold zone in between described
  as a "black hole" that training intensity "tends to get pulled into" if a coach isn't
  deliberately avoiding it — a vivid, quotable coaching image.
- **[NOVEL]** Historical training-volume doubling in elite cross-country skiing: ~450 hours/year
  in 1965 vs. ~900 hours/year in 2015 (Sandbakk, *Int J Sports Physiology and Performance*,
  2017) — cited as evidence of how much total training load has grown across 50 years even
  though intensity-distribution *shape* has stayed broadly similar.
- **[NOVEL]** Cross-sport elite peak annual training-hour chart: Distance Runner ~550h/yr,
  Orienteer ~600h, XC Skier ~900h, Rower ~1000h, Cyclist ~1200h, Swimmer ~1300h. (Near-identical
  chart, with a slightly different XC-skier figure — 850h vs. 900h — already appears in the
  batch-09 digest of Seiler's companion Session 1 deck; flagging for the synthesis pass so it
  isn't extracted twice as if from two independent sources.)
- **[NOVEL]** Historical shift in elite rowers' monthly training-hour split (Fiskerstrand &
  Seiler, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 14:303-310, 2004): in the 1970s, international rowing medalists
  split roughly evenly, ~40 basic-endurance hours/month vs. ~40 high-intensity hours/month; by
  the 1980s–90s this shifted to ~55–60 basic-endurance hours/month vs. only ~28–30 high-intensity
  hours/month — a concrete historical data point showing the sport moving toward a more
  polarized, low-intensity-dominant model over ~20 years. Quoted conclusion: "the optimal
  training organization for maximal performance is a polarized model of training with about 75%
  of training performed well below the lactate threshold and 15–20% well above that intensity."
- **[NOVEL]** Named elite training-log case studies with real numbers:
  - Ingrid Kristiansen (5 world records, world champion) — monthly zone-hour table (Tønnessen/
    Olympiatoppen data) showing Zone 1 dominating every month (~29.5–46.5 hrs/month), peak
    volume in the January–March preparation window (~57–60 total hrs/month), and competition
    months (June–October) holding lower total volume (~35–42 hrs/month) but adding
    proportionally more Zone 3–5 work.
  - A 2:15 marathoner (Sylta) — session-load scatterplot across a marathon build-up showing
    training sessions cluster into visually distinct bands by load: routine LIT sessions
    (load ~100–400), HIT interval sessions (load ~650–800), and "extra-long" LIT sessions
    (load ~900–1650, topping out at the Rotterdam marathon itself) — illustrating that a
    session can be "hard" purely from duration, without high intensity.
  - Eliud Kipchoge — session-type breakdown over the 41 days / 59 sessions before Berlin
    Marathon 2017 (his 2:03 marathon, footnoted as later becoming 2:00:25): 34 easy runs
    (10–22km), 7 medium-intensity runs, 6 tempo runs (30–40km), 6 fartlek sessions (7–14km),
    6 track-interval sessions (5–15km total) — a concrete, citable numeric breakdown of a
    world-record marathoner's training-type distribution.
- **[NOVEL]** Meta-analytic summary (Stöggl & Sperlich, *Frontiers in Physiology*, 2015) across
  many named individual studies (cycling, XC skiing, rowing, running, speed skating) shows elite/
  well-trained endurance athletes' Zone-1 (low-intensity) share runs ~75–95% of total training
  during preparation periods, staying similarly high (mean ~80%) through pre-competition and
  competition periods too — presented as convergent, cross-sport, cross-decade evidence for
  low-intensity dominance regardless of exact periodization phase.
- **[NOVEL]** "80/20 Running" (Matt Fitzgerald) referenced as the popular-press distillation of
  this research; cover-quoted line attributed to Robert Johnson (co-founder, LetsRun.com, in the
  book's foreword): "Do 80% of your running at low intensity, and the remaining 20% at moderate
  to high intensity. The rest is details."
- **[NOVEL]** Trained-vs-untrained physiological-strain comparison: repeated 10×500m intervals at
  *identical absolute power output*, tested in the same subject untrained vs. after 3 months of
  training — %HRpeak during the reps and during recovery both drop substantially post-training
  (recovery HR troughs falling from the 60–70% range down to the 45–55% range), illustrating
  improved economy/reduced physiological cost at a fixed workload after a training block.
- **[NOVEL]** Individual-variation framework for how much of a training outcome is coachable:
  splits response into (1) genetic, non-responsive individual variation ("the bigger this box,
  the less fun our job!"), (2) individual variation that *is* responsive to prescription changes,
  (3) generalizable periodization-characteristic effects (flagged as likely small, "reality may
  not live up to the fancy lingo"), and (4) generalizable effects from the basic
  intensity-distribution/volume-load framework — which the author states his "15 years of work
  suggests... is important," i.e., the biggest lever a coach actually has.

Overall: this deck is a modern sports-science research synthesis on polarized/intensity-
distribution training, not a Lydiard-attributed document at all — but because Lydiard is widely
credited as an early practical originator of the high-low intensity split this literature
formalizes decades later, it's useful supporting/validating context for the site's existing
Steady State coverage rather than being about Lydiard's own words or history.

## Lydiard on Barefoot Running.pdf — "What Lydiard Would Say to the Barefoot/Natural Running 'Fad'"

**Identified author:** René Borg (Director/coach, Borg Coaching Services; head coach of
Glendalough AC), writing on the Champions Everywhere blog (championseverywhere.com), published
June 2, 2012, byline "René" with a full bio block at the article's end confirming identity.
The article is Borg's own analysis and argument, built around extensive direct quotations from
Lydiard's *Running to the Top* (1997) and one 2005 *Run Washington* interview quote, plus one
Percy Cerutty quote and one Keith Livingstone phone-quote. Recommended citation for Borg's own
commentary/argument: `(Borg, Champions Everywhere)`; for the passages he quotes directly from
Lydiard's book, the existing `(Lydiard, Running to the Top)` convention applies since these are
verbatim Lydiard quotations reproduced in a secondary source, not Borg's own words.

- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard direct quote (*Running to the Top*, 1997) establishing that running form
  is a taught skill, not innate: "Not everyone is a natural athlete, with natural
  coordination….How to run well has to be taught and practised….You need to know how to run
  properly and how to develop a technique which will direct all your effort into going forward
  comfortably and as economically as possible."
- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard shared Percy Cerutty's view on posture and cited it approvingly: "To get
  power, you have to avoid that running posture that we call sitting in a bucket. This you have
  to run tall and think running tall to keep your body straight." Lydiard's own writings on
  technique otherwise mostly deferred to Bud Winters' sprint drills, whom he considered the
  best sprint coach of all time.
- **[NOVEL]** Anecdote/quote: Lydiard's early runners (including himself) trained largely in
  canvas shoes with extra rubber on the soles, not barefoot — "In my early days, we ran in
  canvas shoes. They were ideally flexible and we just put some extra rubber on the soles. We
  didn't get plantar fasciitis, we didn't pronate or supinate, we might have lost a bit of skin
  from the rough canvas when we were running marathons but, generally speaking, we didn't have
  foot problems." (*Running to the Top*)
- **[NOVEL]** Named anecdote: Bruce Tulloh, coach of Orbana Healthy Energy, won the European
  Championship 5000m running barefoot; Lydiard specifically noted and admired his ability to
  run barefoot on a cinder track.
- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard described his ideal running shoe, well before minimalist/barefoot shoes or
  "Born to Run" existed, as: "very flexible, little more than a second layer of skin, if that is
  possible" and "If you could just attach a rubber sole to your foot, with nothing on the top,
  you'd have the perfect running shoe" — explicitly likened by Borg to Tarahumara sandals.
- **[NOVEL]** Historical anecdote: while coaching in Mexico, Lydiard encountered the Tarahumara,
  recognized their potential as marathoners, and arranged a marathon trial for them — but local
  officials set up unusually frequent/lavish feed stations along the route, and the Tarahumara
  (used to frugal, minimal-aid running) reportedly stopped to make use of them, undercutting the
  trial's value as a fair test.
- **[NOVEL]** 2005 *Run Washington* interview quote, showing Lydiard held this shoe philosophy
  until his death: "We like flexible shoes, to let your foot function. Shoes that let your foot
  function like you're barefoot – they're the shoes for me, as long as they have some rubber
  underneath to alleviate the jarring."
- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard preferred training on hard surfaces with good traction (tarmac,
  hard-packed trail) over soft grass or forest tracks — even the famous Waitakere long hill run
  was mainly tarmac/hard-packed trail. Borg's own added rationale (not Lydiard's stated one):
  hard surfaces "can't be cheated" on and force correct technique, whereas soft
  ground/minimalist footwear lets poor technique go unpunished.
- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard direct quote skeptical of expensive modern running shoes and misconception
  addressed: "The point we are making is that paying several hundred dollars for the latest in
  hi-tech running shoes is no guarantee you'll avoid any of these injuries and can even guarantee
  that you will suffer from them in one form or the other." (*Running to the Top*)
- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard warned that elevated/high heels on running shoes are more dangerous than
  protective (near-impossible to sprain an ankle in a zero-drop shoe), and rejected the idea of
  training in heavier "protective" shoes while racing in flats — believed in training and racing
  in the same shoe: "As far as I'm concerned you train and race in the same shoes and those
  shoes have got to be more like slipper than the hi-tech monsters they're producing these days
  – light, flexible mid-sole, plenty of resilient cushioning under the feet, allowing your foot
  to function as naturally as possible." (*Running to the Top*)
- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard rejected orthotics as a default intervention, reasoning that
  pronation/supination doesn't occur to an excessive degree in a non-malformed foot running
  barefoot. Direct quote: "In other words; the orthotics are for the shoes, not the feet."
  Borg reinforces this with a secondhand anecdote from coach Tony Riddle (told to him in London):
  "when people come to work with me with orthotics, the first thing I have to do is throw them
  in a bin." A misconception explicitly addressed: that supination/pronation correction via
  orthotics is a routine necessity.
- **[OVERLAP]** — *Strength-training coverage / heavy low-rep lifting protocol*: nuances the
  existing site coverage of Lydiard's strength-training material with a different angle —
  relaxation-cost-of-tension, not frequency or rep-scheme. Direct quote: "I have seen
  advertisements advising people to hold weights in their hands while they're running. That's
  crazy. People who carry weights are upsetting their relaxation and creating extra tensions
  which eventually must disrupt their forward momentum – and, I repeat, that's what running is
  all about." (*Running to the Top*) Also includes the famous "throw those weights into Tokyo
  Bay and come run with me" anecdote about Lydiard's remark to Japanese marathoners' gym-based
  training — Borg explicitly notes this line is commonly misread as blanket anti-strength-
  training dogma, when Lydiard's actual point was that training (even cross-training) must stay
  *specific*; he preferred functional/natural-movement analogies like "a man shovelling gravel
  from one pile to another" over machine-gym exercises.
- **[OVERLAP]** — *Stride-length/economy math and muscle-fiber/technique material*: adds a
  genuinely different take on Lydiard's understanding of foot-strike mechanics. Lydiard believed
  in and taught a powerful "push-off" as central to running technique; modern biomechanics
  understands the sensation of a "push" is actually an elastic "bounce off" (like a rubber ball)
  combined with actively pulling the leg off the ground, not a driving push. Borg's assessment:
  "So while Lydiard may have interpreted this detail wrong, he understood how important it was
  that runners retain the elastic properties of their legs and feet." Companion Lydiard quote
  criticizing rigid-soled shoes for blocking this: "They'll flex at the joint of the foot all
  right but not at the arch, where flexing is a natural foot function. Put a board under each
  foot of an athlete and he couldn't even walk properly, let alone run, but that's very much how
  some of these running shoes affect the arches." (*Running to the Top*)
- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard direct quote establishing that, in his model, technique must be
  established *before* energy-system-specific training begins — a nuance against the popular
  reputation of Lydiard as purely an "energy systems" coach: "Once the nature of the activity and
  the fundamentals of technique are achieved, we can add the endurance and strength to get the
  optimal results out of the athlete…. Correct technique applies to every sport because, if you
  have any kind of fault in your technique or in the equipment you are using, for cycling or
  rowing, for instance, you cannot capitalise on your fitness, however good that is." (*Running
  to the Top*)
- **[NOVEL]** Named coaching cue/anecdote: Lydiard used video recording to correct athletes'
  running form rapidly, quote: "When I was conducting training camps in America, I could change
  runners' form within a week. We used video tapes and in a week we'd have them running like
  Sebastian Coe." Sebastian Coe is cited as Lydiard's exemplar of superior running technique.
- **[NOVEL]** Keith Livingstone quote (phone conversation with Borg), offered as color on
  Lydiard's relationship to formal exercise physiology: "Arthur mangled physiological terms" —
  i.e., Lydiard was not a trained scientist; he observed what worked empirically with his
  athletes first and only adopted scientific terminology later, once physiologists explained to
  him *why* his methods worked.
- **[NOVEL]** Misconception explicitly addressed (Borg's framing, supported by the Lydiard
  quotes above): that barefoot/natural/minimalist running was ever a "new fad" — Borg argues
  Lydiard would have seen it as something old that the running world had simply forgotten,
  comparable to how "periodisation" and "long steady running" are also periodically declared
  dead or faddish despite being long-established.
