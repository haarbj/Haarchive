# Batch 09 digest

## Vienna 2017 Training Monitoring Basics.pdf — "Session 1: Quantifying training in endurance athletes"

**Identified author:** Stephen Seiler (exercise physiologist, University of Agder, Norway;
title-slide byline, PDF author metadata, and repeated self-citations/tweets throughout all
confirm sole authorship). This is **not** Lydiard's own material and does not mention Lydiard
or his method anywhere in the deck's visible text — it is a general sports-science lecture on
training-load monitoring (heart rate, blood lactate, RPE, HRV, session load) that appears to
have been delivered as a companion/guest session at the Lydiard Foundation's Vienna 2017
clinic (per the file's own naming and the existing-coverage.md note that "Vienna 2017 clinic
material" is confirmed uncovered). Recommended citation: `(Seiler, Vienna 2017 Clinic)`.
52 slides total; discarded ~20 pure chart/photo/agenda/acknowledgment slides with no
extractable claim (title slides, "Finito," "special thanks" credits slide, blank framing
questions like "How should we divide up the intensity zones?").

- **[NOVEL]** Training "dose" framework splits into Volume (meters/km, hours, kg lifted,
  jumps, contacts, accelerations) and Acute Intensity (external power, physiological response
  [HR, blood lactate], perceptual response [RPE]) — a general monitoring taxonomy not present
  in the site's existing Lydiard/Livingstone material.
- **[NOVEL]** Cross-sport peak annual training volume chart: distance runners ~550h/year,
  orienteers ~600h, cross-country skiers ~850h, rowers ~1000h, cyclists ~1200h, swimmers
  ~1300h. Explicit caution that volume comparisons **across** sports are "rarely useful" due to
  differing biomechanical loading (be consistent comparing **within** a sport only).
- **[NOVEL]** Biomechanical explanation for why runners cap out at far lower weekly training
  hours than rowers despite similar distances: a 200km rowing week totals ~900,000–1,350,000
  kg of cumulative load (seated, 100% concentric, body weight supported), while a 200km running
  week totals ~28–35 million kg of load (148,000–168,000 footstrikes/week at ~3x bodyweight per
  step, both concentric and eccentric loading) — explaining why rowers survive 25–30h training
  weeks but runners only manage 10–12h. This is a genuinely different (biomechanical
  load/impact) explanation for running's low volume ceiling than anything currently covered.
- **[NOVEL]** Elite endurance athletes self-report training duration honestly overall, but
  different sports have different self-report "traditions" that produce roughly ±10%
  discrepancies when comparing recorded vs. self-reported training time across sports (citing
  Sylta et al., IJSPP 2014).
- **[NOVEL]** As exercise intensity rises: heart rate increases **linearly** up to VO2max, but
  blood lactate concentration and sympathetic stress both increase **non-linearly** (the
  "lactate threshold curve"); perception of effort and post-exercise perceived fatigue also
  increase. This is a different physiological lens than the site's existing oxygen-debt/Steady
  State coverage — not a direct overlap.
- **[NOVEL]** Age-predicted HRmax formulas (220-minus-age) are unreliable individually: of 37
  cyclists (age 25-49) tested under identical VO2max-test conditions, only 11/37 fell within
  ±3 beats of their true measured peak HR, and 12/37 were off by 10+ beats.
- **[NOVEL]** Named 5-zone lactate/HR intensity model (Norwegian Olympic training center
  "Olympiatoppen" reference table): Zone 1 = 0.8–1.5 mmol/L lactate, 55–72% HRmax; Zone 2 =
  1.5–2.5 mmol/L, 72–82%; Zone 3 = 2.5–4.0 mmol/L, 82–87%; Zone 4 = 4.0–6.0 mmol/L, 87–92%;
  Zone 5 = 6.0–10.0 mmol/L, 92–97%. Also presents simplified 3-zone and 2-zone (LIT vs. HIT,
  split at LT ~3-4mM lactate/~80% HRpeak) collapses of the same curve.
- **[NOVEL]** HRV (RMSSD) recovery is delayed longest after threshold and above-VT2 (high
  lactate) sessions compared to low-intensity training, which recovers HRV toward baseline much
  faster post-exercise (Seiler, Haugen, Kuffel, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2007, 39(8):1366–1373).
- **[NOVEL]** Worked example of within-session HR drift: a 6x4min interval session (2min
  recovery) with average session HR of 150bpm (82% of 183 max) actually had an average **peak**
  working HR of 175bpm (96% of max); working HR drifted up ~10bpm and recovery HR drifted up
  ~20bpm across the six reps at identical pace — illustrating that session-average HR
  meaningfully understates true peak intensity when effort/pace varies within a session.
- **[NOVEL]** Alignment table across 4 session types (9 highly-trained orienteers, ~14h/week
  training load, VO2max 72 ml/kg/min): 60min Zone 1 = 68% HRmax / RPE 9.7 / session-RPE 2;
  120min Zone 1 = same HR / RPE 10 / session-RPE 2.4; Zone 3 threshold (20-30-10min) = 88%
  HRmax / BLa 2.7 mmol/L / RPE 13.9 / session-RPE 5; Zone 5 interval (20-6x3min-10) = 95% HRmax
  / BLa 7.1 mmol/L / RPE 17.2 / session-RPE 8.1.
- **[NOVEL]** "Session RPE" method for a single training-load number: Training Load = duration
  (minutes) × session RPE (Foster 0–10 scale, where 10 = "just like my hardest race," rated
  ~30 min after finishing). Example: 90min easy run × 3 sRPE = 270 AU vs. 60min HIT session ×
  7 sRPE = 420 AU.
- **[NOVEL]** Comparing three HIT interval prescriptions all performed at self-reported "maximal
  session effort" (27 subjects, 7-week study, Seiler/Jøranson/Olesen/Hetlelid, Scand J Med Sci
  Sports 23:74-83, 2013): 4x16min near-MLSS effort produced session load 435 (64min work time ×
  6.8 sRPE); 4x8min produced load 227 (32min × 7.1); 4x4min produced load 125 (16min × 7.8) —
  despite all three being maximal effort, the longer/lower-intensity protocol accumulated
  roughly 3.5x more total "load" than the shortest/hardest one.
- **[NOVEL]** Proposed model that perceived-exertion "brain stress" scales non-linearly
  (exponentially, not linearly) with RPE score as intensity approaches max — e.g., the jump in
  central stress from RPE 9 to RPE 10 (racing the last km of a 10K for a PB) is claimed to be
  far larger than the jump from RPE 1 to RPE 5, because the RPE scale was originally indexed to
  heart rate, which itself has a non-linear relationship to sympathetic drive at high effort.
- **[NOVEL]** Case study: a named elite Norwegian cross-country skier (Bente Skari)'s full
  14-year, 8,587-hour logged training career breaks down as 78% Zone 1, 12% Zone 2, 3.5% Zone
  3, 6% Zone 4, <1% Zone 5 — presented as an example of a "polarized" career-long training
  distribution (heavy easy volume, minimal moderate-intensity work, small high-intensity dose).
  This concrete "80/20-style" distribution example and the polarized-training framing generally
  are not present anywhere in the site's existing Lydiard/Livingstone coverage.
- **[NOVEL]** Session-load scatter plot across a marathon training build-up (data from
  Sylta) shows "hard" sessions cluster into distinct categories by cause: HIT interval sessions
  (load ~400–900), extra-long low-intensity sessions (load ~1000–1300), and competition/race
  efforts (load ~1600+) — making the point that a session can be physiologically "hard" purely
  from duration, not just from intensity.
- **[NOVEL]** Practical monitoring checklist distilled to 6 items for coaches: (1) standardize
  training-prescription terminology across a squad, (2) log training duration consistently
  (agree what counts), (3) teach athletes the session-RPE scale but never compare RPE numbers
  across different athletes, (4) experiment with a load metric but keep the formula simple,
  (5) log heart rate and/or lactate where available (automatic device-to-diary sync flagged as
  a major practical upgrade), (6) track test/race results over time.
