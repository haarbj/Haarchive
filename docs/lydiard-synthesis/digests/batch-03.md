### Historical/theoretical framing of periodization

- **[NOVEL]** Traces periodization theory's institutional origin to Frederick
  Winslow Taylor's *Principles of Scientific Management* (1911) and Stalin's
  Soviet five-year economic plans, both feeding into Matveyev's Periodization
  Model — framed as a pointed critique that classic linear periodization has
  roots in centralized industrial/economic planning, not sport-specific
  physiology.
- **[NOVEL]** Seiler recounts a personal 1986 research trip to the USSR,
  photographed with Yuri Verkhoshansky and Alexei Medvedyev — establishing his
  direct lineage/credibility in periodization scholarship.
- **[NOVEL]** Cites John Kiely's "Periodization Paradigms in the 21st Century:
  Evidence-Led or Tradition-Driven?" (*International Journal of Sports
  Physiology and Performance*, 2012, 7:242-250) and lists the core assumptions
  Kiely says traditional periodization rests on, presented here as claims to be
  questioned rather than accepted: plan first then execute; use distinct
  training blocks; adaptations occur in a specific order; universal timeframes
  exist for eliciting and retaining specific fitness adaptations; progress is
  predictable and follows a set pattern; future training schemes can be
  accurately pre-planned.
- **[NOVEL]** Lists "cool periodization names" as largely synonymous marketing
  terms for variation — Reverse, Block, Non-linear, Fractal, Conjugate
  Sequence — with the deadpan aside that they're mostly "scientific sounding
  ways to say... Variation (but not TOO much)." A rhetorical device skeptical
  of periodization-theory proliferation.
- **[NOVEL]** Frames "successful endurance training" as three sequential
  requirements: (1) build the athlete's physiological and technical resources
  to maximal levels, (2) ensure they can fully mobilize those resources, (3)
  get the timing right.
- **[NOVEL]** Lists three commonly asserted endurance-training "truths"
  presented skeptically/for debate: "intensity is critical, volume is
  debatable"; "different training intensities give very specific adaptive
  effects"; and "to increase VO2max, just add more interval training" — the
  last explicitly flagged as a misconception via a string of question marks
  rather than endorsed.

### Elite-athlete training-distribution case studies

- **[OVERLAP]** (Steady State / aerobic-base physiology, new numbers) Average
  training-intensity distribution from 12 cross-country skiing Olympic/World
  Champion gold medalists during the year prior to their best gold-medal
  performance: ~91% low intensity, ~3% threshold, ~6% high intensity (Tønnessen,
  Sylta, Haugen, Hem, Svendsen, Seiler, "The Road to Gold: Training and Peaking
  Characteristics in the Year Prior to a Gold Medal Endurance Performance,"
  *PLOS ONE* 9(7): e101796, 2014).
- **[OVERLAP]** (aerobic-base philosophy, new case study) A champion XC skier's
  (Espen Tønnessen's own athlete data) basic season periodization: training
  hours peak around 82-88 hrs/month in June-October, then taper sharply toward
  the Olympics (down to ~6 hrs in April); total sessions per month track
  volume closely; even at peak volume, high-intensity (zone 3-5) sessions stay
  a small fraction of total sessions/hours throughout.
- **[OVERLAP]** (same theme, new numbers) That same skier's monthly training
  hours broken out by discipline (cycle/run/roller-ski/ski-on-snow) and by
  intensity zone (Z1-Z5 plus strength) — Zone 1 dominates every single month of
  the year, including within a few weeks of competition.
- **[OVERLAP]** (same theme, new case study) Across 12 Olympic Champions in
  XC skiing/biathlon, the pre-competitive period (August-October) endurance
  training stayed roughly constant at ~68-71 hours, with zones I1-2 (low
  intensity) still accounting for the overwhelming majority (~63-66 of ~68-71
  hours) even that close to competition.
- **[OVERLAP]** (same theme, new numbers) Training volume in the final 6 weeks
  before an Olympic/World Championship gold-medal race stays in the 11-14
  hrs/week range on average (individual athlete traces from ~7-22 hrs/week);
  meanwhile percent of high-intensity sessions rises steadily from ~20% at
  pre-competition to ~28% (weeks 6-3) to ~32% (weeks 2-1) — i.e., volume holds
  roughly flat while HIT frequency creeps up approaching the goal race.
- **[NOVEL]** A 2-time Olympic gold-medal-winning rower's full annual training
  log (gold-medal year): 1,041 total training hours, 598 total sessions, 4,911
  rowing kilometers, average 21.7 hrs/week and 12.5 sessions/week — with a
  monthly hours/sessions chart showing January as the highest-volume month
  (~124 hrs) and relatively even monthly session counts (51-61/month).
- **[NOVEL]** Same rower's monthly intensity-zone breakdown (I-zone 1 through
  6) shows I-zone 1 as the overwhelming majority of hours in every month,
  including the competitive months of June-August.
- **[NOVEL]** Same rower's specific high-intensity session prescriptions by
  zone, given as concrete numbers: Zone 3 — 60 min effective time (e.g., 3 x 20
  min, 5 min recovery); Zone 4 — 60 min (6 x 10 min, 5 min recovery); Zone 5 —
  20 min (e.g., 4 x 4 min, 3 min recovery).
- **[OVERLAP]** (aerobic-base philosophy, new anecdote/numbers) Ingrid
  Kristiansen — 5 world records, world champion — monthly training-hours chart
  split into Preparation and Competition periods, showing I-zone 1 as by far
  the largest component of volume in every month (peaking ~46-60 hrs/month in
  Jan-March preparation), with only modest I-zone 2-5 volume even during
  competition months.
- **[OVERLAP]** (marathon fueling/training already covered, new case study —
  distinct athlete) A 2:15 marathoner's (Øystein Sylta's) training-session-load
  scatter plot during a marathon buildup (Rotterdam), showing the vast bulk of
  sessions as low-intensity training (LIT) below a defined load threshold, a
  handful of higher-load HIT interval sessions, and a few extra-long LIT
  sessions exceeding even HIT session loads — with the marathon race itself
  registering the single highest session load (~1630) of the entire dataset.
- **[NOVEL]** Eliud Kipchoge's (a "2:03 marathoner (or 2:00:25)" — referencing
  his sub-2 Breaking2 attempt) session-type distribution across 41 days/59
  sessions before the 2017 Berlin Marathon: 34 easy runs (10-22 km), 7 medium
  intensity runs, 6 tempo runs (30-40 km), 6 fartlek sessions (7-14 km), 6
  track interval sessions (5-15 km total) — sourced from a published training
  diary (sweatelite.co).
- **[NOVEL]** Kipchoge's week-by-week session counts for the final 6 weeks
  before Berlin 2017: dominant zone-1 easy-run counts each week (8, 7, 4, 6, 6,
  4 sessions across weeks 6 through 1) plus roughly one each of zone-2
  sub-threshold runs, tempo sessions, fartlek sessions, and track-interval
  sessions per week — illustrating a stable weekly template rather than
  dramatic week-to-week variation.
- **[NOVEL]** Career-long (14-year, 8,587-hour) training-intensity distribution
  for Olympic XC skier Bente Skari: Zone 1 78%, Zone 2 12%, Zone 3 3.5%, Zone 4
  6%, Zone 5 <1% — with Zone 4 flagged as a possible "HIT sweet spot," and the
  explicit framing that achieving "the same or higher gain with less pain"
  supports long-term development and sustainability of the training process.

### The Sylta periodization-order experiment (does the *sequence* of interval intensity across a training block matter?)

- **[NOVEL]** Cites Øystein Sylta's award-winning study, "Does periodization
  matter? — The effect of different high intensity periodization models on
  endurance adaptations" (Norway), which won a "Coaching" category award from
  European Athletics.
- **[NOVEL]** Study design (published *Medicine & Science in Sports &
  Exercise* 48(11): 2165-2174, Nov 2016; N=63 cyclists, ~38 years old,
  VO2peak ~61 ml/kg/min average): three 12-week (3 x 4-week mesocycle)
  periodization arms, each prescribing 8 HIT sessions per mesocycle —
  Increasing (INC: 4x16min → 4x8min → 4x4min), Decreasing (DEC: reverse order),
  and Mixed (MIX: 4x16, 8, and 4 min every mesocycle). All intervals run at
  2 min rest, prescribed as "all-out" but constrained to even/progressive
  power without failure, with the goal of completing all 24 sessions as
  prescribed.
- **[NOVEL]** Session-level physiological load scaled predictably with
  interval duration regardless of periodization arm: 4x16min sessions averaged
  276 W / 4.7 mmol/L blood lactate / RPE 15.0; 4x8min sessions averaged 308 W /
  9.2 mmol/L / RPE 16.2; 4x4min sessions averaged 342 W / 12.6 mmol/L / RPE
  17.1 — i.e., shorter intervals reliably produced harder efforts.
- **[NOVEL]** Key finding: despite the three groups experiencing very
  different *sequencing* of HIT intensity across the 12 weeks, there was **no
  significant difference** in resulting gains in Power@40min, peak power
  output (PPO), Power@4mM lactate, or VO2peak between INC, DEC, and MIX groups
  (though all three groups improved significantly from baseline). A responder
  chi-square test found no significant association between periodization group
  and response-magnitude distribution (large/moderate/non-response) — directly
  challenging the traditional-periodization assumption that block *order*
  within a season is what drives superior adaptation.
- **[NOVEL]** A related 12-week HIT intervention study in well-trained cyclists
  (cited via ResearchGate, "Effects of HIT on Physiological and Hormonal
  Adaptions in Well-Trained Cyclists") found most of the measured gains in PPO,
  Power@4mM, and VO2peak occurred within the first 4 weeks of the intervention
  and plateaued or grew only modestly thereafter, again regardless of which
  periodization pattern (INC/DEC/MIX) was used.
- **[NOVEL]** Hormonal data from that same intervention: total testosterone
  dropped roughly 15-25% by week 4 across all three groups (statistically
  significant), then recovered by week 12 — with the MIX group showing the
  largest rebound (+42% at 12 weeks, significantly higher than the other
  groups). Cortisol showed no clear or consistent pattern across groups or
  timepoints. Presented as evidence against a simple "periodization sequencing
  drives overtraining/hormonal stress" narrative.
