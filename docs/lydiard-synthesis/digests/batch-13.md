## Lydiard Presentation Resources — "Essential Lydiard" by Lorraine Moller, *Running Times*, November 2009, plus a Keith Livingstone book excerpt on Peter Snell's 1962 training

- Opening anecdote: at the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, Lydiard's squad ran 20 x quarters in front of rivals, each rep faster than the last; a Canadian Olympic debutant who tried to match the session the next day failed to advance out of his heats, while Lydiard's runners (Snell — two golds; John Davies — bronze) peaked perfectly. Lydiard's response to a reporter asking about the workout: "I think it was the last nail in his coffin" — because, unlike his own runners, the Canadian "needed" that session. **[OVERLAP]** — reinforces existing overtraining/case-history coverage and "stand-alone workouts mean little" principle, but this specific Tokyo 1964 anecdote/quote is new.
- Ron Daws (Moller's coach) axiom: "Good training and bad training look exactly the same on paper." **[NOVEL]** — quotable coaching maxim.
- Moller's own training arc: coached across her 28-year career by three consecutive Lydiard-lineage coaches (John Davies, Ron Daws, Dick Quax); built to 40 miles/week under Davies as a 14-year-old; explicitly rejects "hard and fast rules... such as the 100-miles-per-week mandate" often wrongly attributed to Lydiard — training was individualized to age, gender, ability, fitness, and event. **[NOVEL]** — biographical detail plus an explicit misconception correction (the "100 mi/wk mandate" myth) not addressed in existing coverage.
- "Five principles" of Lydiard training per Moller: (1) *Miles in the Bank* — maximize aerobic capacity first; even races over 5000m are >85% dependent on aerobic metabolism; Lydiard described miles as "money in the bank," currency to buy ATP; (2) *Feeling-Based Training* — "tune into your inner coach," prescribing effort as fractions (half-effort one day, three-quarters/seven-eighths another) rather than fixed paces, predating heart-rate monitors; (3) *Response-Regulated Recovery* — training causes a catabolic breakdown phase followed by anabolic rebuild; adaptation happens during rest, not the workout; bad training = mismatch between breakdown/buildup; overtraining-needs-recovery symptoms: elevated morning HR, poor sleep, low energy, sore muscles, bad mood; (4) *Sequential Development of Energy Systems* — schedules built backward from goal race: aerobic conditioning (3-6 months) → strength/hills (4 wks) → interval training (4 wks) → speed/skills (4 wks) → taper (3-4 wks); "the bigger the base, the higher the peak"; (5) *Correct Timing* — peaking channels accumulated fitness to one specific point. **[OVERLAP heavy]** — items 1, 3, and 4 restate existing steady-state, overtraining-symptom, and taper-structure coverage; items 2 (the "inner coach"/effort-fraction framing) and the "85% aerobic over 5000m" figure are **[NOVEL]** additions.
- Dr. Izumi Tabata study (National Institute of Fitness and Sports, Tokyo) over six weeks showed interval training more efficient than steady exercise of lesser intensity — cited as commonly misused by "get-fit-quick" advocates, while ignoring that (a) anaerobic development only takes 4-6 weeks to peak, and (b) intervals work best/safest only after an endurance-and-strength base, lowering injury risk and improving interval quality. **[NOVEL]** — a named study used to directly correct a training misconception.
- Peter Snell direct quotes (as PhD in exercise physiology, decades later): "The core of Lydiard training is the quantity and quality of the base training... Long, moderate-pace running is anabolic whereas high-intensity demanding training[,] is catabolic. Thus the base is critical to prevention of overtraining." Snell also says he'd do little differently today except add "some long, easy intervals once a week during the base training." **[NOVEL]** — direct Snell retrospective quote with anabolic/catabolic framing and his one specific modern modification.
- Snell's explanation for why slow marathon-distance training benefits speed events like 800m despite specificity theory: long endurance runs protect against overtraining from high-intensity work (permitting more race-specific training overall); long moderate-pace runs also activate fast-twitch fibers once slow-twitch fibers become glycogen-depleted after 1-2 hours. **[OVERLAP]** — reinforces existing FT/ST recruitment/size-principle coverage, but the glycogen-depletion-triggered fast-twitch recruitment mechanism is a distinct new detail.
- Detailed training-log excerpt (Livingstone, from *Healthy Intelligent Training*): Snell's build to his 1:44.3 800m WR on grass (Jan 27, 1962, Christchurch). Returned exhausted from a European tour in Aug 1961, resumed training Sept 17; twice-daily to/from-work runs; Oct 21 exhibition mile in 4:13; first 100-mile week; by November confidently covering the 22-mile Waiatarua circuit (4 mi to Lydiard's house + full 22-mile loop in 2:11 — his best since before the Rome Olympics); a picnic-meet mile in 4:14 during hill-circuit weeks; competed in the Owairaka Marathon, led through 20 miles, faded, walked at 24 miles, finished in 2:41 fully depleted — that same afternoon, playing social cricket, was "clean bowled" after three balls, "my vision was obscured... there was no coordination at all." **[NOVEL]** — a highly granular dated training log with specific mileage/times, far beyond existing site coverage; the cricket-game glycogen-depletion anecdote is vivid and new.
- Log continues: recovered over the following week; a 9:18 two-mile in first track-training week; 10 x 440 averaging 59.8s off a 9:12 two-mile; a windy 3 miles in 14:23 (~14:50-equivalent 5000m); Christmas Day 1:52/4:15 half-mile/mile double; four days later 10 x half-mile averaging 2:10 with half-mile jog recovery, while maintaining 10-mile morning runs and the weekly long run; eased off, then won an 880y race series in 1:48.2 (800m split 1:47.7); the following Wednesday, despite feeling strong, ran his usual 22 miles instead of racing; ran 1:47.6 to beat Jamaica's George Kerr in the 880y at the 1962 Commonwealth Games (Perth); the next Wednesday ran 880y in 1:47.1 (800m split 1:46.3, tying his Olympic record); ten days later set the world mile record of 3:54.4 (last 440 in 54.8); eight months later won the 880y/mile double at the Commonwealth Games; thirty months later won the 800m/1500m Olympic double. **[NOVEL]** — an extremely granular, dated race-by-race/workout-by-workout log, unlike anything in existing coverage.
- Livingstone's physiological gloss: the pre-marathon hill-circuit fortnight "re-stimulated [Snell's] powerful type IIb fast-twitch fibers with plyometric input that reversed the normal 'size principle' of muscle fiber recruitment (smallest to largest)"; running the marathon at constant pace exhausted slow-twitch glycogen stores, forcing fast-twitch fibers to adapt, taking on aerobic-endurance characteristics alongside anaerobic glycolytic potential (becoming hybrid "type IIa" fibers) — a potential not realized until later hard sessions/races capitalized on it. **[OVERLAP]** — reinforces existing FT/ST size-principle and hill-training coverage, but the specific "long slow marathon reverses recruitment order via hill-plyometric priming" mechanism is a novel, more precise claim.
- Historical epilogue: John Walker ran the lead pack for 20+ miles at NZ's Rotorua Marathon (April 1975), then jogged off-course once his training "mission" was accomplished, ruffling other marathoners; months later became the first man under 3:50 for the mile; his coach Arch Jelley (Owairaka Athletic Club) famously applied Lydiard principles; Walker broke the world mile record (3:49.4) in 1975 after training the Waiatarua circuit with marathoner Kevin Ryan; in 1983, training the same circuit with marathoner Chris Pilone, he ran a 3:49.08 mile PB, still the NZ record. **[NOVEL]** — extends the Walker record narrative with training-partner/circuit specifics not previously captured.
- Barry Magee's observation (via Livingstone): Snell's in-season long training runs were "slow and restorative, about a minute a mile slower than his best aerobic speeds achieved in his buildup." **[NOVEL]** — quantifies the easy-run pace differential (~1 min/mile slower than peak aerobic pace), a specific number absent from existing coverage.

## Intolerable Acidosis from Lydiard Presentation.jpg

Single labeled physiology diagram (not decorative — substantive enough to extract). Shows heart rate/lactate-concentration curves plotted against intensity, with a "deflection point" where the bloodstream turns increasingly acidic toward a zone captioned "Intolerable Acidosis" (illustrated with a distressed cartoon figure). Accompanying text:

- Lactate (C₃H₆O₃⁻) is now understood as a substrate muscles both produce *and use* at very high work intensities — not a metabolic waste product or toxin; it's a 3-carbon glycolytic breakdown product of a 6-carbon glucose molecule splitting into two 3-carbon molecules. As a negatively charged alkali molecule, lactate can buffer positively charged hydrogen ions. **[NOVEL]** — a more precise, modern lactate-not-a-toxin reframing (with specific chemistry) than the existing site's general acid/alkali buffering-logic coverage.
- Rising work rates cause hydrogen-ion-driven acidosis to build in the blood past the "anaerobic threshold"; this is explicitly "no longer thought to be 'lactic acidosis.'" The limit of muscular work is reached when acid levels can no longer be buffered by the bicarbonate system or by mildly alkali lactate. **[NOVEL]** — same reframing point, extended to the exhaustion mechanism.
