## Lydiard Part 2 Principles.pdf — "The Five Lydiard Principles Applied Systematically"

**Identified author:** Keith Livingstone, presenting via his own HITSYSTEM (Healthy
Intelligent Training) clinic branding — every slide carries the HITSYSTEM logo, and two
slides feature a photo of a bald, bespectacled man in a red shirt drinking milk from a
bottle who reappears identically in Part 3 of this same slide series. A web search
confirms Keith Livingstone is the presenter/developer behind "HITSYSTEM," a modern
interpretation layer built on Lydiard's method, distinct from his book *Healthy
Intelligent Training* itself (per `existing-coverage.md`, "HITSYSTEM" material is
confirmed not yet covered on the site, separate from quoting the HIT book directly).
16 slides total; discarded ~2 pure decoration slides (Pisa/Giza tower photos used as a
visual joke, generic "training should be enjoyable" photo). Recommended citation:
`(Livingstone, HITSYSTEM Lydiard Clinic Slides — Principles)`.

- **[NOVEL]** Named framework: "The Five Lydiard Principles" — (1) maximize aerobic
  capacity with a massive aerobic base while improving running technique via *weekly*
  leg-speed drills, (2) make running fun and feeling-based, (3) balance workouts with
  recovery, (4) *sequentially* develop energy systems one day a week while maintaining
  easy aerobic volumes, (5) "timing is everything" (framed via a Shakespeare quote as a
  mnemonic). This specific five-point named checklist is not present in the existing
  coverage list.
- **[NOVEL]** Training pyramid adds a specific detail not in existing hill/fiber-type
  coverage: "Neuro-Muscular Training: Short Alactic Sprint drills" are prescribed
  **year-round**, run alongside every phase of the pyramid (not confined to one training
  block) — a discrete prescription detail.
- **[OVERLAP]** "Two Great Secrets of Lydiard Training" — (1) the greater the intensity
  of main sessions in a training phase, the more low-intensity recovery work is
  required; (2) significant aerobic volume can still be done right up until the peak.
  Reinforces the existing acid/alkali buffering-with-easy-volume logic, but packages it
  as a memorable named "two secrets" framing not previously captured.
- **[OVERLAP]** Acid/alkali fiber-environment triangle (very acidic environment at peak
  races / Fast Twitch IIA vs. mildly alkali environment / Slow Twitch Type I) —
  reinforces the existing fast-twitch/slow-twitch acid-alkali buffering coverage with no
  new number.
- **[NOVEL]** "Adaptation Curve" / supercompensation concept named and diagrammed
  explicitly: a training workout temporarily *decreases* fitness, and the subsequent
  recovery period causes fitness to rebound *beyond* the previous level — termed
  "Super-Compensation." Not present in the existing coverage list as a named concept.
- **[OVERLAP]** "Basic Principle of Adaptation": if workload is too light, or recovery
  too long, there is no fitness gain; if workload is too demanding or recovery
  inadequate, fitness keeps declining (fatigue/breakdown). Generic reinforcement of the
  existing "balance workouts with recovery" and overtraining-symptom coverage, no new
  number.
- **[NOVEL]** Full four-phase weekly training-plan table with actual daily-minute
  prescriptions — a concrete numeric schedule not present anywhere in existing coverage:
  - *Phase 1, Aerobic Buildup/Base* (run "for as long as possible"): Mon 40–60min slow
    jog; Tue hilly steady run 80–90min; Wed fun fartlek after speed drills; Thu hilly
    steady run 90–100min; Fri 40min very easy; Sat high-aerobic "Strong Run" up to
    60min; Sun hilly steady long run 120–150min.
  - *Phase 2, Hill Resistance Exercises* (up to 4 weeks, blending in): Mon 40–60min slow
    jog; Tue hill sessions; Wed 80–90min easy on trails/parkland; Thu fun fartlek after
    speed drills; Fri 40min very easy; Sat hill sessions; Sun long run.
  - *Phase 3, Early Anaerobic Phase* (up to 3 weeks, blending in): Tue VO2max track
    intervals; Sat race-relevant track sessions or early-season races (other days as
    Phase 2 pattern).
  - *Phase 4, Second Anaerobic Phase* (up to 3 weeks): Mon 60min slow jog; Tue lactate
    tolerance intervals; Sat middle-distance track race; Sun long run at easy recovery
    pace.
- **[NOVEL]** Specific peaking-window prescription: a specific hard anaerobic-overload
  session should land **11 days before** the peak race (e.g., hard session on a Tuesday
  before a Saturday race 11 days later), with the exact window adjusted per individual
  athlete and tested via lead-up races over a season. Explicitly credited to the
  supercompensation research of Nikolai Yakovlev (1911–1992) — a named source not
  present in existing coverage.
- **[OVERLAP]** "Too early / optimum / too late" recovery-timing graph illustrating that
  the same workout produces different training effects depending on how many days
  before the goal race it's placed — nuances the existing taper-structure coverage with
  a new visual framing but no new number.
- **[NOVEL]** Direct quote/aphorism (presented as the deck's stated "Lydiard's Energy
  System Training Secret"): *"We aim to have all the muscle fibre types and their
  energy systems well-trained, and well-rested within the training window leading into
  the days before the first important race. This creates race confidence."* A
  memorable, quotable framing tying peaking/taper and energy-system sequencing together
  that isn't in the existing coverage.

## Lydiard Part 3 Aerobic System.pdf — "Training the Muscle Fibre Types and Energy Systems Effectively"

**Identified author:** Keith Livingstone (same HITSYSTEM branding and identical
milk-drinking presenter photo as Part 2 above). Recommended citation:
`(Livingstone, HITSYSTEM Lydiard Clinic Slides — Aerobic System)`. 21 slides total;
discarded ~3 pure photo/diagram-only slides with no independent textual claim (generic
muscle-fiber-bundle anatomy illustration, duplicate peak-racing-phase pyramid already
extracted from Part 2, a bare capillary micrograph credited only in-image).

- **[OVERLAP]** Three muscle fiber types restated (Type IIB alactic anaerobic — no
  acidosis even at very high work output; Type IIA lactic anaerobic — creates acidosis
  at high work output; Type I aerobic — creates H2O + CO2), with tagline "Acidosis is
  the enemy!" Reinforces existing fiber-type/acid-alkali coverage; the explicit
  alactic-vs-lactic split between the two anaerobic fiber types (IIB vs. IIA) is a
  slightly sharper distinction than what's already covered.
- **[NOVEL]** Fuel-source-by-fiber-type breakdown not present in existing coverage: Type
  IIB draws on intramuscular creatine phosphate (described as a "self-priming phosphate
  battery" — very powerful but limited); Type IIA draws on intramuscular muscle glycogen
  (chains of glucose — powerful for longer); Type I draws on both fats and glucose, with
  fats described as "by far the most efficient" fuel.
- **[NOVEL]** Numeric energy-system handoff timeline during exercise to exhaustion:
  ATP/PC system (Type IIB) is the main energy source in the first 10 sec, peaking at
  ~5 sec, fatiguing from ATP/PC depletion; the lactic acid system (Type IIA) is the main
  source from 10–30 sec, peaking at ~20 sec, providing energy for up to 2 min, and
  notably "CAN acquire aerobic properties with endurance training" (a fiber-type
  metabolic-conversion claim); the aerobic system (Type I) becomes the main source from
  30 sec onward with effectively unlimited capacity absent a fuel-supply limit.
- **[NOVEL]** Fiber contraction-power-vs.-load chart (FTx/Type IIB, FTa/Type IIA, ST/Type
  I): FTx generates by far the highest peak contraction power (~80 Newtons) around
  20–30% of maximum load before dropping sharply; FTa peaks lower (~50N); ST/Type I stays
  low (~10N) across the load range — a specific physiology figure not referenced
  elsewhere in existing coverage.
- **[NOVEL]** Molecular/biochemical detail absent from existing coverage: glucose
  (C6H12O6), glycogen (chain of stored glucose molecules), and palmitic acid (a
  long-chain fatty acid, C16H32O2) presented with full chemical structures, plus the
  actual metabolic equations and ATP yields — anaerobic glycolysis in Type IIA fibers
  (glucose → 2 lactate + 2 ATP, indefinable R.Q.), aerobic glycolysis in Type IIA fibers
  (glucose + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + 36 ATP, R.Q. = 1.0), and aerobic lipolysis of fat in
  Type I fibers (palmitic acid + 23O2 → 16CO2 + 16H2O + **130 ATP**, R.Q. = 0.696,
  stated to hold constant "throughout the aerobic intensities"). The 2-vs-36-vs-130 ATP
  comparison across pathways is a genuinely new hard number for the site.
- **[NOVEL]** Named coaching term "bread and butter" for steady aerobic/recovery runs,
  paired with an example weekly mileage distribution: Mon 10mi, Tue 15mi (steady "bread
  and butter"), Wed ~10mi (light fartlek with short sharp alactic strideouts), Thu 18mi
  (higher-effort sub-threshold aerobic), Fri 10mi, Sat 15mi, Sun 22mi (steady "bread and
  butter"). Specific numeric example not in existing coverage.
- **[NOVEL]** Specific heart-rate-zone number for endurance-base building: best trained
  via long efforts at perceived-steady intensity in the "bread and butter" zone of
  **60–75% MHR**; notes that actual running speed at the same perceived intensity/HR
  gets faster over time as an aerobic-economy marker.
- **[NOVEL]** Specific dosage guidance for sub-threshold marathon-pace running within the
  aerobic pyramid: described as "extremely useful and safe" but capped at **1 hour,
  one-to-two times per week** — a concrete frequency/duration prescription not in
  existing coverage (existing coverage references sub-threshold/marathon-pace running as
  a category but not this specific dosage).
- **[OVERLAP]** Aerobic training benefits list (strengthens muscles/joints/tendons/
  cardiovascular system; capillarizes running muscles) reinforces existing
  capillary-density coverage; **[NOVEL]** nuance: aerobic training also "capillarizes...
  the heart itself" and "hugely increases the oxidative enzymes in the muscle cells'
  MITOCHONDRIA... as well as the population of MITOCHONDRIA in the slow twitch
  muscles" — a mitochondrial-biogenesis mechanism not named in existing coverage.
- **[OVERLAP]** "Best training for Type I slow twitch fibres: long, relaxed training
  efforts over varied circuits, enjoyment is key" — reinforces the existing "make
  running fun" philosophy and beginner "pleasantly tired" coverage with no new number.
- **[NOVEL]** Cardiac-muscle-specific physiology not in existing coverage: cardiac
  muscle is characterized as "a highly specialized slow twitch muscle that never rests
  for the duration of a lifetime," which benefits from deep capillarization via aerobic
  training overload — extending the existing (skeletal-muscle) capillary-density
  coverage specifically to the heart.
- **[NOVEL]** Two distinct types of exercise-induced ventricular hypertrophy, presented
  with real academic citations: **eccentric hypertrophy** (from aerobic endurance
  overload) produces a bigger ventricular volume with a mildly thicker wall and larger
  stroke volume, vs. **concentric hypertrophy** (from resistance-training overload,
  e.g., heavy back-pressure loading) which produces an unchanged/smaller ventricular
  volume with a much thicker muscular wall and a powerful ejection fraction but smaller
  stroke volume. Cited to Lewis, McKillop & Banks, "The Morganroth hypothesis
  revisited," *J Physiol* 2012;590(Pt 12):2833–2834, and "The Nature of Cardiac
  Remodeling Due to Physical Exercise," *Arc Bras Cardiol* 2018;111(6):782–783. This is
  the richest genuinely new scientific claim in the batch — nothing on cardiac
  remodeling type (eccentric vs. concentric hypertrophy) appears in existing coverage.
- **[OVERLAP]** Before/after endurance-training capillary micrograph (credited to D.L.
  Costill, photo by L. Hermansen) showing visibly denser capillary network post-training
  — reinforces the existing capillary-density coverage with a different named source/
  image than the Swedish-vs-Kenyan quadriceps study already covered.
- **[NOVEL]** Fat-adaptation ceiling claim with a specific number: a trained aerobic
  athlete can run on fat fuel at 80% of capacity at what was formerly their 100%
  capacity (untrained), without depleting carbohydrate stores — i.e., training raises
  the fat-oxidation ceiling. Ties to existing honey-vs-carb-loading fueling coverage but
  is a distinct claim/mechanism (extending fat-burning capacity via aerobic training,
  not marathon-day fueling choice) with its own number.
- **[NOVEL]** Named athlete case study: René Borg (Dublin) — a "steady state pace"
  progression chart from 2008–2012 showing year-over-year increases in both steady-state
  and max pace (kph) over a 5-year training arc, used to illustrate real-world aerobic
  progression. Not referenced anywhere in existing coverage.
