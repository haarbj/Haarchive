## `resources/Lydiard/Lydiard Method Presentation.pdf`

**Title:** *Arthur Lydiard's Athletic Training* — "A Guide to the Brooks / American Track & Field Lydiard Running Lecture Tour in 1999." Byline: "by Arthur Lydiard." Prepared by Nobuya "Nobby" Hashizume and Steve Bobenhouse (Fitness Sports, Des Moines, IA). The body text is first-person Lydiard ("I always tell runners...", "I discovered years ago...") transcribed/prepared by Hashizume & Bobenhouse for the tour guide — **not** the *Running to the Top* book, so cite as `(Lydiard, Athletic Training — 1999 Lecture Tour)`. One embedded subsection is explicitly credited to a different author (see below) and must be cited separately.

Note: the file's own table of contents promises a Glossary, a sample 10K schedule, and a blank schedule template as items 16–18, but the PDF (29 pages, read in full) ends mid-glossary on page 29 — those sections are not present in this copy.

### Biography / historical anecdotes (Lydiard, as prepared by Hashizume & Bobenhouse)

- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard born Eden Park, NZ, 1917; ran and boxed in school but was most interested in rugby; dropped out at 16 during the Depression to work in a shoe factory.
- **[NOVEL]** Origin story: Jack Dolan (president of the Lynndale Athletic Club) took a "pretty fit" Lydiard on a 5-mile training jog that left him completely exhausted, prompting him to wonder how he'd feel at 47 if a 5-mile run wrecked him at 27.
- **[NOVEL]** Found F. Wesbter's *The Science of Athletics* at the club library; judged its schedules too easy and began experimenting on himself.
- **[NOVEL]** Ran 7 days/week up to 12 miles/day early on — "exceptional" for the era; later tested himself up to **250 miles/week** as his own guinea pig.
- **[NOVEL]** 1945, age 28: returned to racing but struggled to win despite higher mileage than his rivals because he was "hitting form at the wrong times" — this frustration led him to experiment with hard/easy day alternation.
- **[NOVEL]** Lawrie King, after two years training with Lydiard, won a provincial 2-mile championship by 80 meters — the result that established Lydiard as a coach, "a qualification he neither sought nor particularly wanted." King went on to become NZ cross-country champion, 6-mile record holder, and a 1954 Empire Games rep.
- **[OVERLAP]** — team-coaching habits (Magee) — 1951: 17-year-old Murray Halberg's coach Bert Payne consulted Lydiard; by 1953 Halberg trained fully under Lydiard and was joined by Barry Magee. Adds the specific origin timeline, not previously captured.
- **[NOVEL]** 1955: Lydiard stopped racing to focus on business; worked two jobs until 1957, one of which was delivering milk in the middle of the night.
- **[NOVEL]** 1957: marathoner Ray Puckett won the NZ national championship with Lydiard (age 40) placing second.
- **[NOVEL]** Rome 1960 results: Peter Snell outsprinted Roger Moens for 800m gold; Murray Halberg broke away half a mile from the tape for 5000m gold; Barry Magee took marathon bronze behind Bikila and Rhadi.
- **[NOVEL]** Direct characterization: by 1960 Lydiard "knew his methods worked... though he didn't know the how" — he had only basic physiology knowledge, no formal coaching/physiology education, and never attended college.
- **[NOVEL]** Tokyo 1964: Snell won gold in both 800m and 1500m; John Davies (also Lydiard-trained) took 1500m bronze.
- **[NOVEL]** Mid-1960s: Lydiard pivoted to working with coaches rather than athletes. Early 1966, via an invitation arranged by Bud Winter (ex-San Jose State coach), he spent ~8 months in Mexico, producing Alfredo Penaloza (3rd at Boston 1969), Pablo Garrido (2:12'52), and Juan Martinez (4th, 1968 Mexico City Olympics 5000m & 10000m).
- **[NOVEL]** Finland: Lydiard spent 19 months there at the Finnish Track & Field Association's invitation, to "mixed reviews" — the Finns had misapplied 1950s interval concepts and become "interval junkies," and were stubborn/reluctant to accept his suggestions. Coaches Oekka Vasala and Lasse Viren listened and blended his ideas with Percy Cerutty, Nurmi, and Mihaly Igloi. Payoff: Olavi Suomalainen won the 1972 Boston Marathon.
- **[NOVEL]** Munich 1972: Viren fell in the 10,000m, got up, and won gold with a world record; won the 5000m too (final mile 4:01); Pekka Vasala took 1500m gold, Tapio Kantanen steeplechase bronze. It had been 7 years since a Finnish distance record fell before Lydiard arrived; 4 years after he left, Finland again owned world records, Olympic golds, and international titles.
- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard's methods were also applied to rugby, cycling, canoeing, squash, and gridiron football.

### System / physiology (Lydiard)

- **[NOVEL]** Cites Morehouse & Miller's *Physiology of Exercise* with a specific numeric table: raising speed 5.56→6.45 yd/sec costs an extra 3.67 L/min of O2 (5.08→8.75 L/min); raising speed 9.10→9.23 yd/sec costs an extra 5.50 L/min (28.46→33.96 L/min) for a much smaller speed gain. States aerobic exercise is **"19 times more efficient"** than anaerobic per Morehouse & Miller.
- **[NOVEL]** Explicitly frames conditioning-phase intensity as 70–100% of max aerobic effort and states outright "this, therefore, is **not** Long Slow Distance."
- **[OVERLAP]** — steady state/oxygen-debt ceiling — gives the illustrative worked numeric example: Steady State of 3 L/min running at a pace requiring 4 L/min lasts ~15 min (1 L/min debt); at 5 L/min requirement, empties in ~7.5 min (2 L/min debt). Same 15–18 liter absolute ceiling as existing coverage, but adds this specific worked math.
- **[NOVEL]** Practical anaerobic-training rule: distance must be **at least 200m** to lower blood pH generally — stated as the key threshold.
- **[NOVEL]** Coaching cue: anaerobic work doesn't need a track — "pick a tree or a marker to run to and jog back after each run" on a forest trail or grassy field.
- **[OVERLAP]** — sharpening concept — specific prescription: short sharp sprints of 50–100m with 50–100m floats between, done once weekly, is most effective for maintaining anaerobic development without dragging condition down.

### Marathon conditioning (Lydiard)

- **[NOVEL]** Direct quote: **"miles make the champions."**
- **[NOVEL]** Claim: marathon runners are usually better performers nearer age 40 than in earlier years, if they continue systematic long aerobic training — general cardiac efficiency takes years to build.
- **[NOVEL]** Practical pacing technique: run out for a fixed time (e.g., 10 min) and try to run back in nearly the same time; if the return takes longer, you went out too fast.
- **[NOVEL]** Specific time-based weekly build-up template (not mileage-based): Mon 1hr, Tue 1.5hr, Wed 1hr, Thu 1.5–2hr, Fri 1hr, Sat 2hr+, Sun 1–1.5hr.
- **[NOVEL]** Once base is established, switch to per-mile pace control: run a measured course, time it, then hold/tighten that pace week over week (worked example: 1hr for a 10-mile trial → target 6:00/mi next week → tighten toward 5:55/mi as fitness improves).
- **[OVERLAP]** — capillary-density study — Lydiard's own historical practice: settled on ~100 miles/week at near-best aerobic effort, discovered alternating 10mi one day / 20mi the next produced better results than a flat 15mi/day, attributing it to better muscular capillarization from the longer (2+ hour) efforts. Adds a new personal number and a new specific claim (alternating long/short beats flat mileage) to the capillary-density material.
- **[NOVEL]** Full specific sample weekly mileage schedule with effort levels: Mon 10mi(15km) 1/2 effort undulating; Tue 15mi(25km) 1/4 effort flat; Wed 12mi(20km) 1/2 effort hilly; Thu 18mi(30km) 1/4 effort flat; Fri 10mi(15km) 3/4 effort flat; Sat 22mi(35km) 1/4 effort flat; Sun 15mi(25km) 1/4 effort any terrain (~102 mi/week total).
- **[NOVEL]** Running-form cue: relaxed arms following through low and loose, "thumbs brushing the side seams of the training shorts"; hips comfortably forward; head up, looking ~30 yards ahead; knees to a comfortable height, not shuffling.

### Hill resistance training (Lydiard)

- **[NOVEL]** Claim that a form of "osotonic" [sic, likely isotonic] exercise develops white/fast-twitch muscle fibers better than isometric exercise, with speed developing quickly — a contrasting take to the isometric-hold progression already on the site.
- **[OVERLAP]** — three hill-exercise types — reinforces up-hill springing using body weight as resistance, stretching muscles/tendons to competition-level extremes for flexibility/speed and injury prevention.
- **[NOVEL]** Mechanistic claim: strong, flexible ankles directly increase stride length.
- **[NOVEL]** Biomechanical explanation: hips comfortably forward → knees higher → feet follow through higher → shortens the lever → faster leg action.
- **[NOVEL]** Specific hill-circuit spec: grade "near one in three, or a little steeper"; 200–300m+ long; flattish base area 200–400m for sprinting; jog area at top.
- **[NOVEL]** Detailed circuit protocol: 15+ min warm-up; discard extra clothes at the base; spring uphill with a bouncing action and slower forward progression (more resistance the slower you go); lift the center of gravity up and down, not just the knees; relaxed upper body, loose arms; head up (looking down throws the hips back); knees high, hips forward.
- **[OVERLAP]** — downhill/eccentric technique — adds specifics: jog ~3 min at the hilltop before running downhill fast with a relaxed striding action to build leg speed and stretch for stride length.
- **[NOVEL]** Windsprints at the hill base: vary 50–400m each circuit, every 15 minutes if the circuit is short; best results using 50/100/200/400m windsprints.
- **[NOVEL]** Full weekly structure: ~1-hour hill sessions, 15+ min cool-down, 3 days/week; alternate days for leg-speed running (also 3x/week); one day a long run of 1.5–2 hours.

### Leg-speed / track training (Lydiard)

- **[NOVEL]** Leg-speed protocol: nearly flat area with a gradual decline, 120–150m; 15+ min warm-up; run the course 10 times with a full 3-minute recovery interval (don't rush it).
- **[NOVEL]** Coaching cue: **"MOVING THE LEGS FAST"** — think only about leg turnover, not stride length; compares it to descending stairs one at a time as fast as possible.
- **[NOVEL]** Best results from 4–6 weeks of leg-speed training; train twice daily (even just 15 min in the morning) during this period.
- **[OVERLAP]** — taper/interval-pace periodization — the 10-week, 3-section track-training framework itself (anaerobic/speed weeks 1–4, then 4.5-week coordination block, then freshening) is the broader periodization skeleton already reflected on the site, but this document supplies much more granular numbers throughout (see below), so flagged overlap-with-new-numbers rather than pure overlap.
- **[NOVEL]** First-4-weeks interval menu: target ~3 miles/5000m of fast running per session — e.g., 12×400m, 6×600m, 5×1000m, or 3×1mile, with equal-distance recovery jogs; form doesn't matter as long as the athlete finishes fatigued.
- **[NOVEL]** Claim: younger athletes should do less anaerobic training; the anaerobic-to-aerobic ratio only increases as athletes get older and fitter.
- **[NOVEL]** Detailed alternate-day sprint-drill workout: warm-up 15 min, exercises 10–15 min, easy run 5 min, then 70–100m runs w/ 3-min jog between, targeting (A) stride length via exaggerated strides + hard back-leg push-off, (B) running tall/high knees/high on toes, (C) fast leg turnover — each run 2–3 times.
- **[NOVEL]** "Sharpening" example, called **"putting the edge on the knife"**: 400m×20 takes a long time and exhausts you, vs. 5 laps sprinting 50m every 100m (floating the other 50m) = 20 sprints in only ~7–8 minutes for a mature athlete, similarly tiring — done once weekly, typically Monday.
- **[NOVEL]** Diagnostic time-trial protocol: for 5000/10000m runners use 5000m trials (occasional 10000m); for 800/1500m use under-distance trials (600m for 800, 1200m for 1500) because the speed involved causes larger O2 debts/longer recovery. Use splits to diagnose weaknesses — easy early but tired late → run over-distance next; hard throughout but strong finish → run under-distance.
- **[NOVEL]** Full weekly coordination-phase template: Tue time trial, Wed sprint training + club sprint race, Thu pace-judgment at race pace 2–4x (or more sharpeners), Fri leg speed 120m×4–6, Sat open competition (development races, over/under distance), Sun leisurely long run.
- **[OVERLAP]** — taper structure — freshening-up duration given as ~10 days on average (individualized via trial and error), with a passing reference to "Super Compensation" as a term some coaches use for the same concept.
- **[NOVEL]** Complete reverse-periodization algorithm, counting back from race date: 10 days freshening → 6 weeks coordination (incl. freshening) → 4 weeks anaerobic development → 4 weeks hill resistance → remaining time = conditioning (aerobic mileage only, then easy fartlek + strong 5K/10K runs, with 10K only once/2wks and 5K three times/2wks) → continuation racing every 3 weeks.
- **[NOVEL]** Very granular week-by-week schedule tables for each phase (Conditioning 2-week cycles, Hill Resistance 4 weeks, Anaerobic 4 weeks, Coordination 4 weeks, Freshening 2 weeks) — built from already-covered building blocks but with specific day-by-day durations/rep counts not previously captured (e.g., Coordination Mon: wind-sprints totaling 2000–4000m, either 50m/100m or 100m/200m pattern, 10–20 reps).
- **[NOVEL]** Race-week/non-race-week templates for cross country and road, both reinforcing "race every 3 weeks" but with sport-specific numbers (e.g., road non-race week: reps 1mi×3 or 880yd×6, time trial 3mi twice/week; road race week: windsprints 100m×6–10, time trial 1mi, striding 100m×4–6, jog, race).

### "Training Considerations" — **by John Davies, Olympic medalist** (separate author, cite as `(Davies, Lydiard Method Presentation)`)

21 numbered points; most novel or notable ones:
- **[NOVEL]** "You need to be at a peak for the day. It is not necessarily the best athlete who wins, it is the best prepared."
- **[NOVEL]** "Choose the event that best suits you internationally. Basic speed is the governing factor."
- **[NOVEL]** "Once anaerobic training is started, it must be continued, otherwise development is lost."
- **[NOVEL]** "Once conditioning is finished, the performance level is determined."
- **[NOVEL]** "Anaerobic development only takes 10 to 12 weeks to achieve maximum levels."
- **[NOVEL]** Misconception explicitly addressed: **"It is a fallacy that anaerobic training develops speed. It in fact counteracts speed."**
- **[NOVEL]** "Anaerobic development requires volume training, i.e. longer repetitions, not short sharp ones or short intervals. After three weeks of heavy overload anaerobic training, the athlete needs to decide whether to back off during the fourth week and start the shorter sharper workouts" — a specific 3-weeks-on/4th-week-back-off protocol.
- **[OVERLAP]** — misconception addressed re: anaerobic training during conditioning — "It is a mistake to use anaerobic training during the conditioning phase," plus several other points (aerobic threshold requirement, athlete-determined rep counts, evaluate daily reactions, balance aerobic/anaerobic/speed) reinforce material already on the site without adding new numbers.

### For Joggers / general jogging physiology (Lydiard)

- **[NOVEL]** Claim: Sports Medicine Institutes worldwide rate jogging as the best exercise for developing cardiac efficiency, second only to cross-country skiing.
- **[NOVEL]** Specific figure: during strenuous exercise, the heart can pump about 34 liters of blood per minute.
- **[NOVEL]** Novices should stay well within capacity for at least six or more weeks before benefits (higher O2 uptake, sustainable faster pace) become evident.
- **[OVERLAP]** — beginner walk/jog progression — more granular numeric progression: if accustomed to 15 min daily, increase to ~30 min every third day, reverting to 15 min on the two days between, until stamina improves; continue until able to run an hour or more; then move to 2–3 longer runs/week (30 min ×2, plus 45min–1hr on the third day).

### Technique, "Golden Rules," and misc. practical cues (Lydiard)

- **[NOVEL]** Technique detail: upright posture, hips directly under torso, relaxed arms coming through low and up so thumbs are "just inside the shoulders" (note: a second cue elsewhere in the same document says thumbs should brush the shorts' side seams — an internal inconsistency worth flagging rather than resolving); staying tall enables a stronger drive off the back leg and lighter footstrike; don't hit the ground hard with bent knees/sitting back.
- **[NOVEL]** Stride-out drill: stride ~100m+ then jog ~300m before repeating, gradually coordinating speed with endurance training.
- **[NOVEL]** Counterintuitive surface/jarring claim: dry grassy areas are best for beginners specifically because the *slower* you run, the *harder* you land — i.e., slow running produces more jarring, not less.
- **[NOVEL]** Specific fat-metabolism claim: after about 30 minutes of running at a good speed, fat metabolism starts providing most of the energy.
- **[NOVEL]** Specific digestion rule: give the digestive system 2–3 hours before exercising; don't jog straight after meals.
- **[NOVEL]** Specific claim: marathoners need relatively little anaerobic training since marathons are run mostly at fast aerobic effort; 5K–10K time trials are sufficient to develop the anaerobic capacity needed to race a marathon.
- **[NOVEL]** Heat-adaptation mechanism: hot-weather training develops the skin arterioles that allow more blood to be pumped to the skin surface for cooling; sauna baths can assist this adaptation.

### "When You Run a Marathon, Be Sure That You" — 18-point race-day checklist (Lydiard)

- **[NOVEL]** Specific honey protocol: eat up to 8oz of honey supplementary to normal meals in the two days prior to the race (more specific than the existing general "honey over carb-loading" coverage).
- **[NOVEL]** Finish eating ~3 hours before the start; light breakfast of cereals, honey, toast, tea/coffee.
- **[NOVEL]** Use lubricant (olive oil, lanolin) under arms and crotch; force heels hard into the shoe backs before lacing firmly (not too tight) to prevent blister-causing foot movement.
- **[NOVEL]** Don't exaggerate knee lift on race day — relax and lift knees only as high as necessary to conserve the leg-lift muscles.
- **[OVERLAP]** — salt-tablet skepticism — reinforced ("Do not take salt tablets"), plus new specifics: prepare electrolyte drinks weaker than directed and add honey; drink water/electrolytes throughout a hot race, with a glass just before the start.
- **[NOVEL]** Keep the body wet — sponging is "the best insurance against dehydration and high body temperatures."
- **[NOVEL]** Do not use anti-perspirants (presumably to preserve sweat-cooling).

### Shoe lacing and nutrition (Lydiard)

- **[NOVEL]** Lacing technique (with diagram): lace so tightening doesn't pull down on the sinews/metatarsals on top of the foot; improper lacing creates pressure points that become painful as the foot swells and can restrict/damage the foot.
- **[NOVEL]** Claim: natural foods are the best nutrition source because they contain not just vitamins/minerals but the enzymes needed to use them.
- **[NOVEL]** Extensive micronutrient rundown not represented anywhere in existing coverage — calcium (cramp relief, bone depletion if deficient), magnesium (sleeplessness as a deficiency sign), potassium (**overlaps and substantially extends the existing salt-tablet/potassium mechanism** with a symptom list — nausea, muscle weakness, cramps, irritability, collapse — and food sources: bananas, oranges, tomatoes, cabbage, celery, carrots, grapefruit, apples, beans, fish), iron (~18mg/day, aided by vitamin C), chromium, zinc, vitamins A, B1 (~5mg/day), B2, B3/niacin, B6, B12, B15 (claims it doubles ADP-to-ATP recovery and speeds low-pH blood recovery), vitamin C (extraordinary claim of detoxifying "heroin, nicotine, alcohol and cancer causing pollutants"; 1000mg with a meal claimed to boost iron absorption tenfold), vitamin D, vitamin E. Flagging the vitamin C claims specifically as dubious/unverified rather than usable physiological fact.

### Training Terms glossary (Lydiard, cuts off mid-list at document end)

- **[NOVEL]** Distinguishes named terms: "Easy Fartlek Running" (undulating terrain, mixing fast strides/hill sprints/downhill striding, easy jog as desired) vs. **"Strong Fartlek Running"** (same but pushed, finishing in a tired state) — the strong/easy fartlek distinction as named terms is new.
- **[OVERLAP]** — hill-exercise types — "Hill Springing" defined again here (gentle slope, bouncing action, slow forward momentum, hard push off toes on alternating feet).

## `resources/Lydiard/Lydiard First Steps 2018 (1).pdf`

**Title:** *Lydiard® FIRSTEPS — Beginners Running Course, "Igniting Life-long Fitness & Vitality."* Publisher: Lydiard Foundation Headquarters, Boulder, CO. Principal instructors named on the closing pages: **Lorraine Moller** (President, 4-time Olympian) and **Nobuya "Nobby" Hashizume** (Director of Coaching). This is a modern Lydiard Foundation product, not Lydiard's own words — cite as `(Lydiard Foundation, First Steps)`.

- **[NOVEL]** Diagnostic self-test for program entry: go outside and jog, timing how long you can go without stopping; if you can't run 15 minutes continuously, this program is for you.
- **[NOVEL]** Explicit misconception addressed: contrasts itself with modern marathon-training programs geared to getting beginners to just "survive" a marathon (5–7 hours after 3–4 months) via heavy walk breaks and linear long-run increases regardless of individual response — without systematically building both cardiovascular and neuromuscular bases first, injury/fatigue/burnout risk is much greater.
- **[NOVEL]** Names a "5 Lydiard® Principles" framework: (1) Maximize Your Aerobic Capacity / "Building Your Base," (2) Feeling-Based Activity ("body rapport is essential"), (3) Response-Regulated Recovery (adaptation happens during recuperation, not the activity itself), (4) Sequential Development of Energy Systems (endurance → strength → anaerobic → pace → speed, each phase building on the last; insufficient endurance base means pace ultimately suffers), (5) Correct Timing — "a silver bullet workout, done at the wrong time, can have a completely opposite effect of that which you are seeking."
- **[NOVEL]** Detailed interval-progression mechanics for the Stage 1/Stage 2 program: begin 3x/week, warm-up 3–5 min walk, then Jog 1–4min/Walk 1–4min repeated 3x, cooldown 3–5 min (first workouts total only 15 min); jog segment lengthens from 1→4 minutes over 4 weeks while the walk segment shortens by the same amount; each new week resets to the easy workout (usually Day 2) before Day 3 pushes further.
- **[NOVEL]** Stage 2 specifics: introduces a 10-minute continuous run on Day 3, building to 20 minutes continuous by the end of the 4-week stage; by Week 8 the longest single session reaches ~30–31 minutes.
- **[NOVEL]** "Phasing Out Walking Breaks" coaching-psychology point: walking breaks let beginners cover more distance/duration than ever before, but it's the *continued* effort that drives cardiac adaptation; heart rate drops surprisingly fast during a walk break, and walk breaks become less physically necessary faster than expected — don't let them become a mental crutch, since the sooner you drop them the faster you progress (and once dropped, having to walk again starts to feel like an annoyance).
- **[NOVEL]** "Time on Your Feet" principle, stated as an explicit misconception: if you're slowing down toward the end of a continuous run, slow the *overall* pace rather than push — "Don't let anybody fool you that you can be better off doing faster for less time." Total time on feet matters more than speed at this stage.
- **[NOVEL]** Memorable line on habit formation: **"the hardest part of any running program is turning the doorknob."** Claims it takes a minimum of 3 weeks for a new habit to form and for physiology to "kick in," after which the body will not only expect the exercise but relish it.
- **[NOVEL]** Explicit claim on cross-training: running is unique in pushing full body weight against gravity, unlike cycling or swimming where body weight is supported; certain adaptations may not transfer from non-weight-bearing substitute activities, so an extra easy running day (vs. cross-training) speeds recovery more.
- **[NOVEL]** HR monitor guidance with a named analogy: use a heart-rate monitor like "training wheels on a bicycle" — useful until you become proficient at reading your own body signals, since long-term success depends on that internal gauge, not the device.
- **[NOVEL]** Named, worked target-heart-rate formula (Karvonen-style variant): (220 − Age − Resting HR) × 70% + Resting HR = Target HR (women add 3); ±5bpm gives the training range. Worked example: Hanna, age 30, resting HR 70 → target 157 → range 152–162.
- **[NOVEL]** Explicit misconception addressed: "no pain, no gain" mentality "has derailed more beginners than anything else." Explains the cardiovascular system develops much faster than the muscular system, and muscles/joints/ligaments (with lesser blood supply) need more time to strengthen even though the athlete may *feel* capable of running faster sooner.
- **[NOVEL]** 4-signal fitness-progress checklist for beginners: (1) running further in the same duration, (2) reaching the turnaround point in less time, (3) reaching the turnaround point in the same time at a lower pulse, (4) running faster at the same pulse. If none of these show — especially with an elevated pulse — you're over-working and should back off. Paired with the mantra: **"LET THE PACE COME TO YOU! You cannot, and should not, squeeze it out of yourself."**
- **[OVERLAP]** — "pleasantly tired"/"you can never run too slowly" philosophy — the First Steps "Golden Rules" restate this Lydiard through-line a third time across this batch (also appears twice in the companion PDF above), in its own phrasing: "Train, don't strain!"; "You can NEVER run too slowly... If in doubt, do less"; "Always listen to your body; not the predetermined schedule." Worth noting as a heavily recurring Lydiard motif across sources, even though each restatement itself is not new content.
- **[NOVEL]** Outro names a "Lydiard Training Pyramid" and "Ascent to Peak Fitness" as branded framework/marketing terms tied to RunningWizard.com's paid program tiers (Up & Running, Up & Racing, Bucket List plans) — low value, mostly promotional, but a distinct piece of Lydiard-brand terminology not seen before.
- **[NOVEL]** Biographical anecdote: Nobuya Hashizume discovered Lydiard's book *Running the Lydiard Way* in 1978, was impressed enough to contact Lydiard directly, relocated to New Zealand, and was personally coached by Lydiard for 12 months.
- **[OVERLAP]** — Lorraine Moller / *On the Wings of Mercury* — additional biographical detail (4-time Olympian, Olympic bronze medalist, 16 major-marathon wins incl. Boston, only woman to run every 20th-century Olympic women's marathon; her 2008 autobiography was launched by NZ PM Helen Clark and hit #2 on the NZ bestseller list). This is context about Moller and her book, not a new anecdote drawn from the book's contents, so low incremental value against the existing Moller citation.
