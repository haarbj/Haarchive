### Heart rate / threshold physiology (pp. 43-45)
- **[NOVEL]** Training adaptation illustrated as three paired heart-rate/effort curves: resting
  HR drops post-training, the gap between new resting HR and max HR ("work capacity") widens,
  and the acidity (lactate)-vs-effort curve shifts right. The rightward shift is labeled the
  **"deflection point"** — a specific named term for the training-induced shift in lactate
  threshold speed. This term/framing isn't in the existing-coverage list, though it nuances the
  already-covered steady-state/oxygen-debt physiology topic.

### On technique — direct Lydiard quotes and named cues (pp. 47-50)
- **[NOVEL]** Direct quote, *Running to the Top* (1997): "Not everyone is a natural athlete with
  natural coordination... How to run well has to be taught and practiced... you need to know how
  to run properly and how to develop a technique which will direct all your effort into going
  forward comfortably and as economically as possible." A discrete, quotable statement on
  technique not previously captured.
- **[NOVEL]** Named coaching cue, quoted from Lydiard directly (not book-cited): *"To get power,
  you have to avoid running posture we call sitting in a bucket. You have to run tall."* Paired
  with a Lydiard Foundation diagram ("Correct Body Position," prepared by Nobby Hashizume)
  showing over-flexed/over-extended vs. neutral spine posture, and a photo of Kip Keino and
  Vasala at the 1972 Olympic 1500m. "Sitting in a bucket" is a distinct, memorable, previously
  uncaptured coaching cue.
- **[NOVEL]** Quote, *Running to the Top*: "Once the nature of the activity and the fundamentals
  of technique are achieved, we can add the endurance and strength to get the optimal results out
  of the athlete... Correct technique applies to every event because, if you have any kind of
  fault in your technique or in the equipment you are using... you cannot capitalize on your
  fitness, however good that is." Paired with a biomechanical visual labeled **"Thud versus
  Spring"** contrasting heel-strike impact loading against a more elastic forefoot strike —
  another named/coined term not elsewhere captured.

### Shoe history and philosophy (pp. 52-61)
- **[NOVEL]** Historical/anecdotal claim, *Running to the Top*: "In my early days, we ran in
  canvas shoes. They were ideally flexible and we just put some extra rubber on the soles. We
  didn't get plantar fasciitis, we didn't pronate or supinate, we might have lost a bit of skin
  from the rough canvas when we were running marathons but, generally speaking, we didn't have
  foot problems." A specific injury-history claim tied to minimal, flexible footwear —
  not on the existing-coverage list.
- **[NOVEL]** Lydiard's description of the ideal shoe, two sourced quotes: *Running to the Top* —
  "very flexible, little more than a second layer of skin, if that is possible" / "If you could
  just attach a rubber sole to your foot, with nothing on the top, you'd have the perfect running
  shoe." And a **2005 interview** (source distinct from the book, not seen elsewhere in this
  folder) — "We like flexible shoes, to let your foot function. Shoes that let your foot function
  like you're barefoot — they're the shoes for me, as long as they have some rubber underneath to
  alleviate the jarring."
- **[NOVEL]** Misconception addressed, *Running to the Top*: "The point we are making is that
  paying several hundred dollars for the latest in hi-tech running shoes is no guarantee you'll
  avoid any of these injuries and can even guarantee that you will suffer from them in one form
  or the other." Direct skepticism of expensive high-tech shoe marketing, tied to injury risk.
- **[NOVEL]** "Trainers vs. Racers" quote, *Running to the Top*: "As far as I'm concerned you
  train and race in the same shoes and those shoes have got to be more like slipper than the
  hi-tech monsters they're producing these days — light, flexible mid-sole, plenty of resilient
  cushioning under the feet, allowing your foot to function as naturally as possible." Slide adds
  a historical aside: "Old School Tigers were trainers and racers in the early 70's" — a small,
  concrete historical footwear anecdote.
- **[NOVEL]** Misconception addressed re: arch supports, *Running to the Top*: "So we want to
  learn to run faster and we're being fitted with shoes which won't flex to help us to do that.
  They'll flex at the joint of the foot all right but not at the arch, where flexing is a natural
  foot function. Put a board under each foot of an athlete and he couldn't even walk properly,
  let alone run, but that's very much how some of these running shoes affect the arches." A
  specific, quotable analogy (board-under-the-foot) not previously captured.
- **[NOVEL]** Presenter's own addition (not attributed to Lydiard): a "Short Foot Posture
  Exercise" / "Short Foot Manoeuvre" is named and diagrammed as a way to "discover your arch,"
  contracting the foot's intrinsic arch muscles. A named exercise/cue distinct from Lydiard's own
  hill-exercise vocabulary.
- **[NOVEL]** Presenter's own biomechanical framing: "The Human Spring and Its Development and
  Decommissioning Over a Lifetime" — a tendon/spring-loading diagram, with the claim that "muscle
  spasms preload internal pressure, decreasing energy stored and released and decreasing
  protection from the forces of impacts" (implicitly describing chronic tightness/dysfunction
  degrading the foot's elastic-recoil function over a lifetime). This is the presenter's own
  synthesis, not a Lydiard quote, but supports the shoe/technique argument.
- **[NOVEL]** A illustrated life-stage progression used as a teaching device/misconception device:
  "I am born barefoot" → "I run fast barefoot, I am a child" → "I jump and bound high and far" →
  "I run in thin and flexible shoes" → "I run in supportive shoes" → "I run with orthotics and
  supportive shoes" → "I can't run so I walk with orthotics and supportive shoes and a cane" →
  "...and a walker." Each stage shows the foot's arch/spring diagram flattening progressively.
  This is the presenter's rhetorical argument that increasingly supportive footwear over a
  lifetime correlates with progressive loss of natural foot function — not a Lydiard quote, but a
  distinctive, memorable teaching device addressing the "supportive shoes are protective"
  assumption.

### Hill exercises "Lydiard Style" — with new specifics (pp. 69-71)
- **[OVERLAP]** Reinforces the already-covered three hill-exercise types (steep hill running,
  hill bounding, hill springing), but adds concrete prescriptive detail not previously logged:
  - Steep hill running: "slow forward progress with accentuated full leg and foot extension with
    each stride... probably best done alactic (10 seconds or less), 50m or less, uphill." — new
    numbers (10-second/50m alactic guideline).
  - Hill bounding: "long bounding strides like a triple jumper, concentrating on full rear leg
    extension."
  - Hill springing: "very slow forward progress, with most movement coming from the ankle
    flexors... very slow exercise, can be done over a longer time without problems."

### Fat/carbohydrate fuel-substrate physiology (pp. 72-78)
- **[NOVEL]** Specific numeric claim, sourced to "*Distance Running*, TAFNEWS, Dr. David Costill,
  1979" (chart "image courtesy Keith Livingston[e]"): "A well-trained athlete is able to perform
  up to 80% of his total capacity on fats as a source of energy. When he performs between 80-100%
  of his total capacity he is burning up carbohydrates. An untrained athlete cannot tax his
  fat-fuel system above 50% of his total capacity — must resort to carbohydrates sooner." This is
  a specific, sourced number not on the existing-coverage marathon-fueling list (which covers
  honey-vs-carb-loading and salt tablets, not this fat/carb-capacity split).
  Note: though credited to Livingstone as the chart's source, this specific 80%/50% figure and

- **[NOVEL]** Modern research citation added by the presenter (not Lydiard-sourced): "Crossover
  Point ~ Ventilatory Threshold" and "Peak Fat Oxidation" graphs, citing Achten et al., *Med Sci
  Sports Exerc* 34:92-97, 2002 — supporting evidence for the fat-adaptation argument, framed as
  modern science backing Lydiard-era aerobic base training.
- **[THIN/tangential]** Presenter's own personal case-study data (VO2 max test results for "Mark
