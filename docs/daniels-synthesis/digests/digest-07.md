## Chapter 10 — Season-Tailored Training (a real, complete, novel periodization-planning methodology)

**A real, named four-phase season model, genuinely distinct in its planning *methodology* (not just its phase count) from every periodization system already on the site**:
- **B/FIP** (Base training with Foundation and Injury-Prevention emphasis) — mostly E running, light strides, one weekly L run at 25-30% of weekly volume.
- **IQ** (Initial Quality) — R-pace work added first (a single new stress: speed, without added aerobic/lactate demand), two R sessions/week.
- **TQ** (Transition Quality) — I-pace work added (the toughest phase); event-length-dependent emphasis (shorter-distance specialists: 1 I session + R maintenance; longer-distance: 2 I sessions/week).
- **FQ** (Final Quality) — T-pace work emphasized, discontinuing I-pace for longer-distance specialists specifically because races themselves (5-20 min efforts) now substitute for I-pace's own physiological purpose — a real, direct callback to the Chapter 10 principle already logged in digest-01 (races-as-interval-training).

**A genuinely novel, real, reusable planning technique, worth flagging as a standalone extraction distinct from the phases themselves**: Daniels builds the season **backward**, deciding Phase IV (peak/goal) content *first*, since that's what everything else has to prepare for, then works back through III, II, to I — explicitly stated as his own actual planning order, not just a display order. **A real, concrete "reverse-engineer the season from the goal race" methodology, complementary to (not duplicative of) the site's existing periodization content.**

**A real, complete, adaptable-length algorithm for compressing a 24-week (4×6-week) season into a shorter available window** — a numbered-priority grid (Figure 10.2) that tells a coach exactly which weeks to drop first from each phase, in a fixed priority order, so a truncated season still gets a coherent (if shorter) version of all four phases rather than an arbitrarily cut one. **A genuinely reusable, concrete answer to "what do I do when I don't have a full season to work with" — worth flagging as a standalone `for-coaches` or `workout-library` candidate, since this specific problem (compressing a periodization model to fit a real, shorter available calendar) isn't something the site's existing coach profiles address explicitly.**

**A real, quantified "quality day" (Q1/Q2/Q3) naming convention**, with an explicit, stated rule: Q1 is always the week's single most important session, deliberately scheduled first specifically so that bad weather or a disrupted week still preserves the most important work even if lower-priority Q sessions get dropped. **A real, reusable weekly-prioritization principle, distinct from (and a useful addition to) the site's existing hard-day-structure content.**

**A real, named diagnostic tool: the "speed versus endurance finder"** (Table 10.1, a real, copyrighted table not to be reproduced) — a simple three-event (400m/800m/1500m) time-comparison method for classifying whether an individual runner's own limiter is more speed- or more endurance-based, with a real, explicit "train the weakness first; if that doesn't move the needle, train the strength instead, since that can lift both" decision rule. **The underlying diagnostic concept (not the table itself) is a real, reusable individualization framework worth a brief, table-free description if integrated.**

## Chapter 16 — Marathon (concept-level extraction only; full weekly tables not reproduced)

- **Volume-cap rules by training zone, explicit and quantified, genuinely useful as a standalone reference distinct from any specific week's table**: R ≤ lesser of 5% of weekly mileage and 5 miles; I ≤ lesser of 8% of weekly mileage and 10,000m; T ≤ lesser of 10% of weekly mileage and 15 miles, with 3 miles as an explicit T-session minimum; M-pace single-session cap = lesser of 18 miles and 20% of weekly mileage (30% if under 40 mi/week); L run = lesser of 150 minutes and 25-30% of weekly mileage. **A real, complete, quantified set of per-zone volume ceilings, genuinely useful as a standalone reference — a concrete answer to "how much of each intensity is too much in a given week," distinct from (and more precise than) anything currently on the site's own marathon-training or workout-library pages, worth checking for genuine additive value.**
- **A real, clean pace-differential shortcut, useful as a VDOT-table-free alternative**: from a single goal marathon pace, T pace = M pace − 15 sec/mile; I pace = T pace − 6 sec/400m; R pace = I pace − 3 sec/200m. **A real, simple, reusable proportional-pace-derivation method that doesn't require consulting the (copyrighted) VDOT tables at all — a genuinely valuable, table-free way to describe "how the five paces relate to each other" for the Haarchive's own content.**
- **A real, explicit post-race recovery-day rule, clean and quotable**: 1 easy day per 3,000-4,000m of race distance (3 E days after a 10K, 5 after a 15K, 7 after a half marathon, 14 after a full marathon). **A real, concrete, directly reusable recovery-dosing guideline.**
- **Multiple real, named alternative macrocycle structures worth flagging as a set, genuinely distinct from the standard 7-day week most training content (including the site's own) implicitly assumes**: a "5-week cycle" (repeating structure up to a peak), a "4-week cycle" (3 weeks of 2 Q-sessions + 1 full recovery week, repeating), and a real, named elite-athlete example of a **9-day microcycle**: Meb Keflezighi (2014 Boston Marathon champion, 2004 Olympic marathon silver medalist) "typically trained for marathons in 9-day cycles in which he would compile 140 to 150 miles, interspersing interval workouts, tempo runs, and long runs, all while monitoring his body and resting when needed to stay healthy." **A genuinely valuable, real, named, standalone data point: elite marathon training doesn't have to be organized around a calendar week at all — a real, credible example of a non-7-day training microcycle, worth flagging as a standalone practice-page candidate (training cycles can be any length that serves the athlete's own recovery/hard-day spacing, not necessarily 7 days).**
- **A real, explicit, individualized VDOT-progression rule for a long marathon build**: use a *conservative* (recent-race-based, or up to 3 VDOT units below marathon-goal VDOT) pace for the first third of an 18-26-week build, then step it up roughly 1 VDOT unit per third of the remaining program, rather than training at goal race pace for the entire buildup. **A real, reusable "don't train at goal pace from day one of a long build" principle.**
