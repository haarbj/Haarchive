## Chapter 17 — Ultradistance (completing digest from the prior session)

- **A real, honest epistemic admission from Daniels himself, worth preserving as a model of intellectual honesty**: "I have not had the pleasure of coaching runners for ultraraces... to my knowledge there has not been extensive research associated with events of this duration." Rather than guessing or extrapolating his own system past its real evidence base, the entire chapter is structured as a first-person Q&A interview with **Magda Lewy-Boulet**, an athlete Daniels *did* coach in her pre-ultra career (VDOT ~68 across the 1500m, 5000m, and a 2:26 marathon that qualified her for the U.S. Olympic team), now a successful professional ultrarunner in her own right. **A genuinely valuable, standalone coaching-practice principle: explicitly deferring to a credentialed practitioner's own direct experience rather than stretching one's own expertise past its real boundary — worth flagging alongside the existing "12 Laws"/coaching-philosophy content as a real, modeled example of intellectual honesty about the limits of expertise.**
- **Real, quotable, first-person ultra-training content, attributed to Lewy-Boulet's own practice, not Daniels' prescriptive system**: long runs of 2.5-4.5 hours for 50-100 mile races; a longer effort (50K-100K, sometimes an actual race) roughly monthly; deliberate back-to-back long-run weekends about once a month (3-4 hours Saturday, 2-3 hours Sunday) — "training the body to run on fatigued legs." Long runs are deliberately slow: "ultrarunning is very much about spending time on your feet being as comfortable as you can be for a very long time... also about learning (and teaching your body) how to fuel properly over hours of exercise."
- **A real, deliberate night/sleep-deprivation training practice, genuinely novel and worth flagging as a strong, independent corroboration of content already on this site**: for races expected to run 20+ hours, Lewy-Boulet deliberately runs late-evening or overnight, reasoning "your brain behaves differently after being awake for a full day, and it's even good to practice how you will feel mentally at that point... running when your brain is fatigued is perfect simulation of what an athlete will experience in later stages of an ultra." **This directly, independently corroborates the site's existing content on sleep deprivation in multi-day racing (Guillaume Millet's Tor des Géants research, already in `recovery`) — a real, second, practitioner-level voice (not another researcher) making the same point from lived experience rather than a lab study, genuinely additive rather than duplicative.**
- Real, useful content: R-pace work (8-12 x 200m, weekly) and T-pace work (30-60 min total, weekly) are both maintained even during ultra-specific training, explicitly to "keep overall fitness up" and to "feel stronger at slower paces" — a real, counterintuitive point that ultra training isn't pure slow volume, real speed work has a stated ongoing role.
- Real, practical nutrition content, framed as "eating is training": deliberate nutrient timing around sessions, "training the gut" (practicing race-day fueling intake during training specifically to build gut tolerance and reduce GI-distress risk), and full rehearsal of the actual event-day nutrition plan before race day. **Likely substantially overlapping with the site's existing `trail-and-ultra-training` fueling content (Western States exit-survey data on GI distress as a leading DNF cause is already there per the Buffaloes-session grounding audit) — the specific "train the gut" phrasing is a clean, minor, optional addition, not a new claim.**
- Real logistical/cultural color: most major ultras (Western States, UTMB) are entry-limited via lottery or long wait times; most ultrarunners come from a cross country or road-racing background rather than starting in ultras; multi-day stage races (Marathon des Sables, TransRockies) are real but less common formats. **Tier 3/4 color, useful only as historical/cultural context, not a standalone principle.**
- Power-hiking steep climbs is mentioned as a deliberately practiced skill — **this appears to directly duplicate the site's existing `trail-and-ultra-training` "Power-Hike the Climbs" content already logged from the Buffaloes-session grounding audit; treat as confirmatory, not novel, and do not re-add.**

## Chapter 18 — Triathlon

- **Real, personal historical context**: Daniels competed in modern pentathlon (fencing, swimming, equestrian show jumping, pistol shooting, and cross country running combined) at two Olympics and three World Championships, including four years while serving in the U.S. Army, before his primary running-coaching career. A genuine, real, personally-lived multi-sport training background, distinct from his more commonly cited running-only credentials.
- **A real, further, independent instance of the uniform-team-schedule critique already logged from chapter 14 (cross country warm-ups)**, now from Daniels' own lived experience in a different multi-sport context: "All members of the U.S. training team had to follow the same schedule, regardless of individual strengths and weaknesses... It was particularly difficult for the weaker runners to run after 2 hours of riding, 2 hours of fencing, and over an hour of swimming." **A second, independent, personally-experienced instance of the same individualization principle — worth noting as reinforcing, not new.**
- **A real, two-part structural principle for any multi-discipline training, genuinely useful if the site ever develops triathlon-adjacent or brick-workout content, though currently narrow given the site has no triathlon page**: train each discipline both while relatively fresh (to develop that discipline on its own terms) and, deliberately, while already fatigued from a prior discipline (since triathlon's actual run always starts fatigued) — both conditions are needed, not just one.
- Real, named elite-athlete color: Tim O'Donnell (fastest American ever at the Kona Ironman World Championship, 2019) trained roughly 5 hours/day, with 1 rest day/week and a full day off every 3 weeks. Paula Newby-Fraser ("Queen of Kona," 8x Ironman World Champion, 21x Ironman titles) recommends building a training program around "the realities of their daily lives," efficiently and comfortably covering desired weekly volume in each discipline rather than chasing a maximal number.
- Chapter epigraph, real and generalizable beyond triathlon specifically: "Sometimes going a little slower brings you to the finish line faster."
