## Altitude (completing chapter 6)
- **Real, quantified figures**: VO2max drops ~12-16% at altitude on arrival; actual performance drops only ~6-8% (economy improves in thinner air, partially offsetting the aerobic-power loss) — a real, specific number pair, stronger/more precise than the ~13%/~6% figures logged in digest-02's vVO2max section (that was a research-study figure; this is Daniels' own general coaching rule of thumb — both point the same direction, worth citing the coaching rule as the more practical, reusable one).
- Real, explicit "moderate altitude" definition: 1,200-2,500m (~4,000-8,000 ft); most of Daniels' own altitude coaching/research was at 2,130-2,255m (7,000-7,400 ft); explicit claim that problems don't really start until ~3,000 ft, so going from 5,000→7,000 ft is roughly equivalent in added stress to going from sea level→5,000 ft.
- **A real, complete, numbered 13-point practical altitude-training protocol** (condensed): don't reduce overall training volume on arrival at altitude; keep R-pace at normal sea-level speed but allow longer recovery; slow I-pace ~3-4 sec/400 and T-pace ~12-16 sec/mile; use feel/breathing (not pace) for E/L runs; get competitively acclimatized via practice altitude races well before a goal altitude race; treat altitude as "one more type of training stress" that teaches an athlete to tolerate discomfort; allow a real re-adaptation window back at sea level (not urgent same-day competition); altitude fitness gains are **not** transient as long as continued training stress is maintained — persistence, not automatic loss, is the default; "believe in it if you try it, but don't hesitate to abandon it if it doesn't work" for you personally; expect worse performance a few days in (not day one) due to a real dehydration/adaptation dip — hydrate deliberately; intermittent sea-level exposures during an altitude block speed adaptation (a real, cited hormone-marker [adrenaline/noradrenaline via 24h urine] study); doing a hard time trial immediately on arrival, rather than waiting to "feel ready," is recommended — it forces early realistic recalibration and guarantees a second-test improvement (a real psychological benefit); a runner may end up fitter, not just altitude-adapted, if the training stress genuinely was a step up.
- **A real, important methodological/attribution point, directly reinforcing digest-04's altitude-camp-confound flag**: Daniels explicitly argues that a common "post-altitude-camp sea-level PR" story may really reflect *escaping sea-level life stress* (finals, heat/humidity, job demands) rather than altitude itself — a real, second independent instance of this same skeptical point, worth treating as a settled, repeated position in the book, not a one-off aside.
- **A real, historically verifiable, genuinely striking anecdote**: Jim Ryun (Daniels' own long-time athlete/friend) ran a **mile world record (3:51.1) on the evening of the same day he returned to sea level** after 3 weeks at altitude, and a 1500m world record (3:33.1) the following day — offered as real, concrete evidence against the "must wait X days after altitude before racing well" folk rule for short races specifically (with an important caveat: sea-level hyperventilation habit carried over from altitude is a real, transient effect, but doesn't have time to hurt a short race before it's already over).
- **A real, useful, explicit event-length-dependent racing rule**: shorter races (up to 800m) are essentially unaffected by altitude and can be raced at normal sea-level effort; races longer than the mile generally need a longer sea-level re-adjustment window (a week or more) before racing well, especially if sea-level conditions (heat/humidity) differ from the (typically cool/dry) altitude training environment.
- Race-day pacing rule for altitude: go out more conservatively than sea-level habit dictates; real quantified time-adjustment tables exist (Table 6.1: seconds added per race duration/altitude; Table 6.2: temperature's separate, compounding effect on marathon time/fluid loss) — **tables not to be reproduced verbatim in production content, per copyright guidance; the qualitative rules (go out conservative, longer events need more adjustment, temperature and altitude compound) are fine to describe.**

## Chapter 7 — Treadmill Training

- **A real, useful reframing, worth flagging**: treadmill running isn't just a bad-weather fallback — it's presented as a legitimate training tool in its own right, with one real, concrete advantage over outdoor running: **precise, independent control of both pace and grade simultaneously**, letting a coach set an exact physiological demand outdoor terrain/wind can't replicate on demand.
- Several real, human, first-person coaching anecdotes (a Navy sailor limited to ship-deck/treadmill training who still ran 80mi/week; state-prison inmates racing a marathon on a 1-mile prison-yard loop; a law student who ran 80 laps of a 400m track daily for solitude, not fitness, in minimal winter clothing) — **Tier 3/4 color, useful mainly to illustrate that serious training can happen under highly constrained/unusual real-world circumstances, not a standalone principle.**
- **A real, practical technique for simulating downhill running on a treadmill** (wedging a block under the rear support to shift the effective grade range negative) — a genuine, concrete DIY method, worth noting only if downhill-specific treadmill training content is ever built (narrow, optional).
- **A real, explicit taxonomy reframe worth flagging as a clean, reusable structural point**: "there are only two types of training a person can do: steady running and intermittent (work/recovery) running." Any intensity above T pace is essentially always intermittent (I/R), since sustaining above-threshold effort continuously isn't really viable; M and T pace can be run either steady or (per Table 4.2's cruise intervals) intermittently. **A clean, simple binary classification, complementary to (not duplicative of) the five-zone E/M/T/I/R taxonomy — worth a short mention if the workout-purpose taxonomy is integrated, as a useful "there are really only two structural shapes a session takes" framing.**
- A treadmill-specific practical technique: alternating two runners on/off one treadmill for interval-style sessions in a group with only one machine — minor, practical, optional.
