# Digest 02 — Chapter 3 (Physiological and Personal Training Profiles)

## vVO2max — Daniels' own coined, foundational concept

**Daniels explicitly credits himself with originating the term "velocity at VO2max" (vVO2max)**, combining VO2max (aerobic power) and running economy (efficiency) into a single number: the pace at which a runner's VO2 demand equals their VO2max. Explicit claim: "vVO2max is a better way to compare potential running performance than is either economy or VO2 [alone]." **This is the direct conceptual ancestor of VDOT — worth getting the lineage right in any Coaching Library update: VDOT is built on vVO2max, which is itself built on combining two separately-measured variables Daniels found neither one alone reliably predicts performance from.**

**A real, striking, concrete illustrative case (his own tested data), genuinely valuable as a teaching example**: two teammates who "consistently ran similar times in various races" had VO2max values differing by **over 15%**. Naively, the higher-VO2max runner looks like "the better runner" — but both runners' economy curves, extrapolated to their own respective max, converged on nearly the same vVO2max (~325 m/min). **A second, independent version of the same point**, from a different dataset: three elite female runners with materially different VO2max (73, 69, 60 ml/kg/min) had nearly identical vVO2max (328, 329, 331 m/min) and nearly identical real 3000m times (9:04, 9:07, 9:08) — notably, "the runner with the lowest VO2max was a U.S. collegiate national champion... and the one with the highest max was also very successful internationally." **A genuinely strong, standalone physiology teaching point: VO2max alone is a poor predictor of performance between two athletes; running economy can fully compensate for a large VO2max gap, and the two variables have to be considered together (as vVO2max), not separately.** Directly reusable, and a stronger, more concrete version of a point the site's `exercise-physiology` page likely already gestures at — needs a duplication check, but this specific two-teammates/three-women data is probably novel to the site.

**A real, quantified training-progression case**, same runner tested early- and mid-season: max heart rate unchanged (196 both times — i.e., max HR does *not* rise with fitness, a useful point on its own), but VO2max rose ~7% (73→78 ml/kg/min), vVO2max rose ~8% (358→387 m/min), and the pace associated with a fixed 5.0 mmol lactate value rose ~7.5% (330→355 m/min). **A clean, real, three-metric before/after case study of what a season of good training actually moves (VO2max, vVO2max, lactate curve) and what it doesn't (max HR).**

## Altitude: a real, quantified economy/VO2max tradeoff

**A genuinely valuable, concrete data point directly relevant to the site's existing altitude content**: runners tested at sea level and at 2000m altitude (both on track and treadmill) showed VO2max dropping **~13%** at altitude — but running economy *improved* at altitude (less dense air = less air resistance to push through), enough that **vVO2max (and thus actual performance) differed by only ~6%** between sea level and altitude. **A clean, real, two-number version of "altitude costs you less than the VO2max drop alone would suggest, because economy partially compensates" — a genuinely useful complication/refinement of a simpler "altitude reduces VO2max, full stop" framing, worth checking against the site's existing altitude-calculator/altitude-training content for a real, additive contribution.** Also notable: VO2max was identical on track vs. treadmill at both elevations, but economy was meaningfully *better on the treadmill* than on the track at both elevations (no air resistance on a treadmill) — a real, separate, useful nuance for the site's treadmill-training content if any exists.

## Male/female VO2max and economy comparison — a real methodological correction

**A genuinely important methodological point, real and reusable**: naively comparing men's and women's running economy at the *same absolute pace* unfairly penalizes women, because at a shared absolute pace, women are working at a *higher fraction* of their (typically lower) VO2max than men are — so of course they show higher relative oxygen cost. **The correct comparison is at equal % of VO2max, not equal pace** — Daniels walks through a full worked numeric example (a man and woman both near 78%/89.5% of their respective max at a shared 300 m/min pace, corrected via an O2-cost-per-kilometer calculation, showing the two are actually equally economical once the comparison is done fairly). Real conclusion, stated directly: "the men are slightly more economical than the women, but the difference is not much at all" once compared correctly. **A real, useful, correctable-bias teaching point for any Haarchive content on sex differences in physiology — the "women are less economical" claim is shown to be substantially a methodological artifact, not a robust underlying difference, when tested properly.**

## Maximum heart rate — real, concrete evidence against age-based formulas

**A genuinely strong, directly reusable corrective, with real individual data**: age-predicted max HR (220 − age) "can be very misleading for a specific person." Two real, named (by data, not name) case examples: (1) one runner had max HR 148 at age 30 and 146 at age 55 — **virtually unchanged over 25 years**, far higher deviation from the 220-age formula's predicted decline than the formula assumes; (2) a second runner had max HR 186 at 25 and **192 at 50** — actually *increasing* with age, the opposite direction the standard formula predicts. **This directly and substantively complicates any Haarchive HR content that uses or implies 220-minus-age as a reliable individual estimate — worth checking `hr-model.ts` and the HR Threshold Calculator's own methodology and messaging for whether this nuance is already present or would be a genuine, valuable addition/caveat.**

**A real, practical field-test protocol for determining true individual max HR** (no lab needed): run several hard 2-minute uphill efforts, checking HR at the top of each; once an additional rep no longer produces a higher HR reading than the previous one, that plateau is a reliable real max HR. A track-based alternative (a few hard 800m efforts) is offered for runners without hill access. **A concrete, actionable, safe self-testing protocol — a real, standalone practice tip.**

**A related, real caveat about HR-based pace control worth flagging**: heart rate is not a stable proxy for a fixed *pace* across different conditions — heat, wind, hills, or rough footing all raise HR at a given pace (more blood diverted to skin for cooling under heat, e.g.), so training "to a heart rate" under adverse conditions will produce a slower-than-intended pace even though the *intensity* is still correct. Daniels' own explicit framing: decide first whether the workout's purpose is a specific *pace* or a specific *intensity* — if pace matters, don't let HR govern; if intensity matters, HR is a legitimate guide *as long as the runner understands how conditions shift the numbers*.

## Hemoglobin — a real, quantified performance-relevant data point

Normal hemoglobin range: 12-18 g/dL (varies by age/sex); anemia thresholds cited as <13.5 (men) / <12 (women). **A real, concrete performance-cost estimate offered directly**: "the difference between a 12 and 13 in hemoglobin could be about 30 to 40 seconds in a 5K." Explicit caution against artificially elevating hemoglobin beyond normal (increases blood viscosity, straining the heart and potentially *slowing* circulation) — a real, implicit but clear reference to the doping-adjacent risk of artificially boosting hemoglobin (EPO, blood doping) without naming it explicitly. Practical guidance is nutrition-focused (adequate dietary iron) rather than any artificial intervention.

## The Runner Profile — a real, complete, reusable coaching-intake tool

A full, real, reproduced 9-question intake questionnaire (with header fields: name, age, height, weight, gender) Daniels says he uses with every athlete he coaches, including by-mail/email coaching relationships: recent 6-week average weekly volume (miles *and* minutes — both asked, matching Daniels' own time-vs-distance flexibility elsewhere), longest single run in 6 weeks, any recent race results, daily time availability for training, days/week available, facilities/terrain available, specific recent workout detail, planned races in the next 4 months, and the single most important goal race in the next 6-12 months, plus a free-text "current health or injury problems" comment field. **A real, complete, directly-adoptable coaching-practice tool — a genuinely strong, standalone `for-coaches` candidate: a concrete template for what information a coach should systematically gather before writing any training plan, rather than leaving intake ad hoc.**
