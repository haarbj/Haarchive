## Chapter 11 — 800 Meters

- **Two additional named training paces beyond the core E/M/T/I/R five, specific to shorter events**: **FR** ("fast repetitions," roughly 800m-race-effort, defined precisely relative to R pace: 2-3 sec/200m, 4-6 sec/400m, 9-12 sec/600m faster than current R pace) and **Mod** ("moderately long," roughly 20-30 sec/mile faster than E/L pace, used for a medium-effort long run). **H** ("hard") is explicitly just I-pace effort run by feel/terrain rather than a measured pace (e.g., a hard hill circuit). **A real, useful, previously-uncaptured piece of Daniels' own vocabulary — worth flagging as an extension of the core five-pace system for shorter/faster events specifically, not a duplicate of it.**
- **A real, explicit statement that the 800m is now understood to be more aerobic than its \"pure sprint\" reputation suggests**: "it has become more popular to increase endurance training for 800 runners because it was shown that the aerobic contribution of an 800 race is greater than once thought." Framed as "high-speed endurance athletes." **Directly complementary to (not duplicative of, since it's event-specific) the site's existing "Even 400m Racing Is More Aerobic Than It Looks" content in `exercise-physiology`, which covers 400m/800m/1500m aerobic-contribution percentages from a different, older source (Spencer, Gastin & Payne, 1996) — worth a short cross-reference rather than a full new explanation.**
- **Real, named historical/athlete color**: Daniels' own first-person account of watching Peter Snell (1960 Rome), Alberto Juantorena (1976 Montreal), and Joaquim Cruz (1984 LA) race the 800m live, explicitly characterizing Snell and Jim Ryun as more endurance-oriented 800m runners, Juantorena as fundamentally a 400m runner racing up, and Cruz as well-balanced between speed and endurance — **a real, useful illustration that the same event can be won by genuinely different speed/endurance profiles, tying back to Daniels' own book-wide point that athletes should train to their individual strengths (Law 1 of the Twelve Laws, already integrated).**
- Real, concrete resistance-training detail specific to elite 800m runners: "the better 800 runners include a fair amount of resistance training in their overall programs" — Joaquim Cruz did circuit training; Jim Ryun's coach Bob Timmons had him doing real weight training "even in his early training years." **A further, real, concrete data point reinforcing the strength-training core-principle correction already made to the Coaching Library entry.**
- Real, named case: Jarmila Kratochvílová's 800m world record (still standing) at age 32, credited in the book's own photo caption to "farm-girl strength and twice-daily interval training." **Tier 3/4 color; the record's own real-world context (an unbroken, decades-old record, and a training approach — twice-daily intervals — genuinely different from Daniels' own singles-oriented default elsewhere in the book) is worth a brief, careful mention only if 800m-specific content is built out, not a load-bearing extraction.**

## Chapter 12 — 1,500 Meters to 2 Miles

- **A real, precise, quantified intensity-to-distance mapping, genuinely useful and specific**: a hard-raced 1,500m demands an effort roughly **10-12% above** an athlete's own vVO2max; the 3,000m and 2-mile distances are raced right **at** vVO2max — i.e., the same intensity used in demanding I-pace training sessions. **A real, standalone, quotable data point connecting race distance directly to %vVO2max, more precise than anything in the earlier physiology-chapter material — a strong candidate for `5k-training` or `exercise-physiology`.**
- **A real, complete, quantified pacing strategy for the mile/1,500m specifically**: run the first 400m deliberately conservative, the second 400m about 2 seconds faster than the first, which reliably sets up a strong third 400m — "then it's just a matter of hanging on for the final 400." A real, observed cautionary counter-pattern: when a runner goes out too fast, "the split time for the third 400 is often equal to the split for the second 400" — i.e., an early blowup doesn't just cost the third quarter, it flattens the pace into two distinct, slower tiers rather than degrading gradually. **A genuinely strong, standalone, mile/1500m-specific racing-tactics extraction, more precise than the site's existing general "don't go out too fast" content — worth a dedicated addition rather than folding into the already-integrated 5K-level pacing material.**
- **A real, useful team-culture epigraph, worth a short, standalone quotable mention**: "Never miss a chance to tell teammates they are doing well" — opens the chapter as Daniels' own stated principle.
- **A real, useful nuance on R-pace training's psychological role**: "R training is usually less stressful on the body than I training. Middle- and long-distance runners often look forward to spending time with repetition training, seeing it as a break from the demands of intervals." **A real, standalone addition to the R-pace purpose content already logged from chapter 4 (economy-training purpose) — this adds a genuine psychological/recovery dimension (R sessions as a welcome change of pace, not just a training stimulus).**
- **A real, useful, track-season-specific racing-calendar insight**: 400m/800m/1,000m (and even 1,500m/mile) races can be treated as stressing anaerobic/speed capability more than 3K/2-mile races, so a miler can deliberately use a variety of shorter tune-up race distances as training stimulus during outdoor track season — "unlike the cross country season, when every race is about the same in duration." **A real, standalone structural insight specific to how track and cross country racing calendars differ in their own training value — worth a short mention if cross-country/5K-season content is extended.**
- **A real, athlete-preference observation, worth noting only generally (see confidence flag below)**: Daniels states most 1,500m/mile specialists he's known enjoy racing both shorter and longer than their best event, with a slight preference for racing shorter, and that racing the mile makes a 2-mile pace feel comfortable by comparison, and vice versa. **Use only the general finding, not the specific athlete example the book illustrates it with (see confidence flag).**
