# Digest 11 — Chapter 14 tail (season-planning rules), Chapter 15 (15K to 30K), Chapter 16 intro (Marathon, six-approach typology)

## Chapter 14 tail — real, reusable season-compression rules for cross country specifically

- **A real, concrete guideline for a short (10-12 week) high-school-style season**: complete all of Phase I and Phase II during the summer, before the season itself starts, so the actual season can begin in Phase III. **A real, standalone answer to the general "how do I compress a 24-week periodization model" problem already logged (TR-2) — this is the sport-specific version of that same answer for cross country's own real, short-season constraint.**
- **A real, explicit closing summary list, worth noting as a clean, complete season-planning checklist for cross country specifically**: weekly L run (preferably Sunday); most Q sessions on grass/dirt matching actual race terrain; 2 E days before most midseason races; the last Q session before a weekly race should be mostly T-intensity; races provide a similar physiological benefit to I sessions; stop hill work in the final few weeks before important races; set a reasonable goal for every race entered, not just the championship; avoid trying to train harder in the final weeks of a season; start conservatively and work harder as the race progresses; focus on the task at hand.
- Small real detail: uphill R-pace sessions should finish with a few flat R reps afterward specifically so the runner ends the session with the feeling of moving fast again, not just slow uphill grinding — a minor but real, concrete session-design tip.

## Chapter 15 — 15K to 30K

- **A real, named, structurally distinct alternative to the four-phase periodization model, worth flagging on its own**: the "Alien Training Plan" — a repeating 2-week cycle (not a linear phase progression) that simply alternates a set weekly pattern (L/T/R one week, M/T/I the next) as many times as time allows, with a fixed pre-race taper and post-race recovery period layered on top. Daniels' own explicit framing: unlike his phase-based programs, "the alien program does not go through a series of training phases; rather, it presents a series of workouts that are repeated every 2 weeks." **A genuinely useful, standalone structural alternative for a reader who doesn't want or need a full periodized season — worth a brief mention alongside the four-phase model already integrated, as a second, real, named option Daniels himself offers.**
- Chapter epigraph, real and quotable: "Pay more attention to time spent training than to distance covered" — reinforces (not new) the time-vs-distance prescription principle already covered from earlier chapters (altitude, cross country terrain).
- Confirms, without adding new content, that a runner training for 15K-20K races is well served simply by 10K training, and 25K-30K by marathon training — a real, useful practical simplification (no dedicated physiology needed for this middle distance range specifically), but not itself new information beyond what's already captured.

## Chapter 16 — Marathon (introductory material not captured in the earlier, table-focused pass)

- **A genuinely strong, standalone, quotable chapter epigraph — arguably the single cleanest encapsulation in the whole book of the "control the early portion, race the late portion" principle already extracted multiple times (5K/10K's "race starts two-thirds through," the cross country conservative-start case study)**: "Run with your brain the first two-thirds of every race and finish with your heart." **Worth using as a strong, standalone, unifying quote tying together the whole racing-execution thread already built from chapters 13-14, rather than adding yet another separate instance of the same underlying principle.**
- **A real, named typology of six distinct marathon-training program structures** (Novice, 2Q, 4-week cycles, 5-week cycles, Final-18-weeks, Final-12-weeks), each with its own stated rationale for who should choose it (e.g., 4-week cycles for a runner who wants a built-in recovery week every fourth week; 5-week cycles for a runner who wants more M-pace and T-pace work specifically). **The individual weekly tables aren't reproducible, but the typology itself — six real, named, differently-structured approaches to the same goal, each suited to a different runner's real preferences and constraints — is a genuinely useful, standalone concept: there isn't one correct way to structure a marathon buildup, and Daniels offers six real, considered options rather than one default.**
- Confirms the opening statement already implicitly present throughout this synthesis: "each runner needs to have an individualized program... there probably is not a single training program that is best for everyone" — a direct, first-person restatement of the individualization principle, not new content but a clean, quotable version of it.
