## R (Repetition) training

Purpose stated directly: anaerobic power, speed, **and running economy** (not just speed) — R pace work is explicitly framed as an economy-training tool, distinct from I-pace's VO2max-training purpose. Real, concrete coaching principle: recovery must be generous enough that mechanics don't break down — "you don't want to be struggling while running fast... good mechanics will be sacrificed." A real, explicit example of *increasing* rest producing a *better* (not worse) workout: Daniels directly argues 10×400 at a given pace with 3-min recovery can be superior to the same session with only 2-min recovery, since inadequate recovery causes mechanical breakdown that defeats the point.

**A real, concrete team-management caution, worth flagging**: R sessions are explicitly said to be poorly suited to large mixed-ability group training, since faster runners recover faster and are ready to go again before slower teammates — if slower runners are forced to start each rep with the group, they never get adequate recovery and the workout fails its own purpose for them. **A genuinely reusable coaching-practice point, complementary to (not duplicative of) already-logged group-training content**: unlike aerobic/threshold work (where a shared pace target with individualized tiers works fine), R-pace training specifically breaks down in a group format and needs individualized rest, not just individualized pace.

**Real, concrete prescriptive rules**: R-pace work bouts capped at ~2 min each (200m-600m for most runners; 800m only for near-4:00-mile-pace runners); total R volume per session capped at the lesser of 5 miles or 5% of weekly mileage; recovery = 2-3x the work-bout time, or an equal-distance easy jog. A real, concrete example given for why *time*, not distance, should govern group R sessions: a slower runner (90 sec/400) doing 8×400 spends 2 minutes *more* total time+impact than a faster runner (65 sec/400) doing the same 8×400 — Daniels' own suggested fix: give the slower runner fewer reps (6×400) to match total *time* stressed, not total *reps* or *distance*.

## A real, complete, quantified training-load scoring system — genuinely novel, worth flagging as a standalone extraction

**Daniels assigns explicit points-per-minute values to each training zone**, intended as a single comparable "stress" unit across qualitatively different workout types: E = 0.2 pts/min, M = 0.4, T = 0.6, "10K zone" (a named intensity between T and I) = 0.8, I = 1.0, R = 1.5, FR ("fast rep," ~115-120% VDOT, ~800m race pace) = 2.0. Recovery jogs during interval/rep sessions count as 0.2 pts/min (i.e., as E pace). A real, complete weekly/seasonal log template is reproduced, with real, concrete benchmark point totals: beginning HS runners ~50 pts/week, progressing to ~100 pts/week within a year or two; college-level up to ~150 pts/week; post-collegiate elite 200+ pts/week.

**This is a genuinely novel, standalone, directly reusable training-load quantification framework — a real, simple alternative/complement to more complex modern training-load models (TRIMP, TSS), reducing five qualitatively different training purposes to one comparable number. Worth checking the site's existing `workout-library`/`recovery`/`data-and-analytics` content for whether any comparable single-number training-load system already exists before integrating** — if not, this is a strong, standalone `workout-library` or `data-and-analytics` candidate.

## Chapter 5 — VDOT System of Training

**A crucial, precise epistemic distinction, directly addressing the task's own explicit VDOT-research questions**: VDOT is explicitly **not** a literal measured VO2max. It's described by Daniels as a "pseudo VO2max" — a number derived entirely from a real race *performance* (via the economy curve + the race-duration-to-%VO2max curve), not from any lab gas-exchange test. **Direct, real, first-person account of the distinction actually mattering**: Daniels describes real runners contacting him after a VDOT-table value differed from a lab-measured VO2max — his own explicit response is that this is expected and not a problem: the VDOT number reflects that runner's specific, real economy (good or bad) baked into a real performance, whereas a lab VO2max measures only the aerobic-power variable in isolation. "If your lab-measured max is less than we determine, then this means you are more economical than we gave you credit for." **A genuinely important, precise, real distinction worth getting exactly right in any Coaching Library update: VDOT is a performance-derived composite number (economy + aerobic power + the runner's own pacing/mental execution all baked in together), explicitly not a substitute for or equivalent to a literal lab VO2max test.**

**Daniels' own explicit reasoning for why VDOT (derived from a real race) is a *better* predictor than a lab VO2max test alone**: "Race times reflect your max, your economy, your threshold, and your mental approach to racing, all in one measure." **A real, concrete, quotable justification for why the whole system is built on race performance rather than lab testing — directly useful framing for the site's own tools/calculators content on why a recent race result, not a lab number, is the right input for pace prescription.**

**A real, historically verified predictive claim, worth flagging as genuine, checkable evidence of VDOT's real-world track record**: when the VDOT tables were first built, the existing women's marathon world record was associated with a VDOT value considerably below what the women's 1500m/3000m world records implied (both 71+ VDOT at the time) — leading Daniels & Gilbert to explicitly predict in print that "women's times in the marathon will come down below 2:20" — **a prediction the book states has since come true.** Also noted: men's and women's world records across events are "quite close in VDOT values," with men's associated times running ~11% faster than women's at the same VDOT — i.e., VDOT is explicitly framed as a genuinely sex-neutral performance-ability scale (a 70-VDOT woman is a better *performer*, full stop, than a mid-60s-VDOT man).

**A real, stated caveat on cross-distance prediction accuracy, directly answering the task's "appropriate vs. inappropriate use of equivalent-performance tables" question**: VDOT-based time prediction is explicitly *less* reliable the further apart the known and predicted distances are — "performance in a mile is not as good a predictor of a marathon time as would be a half-marathon time." Also: **a time run under poor conditions should not be used to predict a performance under different (better) conditions** — a real, explicit scope-limitation on the whole system, directly relevant to the task's "appropriate vs inappropriate use" question.

**Provenance/copyright note, important for production integration**: the VDOT tables' real published source is *Oxygen Power* (Daniels & Gilbert, 1979) — "81 pages of VDOT tables for more than 40 different distances." **A partial VDOT-value table (VDOT 30-65, mapped to 1500m/mile/3000m/2mile/5000m/10K/15K/half/full marathon times) is reproduced in the book itself as Table 5.1 — per the task's explicit instruction not to reproduce large portions of VDOT tables verbatim, this table (and its extension, likely continuing to higher VDOT values in the pages ahead) must NOT be reproduced in any Haarchive production content.** The concept, the derivation logic, and a small number of individually-cited illustrative values (e.g., "a VDOT of 55.2" as a single worked example) are fine to describe; the full table is not.
