## "Unleash the Lions" (pp. 123-128) — Oct 3, 1998, Rocky Mountain Shootout — the season's first race, in full

Useful for a psychology/pre-competition-routine section as a real, complete, named example, with the athlete's own honest disclaimer preserved (he states directly elsewhere in this same passage, "I can win with 'em or without 'em" — i.e., he does not claim the ritual itself is causally necessary, just personally valuable).**

### The race itself (pp. 124-128)

- **A real, directly-quotable racing-tactics data point on early pace vs. course difficulty**: Goucher runs the course's hardest first mile in 4:46 (subsequently confirmed as *"an astonishingly fast clip considering the first mile is by far the toughest on the course"*), his own post-race assessment: *"that first mile killed me."* Wetmore's own explicit critical assessment, even of a winning, course-record performance, echoing the "100 days to impress me" evaluative caution already logged: *"I got him a little too worked up... He wanted to put on a show. He was way under five minutes at the mile, about ten seconds too fast. He hit two miles in 9:28. He was on the edge for five miles when he was supposed to be in control. But I'm pleased... He might have broken 24 if I hadn't worked him up."* — **A genuinely strong, standalone coaching-evaluation principle: a coach openly critiquing his own pre-race communication (not just the athlete's execution) as a contributing cause of an overly fast, uncontrolled start, even after a course-record win — self-directed accountability for a race-tactics outcome, not only athlete-directed feedback.**
- **A genuinely powerful, real psychology-of-competition/grief story, directly usable for the Haarchive's psychology content**: Chris Severy's father died of cancer five and a half months earlier ("a worse hell than I've ever seen anyone go through," per Severy); during the race, Severy's uncle (standing at the two-mile mark) shouts "Go, Christopher!" — for an instant Severy mistakes it for his late father's voice, is hit by "a torrent of emotions," and has to consciously fight to avoid hyperventilating, regaining composure only by the three-mile mark. Despite this, he finishes 2nd overall (25:11, five seconds faster than the professional open-race winner) — a lifetime-best-adjacent, breakthrough result. Wetmore's own reaction, quoted: *"[Two and a half years ago] outdoors of '96 he ran his last race at the conference meet in May. I'm thrilled with him. I was hoping he would run 25:30 and he ran 25:11."* — **A genuinely strong, real, standalone psychology-of-grief-and-competition case study: an athlete experiencing an acute, involuntary grief-triggered physiological/emotional disruption mid-race (near-hyperventilation) from an incidental external cue unrelated to the competition itself, successfully self-regulating within roughly a mile, and still producing a breakthrough performance — worth using carefully and respectfully (per the same editorial care already flagged for Ponce's story), as a real illustration that grief and elite performance are not mutually exclusive within the same event, and that competition itself can surface unresolved grief unexpectedly, not just before/after competition.**
- **A real, concrete illustration of the coach's own admission of imperfect/limited situational awareness of rival programs, worth noting as an honest complicating data point against any assumption that elite coaches operate with omniscient competitive intelligence**: Wetmore, reflecting on the team's title chances after losing Batliner: *"When we lost Bat, that was a big blow. We'll know where we stand in five days."* — a real, uncertain, evolving competitive assessment, not fixed knowledge.

## "It's in God's Hands" (pp. 134-135) — Oct 7, 1998, pre-Pre-Nationals context + an explicit go-out-slow rationale

- **A real, directly-quotable, explicitly-reasoned articulation of the "go out slow, move from behind" racing strategy already observed in practice multiple times — this is the coach's own stated general theory, not just a specific instance**: *"The team has yet to do any anaerobic work this season, so chances are they will not be running as fast this weekend as they will at season's end. Without any anaerobic work, the runners have to be more careful about getting into oxygen debt earlier than other teams that may have done some anaerobic work in order to be sharper for this race. They will also be running at a faster clip at sea level than they are used to running here, which makes it even more imperative that they go out cautiously. Knowing this, Wetmore always instructs his runners at this point in the season to go out slowly, and attack from behind. Because many runners go out too fast, regardless of their conditioning, it has proven to be a very successful style of racing."* — **A genuinely strong, standalone racing-tactics/periodization-interaction principle: the specific recommendation to "go out slow, attack late" is explicitly grounded here in *where a team is in its own periodization cycle* (still pre-anaerobic-phase, so genuinely less equipped to recover from an early pace excursion into oxygen debt) plus an altitude-to-sea-level pace-perception adjustment — i.e., this isn't a fixed, context-free tactical rule, but one whose rationale changes (and would presumably itself shift) as the team's own anaerobic conditioning develops later in the periodization plan. Directly complements the earlier hill-specific version of this same principle (digest-04's "It's My Last One, Bitch!") by generalizing it beyond hills to flat/sea-level early-season racing, with an explicitly different (periodization-based, not altitude-based) rationale.**
- **A second, real, explicitly-reasoned statement of the "the system is built on faith/patience, evaluated over a full season, not any single early race" theme**, from Tessman, showing genuine internalization of the coaching philosophy by an athlete: *"I think we're gonna win. I really do. We might not this week, but that doesn't matter... [Wetmore's] system is designed to make you run well at a specific time. It is a system built on faith, because you cannot panic if you are not racing as fast as you can during the season. But Wetmore has the results from each cross country season he has been in Boulder to prove that it works. In years past they have always made a significant improvement from Pre-Nationals to NCAA's."* — **A genuinely strong, standalone periodization/psychology-of-patience principle, stated in the athlete's own words: the entire program's competitive strategy explicitly accepts under-performing relative to true fitness at an early-to-mid-season race, justified by a track record (not blind faith) of reliable late-season improvement — directly reinforces and personalizes the "Period A-E" periodization system logic from digest-02 as something athletes themselves understand and articulate, not just the coach.**
- **A real, honest training-logistics mishap, worth flagging as a genuine coaching-error example (the book doesn't smooth this over)**: a scheduled "easy" 70-minute run turns into a 1:52 ordeal due to a real distance-miscalculation by Wetmore plus an unrelated environmental hazard (a nearby prescribed/wildland fire forcing runners to cover their mouths through smoke for half a mile) — three days before a big meet (Pre-Nationals). Goucher is visibly and legitimately angry ("I don't think it's a great fucking idea to run two hours three days before Pre-Nationals," he says angrily); assistant JD's own retrospective take is a genuine, non-defensive mixed verdict: *"They should've known when they got to the place [Mesa Trailhead] in an hour seven. Ah, it'll keep 'em honest this weekend."* — **A genuinely valuable, honest coaching-error case study: even a highly deliberate, carefully individualized program has real, acknowledged logistical mistakes (a mis-measured course compounding into a much longer session than intended immediately before a big race), and the book preserves both the athlete's legitimate frustration and the staff's own imperfect, non-defensive account of what went wrong — useful as a counterweight to any overly idealized portrayal of elite program execution.**
