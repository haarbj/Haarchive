## 1. Executive Research Summary

**What this source contributes uniquely.** *Running with the Buffaloes* is embedded journalism, not a training manual — its value to The Haarchive is not new physiology (the site's Lydiard/Vigil/Daniels/Canova/Pfitzinger/Norwegian System coverage is already deep) but four things nothing else on the site currently provides:

1. A **complete, real, working periodization system in daily operation across a full season**, explicitly self-described by its own coach as "modified Lydiardism" — adapted specifically because a collegiate athlete must peak roughly three times a year rather than Lydiard's original single-peak model. Confirmed via direct read of `docs/lydiard-synthesis/` that this collegiate multi-peak adaptation is never discussed there.
2. An **honest injury ledger sitting alongside the season's successes** — two stress fractures, a season-ending neuroma, a captain's concealed knee injury, and a coach who later, publicly, attributes part of it to his own training-density misjudgment. This is a real counterweight to survivorship-biased program narratives.
3. A **real, second, independent data point in tension with the site's current strength-training stance** — Wetmore explicitly does not prescribe supplemental lifting for his highest-mileage men, on stated grounds that independently corroborate (without duplicating) a tension `docs/lydiard-synthesis/synthesis-map.md` already flagged internally (Lydiard's own skepticism of weight training) but never published.
4. A **documented, real, era-specific male-athlete body-composition pattern** (three independent instances) directly useful to — but currently missing from — the site's existing RED-S content, which per the audit has no comparable case study.

**What it does NOT contribute.** No controlled studies, no new scientific findings, no verifiable physiological measurements beyond qualitative claims. Nothing in this book should ever be cited as evidence on its own — see §7 below for the epistemic discipline this requires.

**Where it overlaps with existing knowledge.** Aerobic-base physiology, hard/easy structure, hill training, mass-start racing tactics, self-talk, goal-setting, and injury/recovery frameworks are all already substantially covered on the site (via Lydiard, Wooden, Beecham, Helmstetter, Fitzgerald, and others) — most of this book's training-philosophy content *reinforces* rather than introduces knowledge. The genuine additions are narrower and more specific than the book's overall page count suggests.

**Most important new knowledge:** the multi-peak periodization system (TR-1), the "100 days to impress me" longitudinal/non-reactive evaluation principle (CO-3, CO-4), the structured crisis-response framework (PS-9, PS-10, CO-9), and the four-factor talent-evaluation rubric (PS-6).

**Most important contradictions/caveats:** the strength-training abstention (§7.1), the body-composition/leanness pattern (§7.2), and injury concealment under competitive/eligibility pressure (§7.5) — see §7 for the full WHAT-HAPPENED / WHAT-SOURCE-CLAIMS / WHAT-HAARCHIVE-SHOULD-SAY breakdown on each.

**Repository audit summary** (full method in `manifest.md`): six live taxonomy categories confirmed as `physiology`, `psychology`, `philosophy`, `practice`, `archive` (Library), `tools` (per `sections.ts`'s own category comment). Coaching Library currently has 7 entries (Lydiard, Daniels, Canova, Vigil, Tom Schwartz, Pfitzinger, Norwegian System) — **no Wetmore entry**. Athlete Library currently has 4 entries (Snell, Kastor, Mosop, Ingebrigtsen) — **no Goucher entry**, and per the Athlete Library's own data-model rule, one can't exist until a Wetmore coach page does. `strength-training` is currently unambiguously pro-lifting with no dissenting callout. `recovery`'s RED-S subsection is thorough on mechanism but has no illustrative case study. `for-coaches` covers goal-communication, captain rotation, and praise/correction asymmetry (all via Wooden) but has no crisis-response, unequal-attention-transparency, or longitudinal-evaluation content.

## 7. Contradictions and Caveats

Each item below is broken into the three columns the task specifically requested.

### 7.1 Strength training

**WHAT HAPPENED:** Wetmore does not prescribe any supplemental strength/lifting work for his male distance runners at 85-95+ mi/week, while the women's team has mandatory lifting.
**WHAT THE SOURCE CLAIMS:** "The men are doing 85 to 95 miles a week. Trying to do circuits would be borrowing from Peter to pay Paul. That'd be taking away energy from their running. Plus, they have a better strength to weight ratio than the women. I leave it up to them if they want to pursue it." (Lear, p. 79)
**WHAT THE HAARCHIVE SHOULD SAY:** The site's `strength-training` section should acknowledge this as a real, documented, volume-specific dissenting view from a successful program — not evidence that strength training is unhelpful for distance runners generally (the book makes no such general claim), and not silently omitted. This should be framed the same way the site already handles the analogous, already-internally-flagged Lydiard tension: a real disagreement preserved, not flattened, with the scope limitation (85-95+ mi/week male athletes specifically) stated explicitly.

### 7.2 Body composition / energy availability

**WHAT HAPPENED:** Three real, separate, documented instances across two named athletes: Goucher's own under-fueling pattern and a coach-directed weight-loss expectation after a "disappointing" result the coach attributed partly to Goucher being "fat" at 145 lb; a coach-assigned 2000-calorie/day diet for Batliner with an instruction to skip meals if he exceeded a target weight; a general coach statement ("Leanness is underrated... skeleton with a condom pulled over your skull") and a direct criticism of Batliner for a 10-15 lb weight gain during injury recovery.
**WHAT THE SOURCE CLAIMS:** The book reports all three matter-of-factly, largely without critical commentary, consistent with its own era's more admiring original tone toward this kind of coaching intensity.
**WHAT THE HAARCHIVE SHOULD SAY:** This must be framed explicitly and only as historical documentation of a harmful, era-specific coaching pattern — never reproduced neutrally, never presented as technique. It should sit alongside, and strengthen, the site's existing RED-S content specifically as a case study illustrating why male distance runners are a genuine risk group (a claim the existing RED-S content already makes, per the audit, but without an illustrative case).

### 7.3 Injury management / racing through injury

**WHAT HAPPENED:** Multiple athletes (both CU runners and rivals) raced on cortisone shots for real, structural injuries (a season-ending neuroma attempt, plantar fasciitis); one athlete raced a roster-securing time trial with a fresh, bleeding wound from a motorcycle crash the night before.
**WHAT THE SOURCE CLAIMS:** Presented largely admiringly, as evidence of toughness/commitment.
**WHAT THE HAARCHIVE SHOULD SAY:** Present as real, documented risk-tolerance decisions made under genuine competitive/eligibility pressure — useful as honest illustration of *why* athletes make these choices (the psychological and career stakes involved), not as endorsed practice. Any recovery/injury content drawing on this should pair it with the site's own existing, more conservative PEACE & LOVE / careful-movement injury guidance as the actual recommended framing.

### 7.4 NSAID use

**WHAT HAPPENED:** Two independent references (Christopher Severy's own account of his freshman year, and a later memorial-service recollection of the same pattern) to training through patellar tendinitis via "twenty Advil a day."
**WHAT THE SOURCE CLAIMS:** Reported within the book's own grief-stricken, memorializing tone as characteristic of the athlete's admired intensity.
**WHAT THE HAARCHIVE SHOULD SAY:** This should be flagged clearly and only as a documented example of a harmful, medically inadvisable self-treatment pattern (chronic high-dose NSAID use to mask training pain) — the fact that it appears within an admiring, grief-stricken narrative context makes the critical reframing more necessary, not less.

### 7.5 Injury concealment

**WHAT HAPPENED:** Team captain Tom Reese concealed a serious knee condition (cracked cartilage atop a previously-repaired cracked femur, real joint swelling/bleeding) from his own coach for an entire season, specifically because it was his final year of eligibility.
**WHAT THE SOURCE CLAIMS:** Presented as a real, serious, athlete-driven risk decision, with the athlete's own detailed self-diagnosis and reasoning preserved directly.
**WHAT THE HAARCHIVE SHOULD SAY:** Useful specifically as a coaching-communication lesson about *why* athletes hide injuries from coaches (fear of losing a final competitive opportunity) — directly relevant to any content about building the kind of coach-athlete trust that makes early, honest disclosure more likely. Not a model to replicate; the book itself does not resolve whether this was the "right" call.

### 7.6 Historical doping culture

**WHAT HAPPENED:** A brief, real, dated (1998) exchange about EPO and the absence of NCAA blood testing at the time.
**WHAT THE SOURCE CLAIMS:** Narrow, real historical color, not a claim about the sport's current state.
**WHAT THE HAARCHIVE SHOULD SAY:** Usable only as narrow, dated historical context (`training-through-the-decades`) — must never be extended into any claim about current testing regimes, which have changed substantially since 1998.
