## 1. Executive summary (chapters 22–23 only)

Davis's own framing (Part V intro) is worth taking seriously on its own terms: he explicitly presents this as a "whirlwind tour, not an exhaustive review," and flags that "much of the material in the coming pages is quite new" — the lactate-shuttle paradigm shift, updated fatigue models, and updated running-economy physiology specifically. That's an accurate self-assessment of chapters 22–23: the content is not Davis's own coaching philosophy dressed up as science (contrast the Daniels/Lydiard/Wetmore syntheses already on the site) — it's a genuine attempt at a plain-language walkthrough of textbook-plus-recent-literature exercise biochemistry, and it holds up well against independent spot-checks (see `source-citations.md`).

**What these two chapters contribute that the Haarchive doesn't already have**: a real biochemical explainer of *how* aerobic energy production actually works (ATP hydrolysis → glycolysis → acetyl-CoA → mitochondrial oxidation), the oxygen-delivery pathway as an anatomical pipeline, the four-fuel model (glucose/glycogen/fat/lactate) unified at acetyl-CoA, the lactate shuttle mechanism, and — most valuably — an explicit, well-hedged correction of the "lactic acid causes fatigue" and "lactate means anaerobic" myths that the site doesn't currently address anywhere.

**What's genuinely new science vs. established textbook material**: the lactate-shuttle framing (lactate as a major aerobic fuel, not a waste product) reflects a real, decades-long shift in exercise physiology associated with George Brooks's work, independently confirmed in this pass (see AE-5/FUEL-6 below). The ATP-hydrolysis mechanism, the RQ-based fat/carb oxygen-efficiency gap, and the liver-vs-muscle glycogen enzyme distinction are all long-established, century-old-or-older biochemistry — none of it should be attributed to Davis as if it were his finding.

**What needs more caution**: two specific quantitative claims (the glycogen-water hydration estimate, and the "~75% of marathon lactate is oxidized by other muscles" figure) are flagged below as needing either a primary-source check or explicit hedging in production language, since Davis's own citations for them weren't visible in the text provided.

Chapter 26 is the strongest single chapter received so far. The Fatmax debunking (not a reliable parameter, no proven training benefit to targeting it specifically) is a genuinely useful corrective against a real piece of popular training culture (heart-rate-based "fat-burning zone" training) that the site doesn't currently address. The running-economy-is-nearly-speed-invariant-below-LT1 finding is a real, non-duplicative empirical addition to the site's existing economy content.

## 7. Chapter 29 extraction — The Four Components of Marathon Fitness

**⚠️ Duplication warning, checked first, before anything else**: this chapter directly overlaps with **existing, real Haarchive content**. `exercise-physiology` (line ~801, per the Phase 0 audit in `manifest.md`) already covers Michael Joyner's 1991 three-variable model (VO2max, running economy, LT2/lactate threshold), the Nike Breaking2 project's real physiological testing of 16 elite marathoners against those exact three variables, and Kipchoge's actual 2:00:25 result falling 25 seconds short of the physiological prediction — all correctly sourced to Hutchinson's *Endure*. **Do not re-explain Joyner or Breaking2 from scratch.** The genuinely new material in this chapter is narrower than the chapter itself: primarily Andy Jones's 2023 "resilience" concept (which explains exactly the gap the existing passage leaves open but never resolves), plus one real empirical nuance about fractional utilization.

### 7.1 Executive summary

The single most valuable thing this chapter contributes is closing a loop the existing Haarchive content already opens: the site currently tells the Kipchoge/Breaking2 story and stops at "he came up 25 seconds short of the physiological prediction," without ever explaining *why* a real athlete would fall short of a prediction built from his own measured physiology. Jones's 2023 resilience paper is the real, independently-verified answer — **confirmed this pass as genuine, primary, currently-citable science** (Jones, "The Fourth Dimension: Physiological Resilience as an Independent Determinant of Endurance Exercise Performance," *Journal of Physiology*, 2023), not merely Davis's synthesis. This should be cited to Jones directly, the same way the existing passage already cites Joyner directly (via Hutchinson) rather than attributing Joyner's model to Davis.

Second most valuable: a real, well-evidenced empirical nuance from Davis's own Figure 29.1 — VO2max alone, running economy alone, and fractional utilization alone are each only modestly (or, for fractional utilization, not at all) correlated with marathon performance, but their *product* (VO2max × fractional utilization ÷ running economy = "speed at SSmax") correlates much more tightly with actual marathon time. This is a stronger, more specific illustration of a point the site's existing VO2max content already makes qualitatively (Prefontaine/Shorter/Clayton; the Radcliffe case study) — worth citing as reinforcement, not a wholesale replacement.

Third: the technical clarification that SSmax is properly a *metabolic power output*, not a speed — treating it (or its proxies LT2/MLSS/critical speed) as a pace "bakes in" running economy, which the four-component model treats as a separate, independent variable. This is a small but genuinely useful scope clarification.

The specific "aspiring vs. elite marathoner" worked numbers (VO2max 56 vs. 73 mL/kg/min, 3:32 vs. 2:06 marathon, etc.) are Davis's own illustrative case-study construction, not real athlete data, and should be labeled as such if used at all — the more valuable version of this same point is the qualitative one (components combine multiplicatively, so modest individual gains compound into large differences), not the specific fabricated numbers.

## 11. Chapter 33 completion — Physiological Roots of Resilience

This completes the full 13-chapter set (22–34). Chapter 33 turns out to be the chapter that most directly explains *why* the marathon-specific phase in the site's already-implemented plan-import feature looks the way it does — and it closes the loop on FC-4 (resilience, from ch. 29) with real physiological mechanisms.

### 11.1 Executive summary

The chapter's central, well-supported argument: of the three components that can deteriorate during a marathon (VO2max, SSmax, running economy), **running economy deterioration is by far the most tractable to study and the most likely actual driver of poor resilience** — SSmax deterioration is honestly flagged as under-researched in runners specifically (only cyclist data exists), and VO2max deterioration data is thin. The evidence for economy deterioration is genuinely strong and two-layered: a real 1991 lab study (multiple test runs at 15/32/42km) found average oxygen cost at marathon pace rose ~5% by 42km, with individual variation from near-zero to ~10% — and a much larger-scale, more recent field study using heart-rate data from **over 80,000 marathoners** found the same pattern (some runners hold steady economy to the finish, others don't, and the ones who hold steady run faster), with Davis appropriately caveating the field data's real limitation (cardiac drift can inflate HR without reflecting real oxygen-cost change) while noting it corroborates the cleaner lab data.

The chapter then gives real, mechanistically specific, independently-verified explanations for *why* economy degrades: localized glycogen depletion at "triad junctions" (a real, specific, published area of muscle physiology research — confirmed this pass) that impairs calcium-based excitation-contraction coupling rather than simply "running out of fuel"; muscle damage (illustrated via the well-known downhill-running research model, which also explains why net-downhill courses like Boston are notoriously hard); and central fatigue that is **muscle-localized, not generalized** — confirmed this pass via a real, specific single-leg-cycling study showing fatigue doesn't cross over to unused muscles, directly extending the site's existing psychobiological/central-governor fatigue content with genuine new mechanistic specificity.

The chapter's practical payoff is clean and well-supported: unusually, all three resilience mechanisms are best trained by the *same* stimulus — high-volume, marathon-pace-specific workouts, ideally through rolling hills — with one added periodization specificity: muscle-damage resistance is transient and must be trained within roughly the final 6–8 weeks before the race. The closing diagnostic point (resilience is the most common missing ingredient for runners who race well at shorter distances but underperform the marathon specifically) is a genuinely useful, well-reasoned coaching heuristic.
