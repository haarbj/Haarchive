## Episode #148: The "What If's" of Training with Brent Stein
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Brent Stein, a coach at Rogue Running (Austin, TX), returning guest (previously on episode 126 discussing his training philosophy). Co-hosted/discussed with Chris (Rogue Running head coach/host). Both are experienced marathoners coaching adult recreational-to-competitive athletes.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core framing: "There's no such thing as a perfect training cycle" — expect the unexpected (missed workouts, bad workouts, vacation, sickness, injury) and plan how to manage/recover rather than avoid it. (PRACTICE)
- Both coaches report their best marathon PRs came off imperfect cycles (one PR'd ~1.5 months after a two-week honeymoon with minimal running). Adversity/imperfection in training may build mental toughness that pays off on race day. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — most sites frame "perfect cycle" as ideal; this is a deliberate reframe of setbacks as beneficial)
- Growth mindset reference: Carol Dweck's framing that failure/pushing to boundaries (regardless of outcome) is a learning opportunity, applied directly to training setbacks. (SCIENCE — researcher named, popular psychology concept, not a running-specific study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if site already covers sports psychology/growth mindset)
- Bad workout protocol: if you start a workout, try to finish some version of it (even slowed/modified) rather than bailing — the mental confidence from finishing outweighs pace precision, except in cases of heat illness, injury signs, or complete form breakdown, where stopping is correct. (PRACTICE)
- "Don't run the whole thing in your head at once" — take workouts/races one rep/segment at a time rather than trying to mentally pre-cover the entire distance. (ANALOGY)
- Never make up missed or bad workouts by repeating them later — doing so adds cumulative load and risks injury; "the body of work matters, not any single workout." (PRACTICE)
- Give yourself permission to be upset about a bad workout for a bounded time (Brent: "2-3 hours to pout") then adopt "short-term memory" — don't dwell, don't let one bad day spiral into skipping subsequent sessions (the "missing begets missing" spiral). (PRACTICE)
- Weekly flexibility rule: workouts/long runs/recovery days can be resequenced within a week (e.g., shifting a missed Wednesday workout to Thursday) as long as the underlying sequence (hard effort → active rest → hard effort) is preserved; but once the window passes, do not compress hard efforts back-to-back. (PRACTICE)
- Named injury-risk claim: "the number one [or top three] reason people get hurt is going from hard effort to long effort (or vice versa) without active rest in between." (SCIENCE — asserted as coaching experience/rule of thumb, no study cited) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific sequencing rule about hard/long-without-rest may not be spelled out on a general site)
- Vacation guidance: 1-2 weeks of reduced/unstructured training will not derail a training cycle; full detraining of the aerobic system takes roughly three weeks of doing nothing aerobic. Use vacation as recovery/recharging rather than a threat to fitness. (SCIENCE — the "3 weeks to detrain" figure stated as fact, no citation given) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific "weeks-to-detrain" heuristic worth fact-checking/adding)
- Sickness rule: hard-and-fast rule is "if you have a fever (99°F+), don't run at all." Below that threshold, use judgment — if you're unsure whether to run, that uncertainty itself is a sign not to. Also: their kids recover from illness faster than they do, hypothesized to be because heavy training volume taxes the immune/recovery system, making illness recovery slower for endurance athletes. (SCIENCE — plausible physiological claim, presented as personal experience/hypothesis, not backed by a cited study) (POSSIBLE GAP — worth checking against actual immunology-of-exercise literature already covered, if any)
- "Go to the doctor" — both coaches admit they/type-A runners resist seeking medical care and encourage listeners to see a doctor promptly rather than try to power through illness. (PRACTICE)
- Missed-time rule of thumb: less than 7 days missed (sickness/injury/life) generally requires no ramp-back modification — just resume the schedule. More than 7 days requires a ramp-back roughly proportional to time missed (miss 1 week → 2-week ramp back; miss 2 weeks → 3-week ramp back). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete ramp-back ratio heuristic)
- Injury pain-scale rule: pain ≤3/10 that doesn't worsen during a run = safe to run (movement = blood flow = healing); pain >3 requires modification. Also a "48-hour rule": if something has bothered you for more than 48 hours without improving, it's no longer normal soreness and needs intervention. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if general injury-management/pain-scale guidance already exists, but the specific 3/10 + 48-hour combo is a fairly precise, actionable heuristic worth checking)
- Warning about orthopedists: Western-trained orthopedists often default to one of three answers (stop running, cortisone shot, or surgery); if you get a black-and-white "stop or operate" answer, seek a second opinion from a PT, chiropractor, or a provider who specifically understands endurance runners. Also caution about "ART" (soft-tissue specialists, referred to as "Erosti" in transcript, likely ART/Active Release Technique) being mismatched for bone-related issues like stress fractures. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — practical guidance on choosing/vetting a sports-medicine provider for runners specifically)
- Best-to-worst cross-training modality ranking for maintaining run fitness: aqua jogging (closest to running motion) > elliptical > bike > swimming — but personal enjoyment/mental engagement with the cross-training modality also matters (a coach used cycling over aqua-jogging because he actually enjoys it, aiding mental recovery through injury). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — cross-training hierarchy is probably already covered, though the "enjoyment matters too" angle may be an addition)
- On resuming after injury: remember that the *entire body*, not just the healed injury site, has detrained and needs to readjust to load — don't just clear the injured part and assume the rest of the body is ready to resume normal volume. Analogy: triathletes/cyclists with huge aerobic engines still can't jump straight into high running volume because impact-tolerance in feet/legs is a separate adaptation. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this "whole-body readjustment" framing distinct from just injury-site healing)
- Distinguishing "getting-used-to-load" pain from "re-injury" pain during return-to-running: if the pain is moving/changing location/intensity, it's likely normal adaptation; if it stays pinpoint and concentrated, that's a red flag. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — useful practical distinction)
- Decision point for pulling out of a goal race: if you've had to significantly modify or stop running altogether for 3-4+ weeks due to injury, that's the point to seriously consider deferring/changing the goal rather than forcing the original race. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #149: Running True-False with James Dodds
**Guest(s) and credentials:** James Dodds, a Rogue Running coach (multiple prior appearances — episodes 132, 136, 139, 141), background includes physical therapy training exposure (references "the Gray Institute's training for physical therapy") and prior running-store/shoe-floor experience. Format is a true/false debate game with host Chris.

**Concepts & claims:**
- True/False #1: "A five-hour marathoner has to train differently than a three-hour marathoner." Host's answer: false in principle — the underlying training principles (volume builds the aerobic engine, hard/easy balance, recovery) are identical regardless of pace; only logistics (mileage vs. time-based prescriptions) differ. James initially argues "true" on the grounds of total time-on-feet/load being much higher for slower runners doing the same mileage, then converges toward agreement. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — the two hosts partially disagree, converging on: same principles, different logistics) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific pushback against "slower runners need fundamentally different programs" is a notable, quotable stance)
- Practical adjustment: recovery/easy days are often prescribed as time-based rather than mileage-based specifically to normalize training stimulus across athletes of different paces (e.g., "30 minutes easy" rather than "4 miles"), while long-run/workout mileage limits still apply to avoid overly long time-on-feet for slower runners, or (for very fast runners) time-based fartlek workouts can overshoot intended mileage. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — nuanced case for when to prescribe by time vs. distance)
- True/False #2: "Running marathons makes you healthier." Host: true, citing stress reduction, better sleep, and psychological benefits, while acknowledging conflicting "science" on inflammatory markers/free radicals from extreme training vs. studies showing no harm — but argues it's true only for people who actually enjoy/are motivated by the pursuit. James: false — marathon training is not the optimal path to "health" (a more balanced regimen of shorter runs, weights, yoga would be more purely healthy); people do marathons for the "epic" adventure/identity, not health per se. (SCIENCE/DISAGREEMENT — explicit disagreement, and note both concede the "is it good for you" science is contested/unsettled, not presented as settled fact) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general "is running healthy" question probably already covered)
- Host reflects on turning 40 and a conversation with an older athlete (50s) who reframed her running goal around longevity ("I want to be running in 30 years") rather than purely performance — leading the host to reframe training for masters athletes around balancing short-term goals against long-term sustainability/longevity. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — masters-athlete-specific longevity framing)
- True/False #3: "Shoes cause injury." Both hosts land near false: imbalance, weakness, and mismanaged training (paces, missed recovery) are the real causes of injury; shoes may subtly contribute but are rarely the root cause. Exception: true biomechanical/foot issues may require corrective footwear/orthotics, and specific case cited — plantar fasciitis can be exacerbated by shoes that are too soft (foot overworks to escape the foam), so switching to a firmer shoe can help healing. Shoe rotation (wearing several different pairs) may reduce injury risk by varying foot-landing patterns. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general "shoes don't cause injury, weakness/training errors do" framing is probably already covered; the specific plantar-fasciitis-and-shoe-softness mechanism and shoe-rotation argument may be a useful specific addition)
- True/False #4: "Focusing on running form improves your health and performance." Both hosts: false, in the sense that consciously trying to change/focus on form while running doesn't work — form improves as a byproduct of increased volume, regular drills, strength/mobility work, and strides, not through direct conscious cueing. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE) — cites a specific study reference: "Alex Hutchinson referenced [in] one of his Sweat Science articles" a study showing runners given form cues to consciously adjust were LESS efficient than runners given no cue. (SCIENCE — a specific researcher/writer and study is named, though not the original study authors) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific "conscious form cueing reduces efficiency" finding, if not already covered, is a concrete, citable, counter-intuitive claim worth flagging for the synthesis pass)
- Warning against "hyperfixating" on achieving theoretically perfect running form, especially for adult recreational runners with years of ingrained compensations (e.g., host's chronic ankle mobility issue from a high school sprain) — trying to overhaul form while maintaining normal training load is described as dangerous; a full form change would require starting mileage over from near-zero. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "you'd have to reset to near-zero volume to safely relearn form" argument is a specific, actionable caution)
- Mention of "Runlab" (a running-form-analysis service Rogue partners with) as a legitimate, iterative (not one-shot-perfect) approach: video analysis → targeted drills/exercises → retest/adjust. (PRACTICE) — presented as a specific service/vendor, not necessarily a generalizable claim.
- True/False #5: "Process goals are better than outcome goals." James: true — process is what's controllable; results are context-dependent and non-linear, so pride/identity should be rooted in process, with outcomes as a "byproduct." Host: false — argues they're equally important and must be integrated (people ultimately want and should celebrate outcomes too); uses NFL analogy (Jason Garrett's Dallas Cowboys as overly process-focused, allegedly to their competitive detriment) to argue pure process-focus can hold you back from adjusting to competitors/outcomes. (DISAGREEMENT — explicit, unresolved disagreement between the two hosts) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — process vs. outcome goals is probably already covered in sports psychology content, though this specific "both matter, don't over-index on either" framing plus the football analogy may add nuance)
- True/False #6: "Great runners make great employees." Both land on false as a blanket statement (talent/results don't guarantee general job skills) but agree that the *discipline* traits developed through consistent, disciplined training (grit, showing up despite discomfort, delayed gratification) plausibly do transfer positively to work performance — distinguishing "talented but complacent" runners from "disciplined" runners. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — transfer of running-discipline-to-career-success is a soft, tangential topic that a training-focused site might not otherwise cover)

## Episode #15: The Importance of Peaking and Training with a Group
**Guest(s) and credentials:** This is an early-run episode co-hosted by Chris and Steve (Sisson) — both are Rogue Running coaches/founders. Steve is portrayed as the more senior/veteran coach (coached at University of Texas for 7 years alongside football staff; extensive personal racing history). This is not a guest interview but the two permanent hosts closing out a 4-part "training principles" series (principles 4 and 5 of 5).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core concept: "peaking" — you can only meaningfully peak 2-3 times per year; training should build toward a small number of "command performances" (one A-race per macro cycle) rather than trying to stay uniformly race-ready year-round. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/peaking is almost certainly already covered generally, but the specific "2-3 times per year max" cap and the "command performance" terminology/framing may be a useful specific addition)
- "Wave" analogy (preferred over the traditional aerobic "pyramid" analogy): the bigger the base of water built into a wave, the farther/messier-but-further it crashes onto shore — used to argue that a big aerobic base creates unpredictable-but-larger peak potential, more accurately capturing the non-linear, messy nature of fitness than a clean pyramid shape. (ANALOGY)
- "Command performance" = the single race in a macro-cycle that all training/other races serve; described as needing to "make your sphincter pucker" — i.e., a goal significant enough to justify total training commitment. Racing too frequently at "full effort" undermines this: named recovery windows after full-effort races — 5K: ~7-10 days recovery; 10K: ~5-10 days; half marathon at full effort: ~14 days — during which key training (anaerobic threshold, aerobic power workouts) gets missed. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — specific recovery-day figures asserted from coaching experience, not attributed to a study) (POSSIBLE GAP — these specific race-effort recovery-day windows by distance are a concrete, checkable heuristic)
- Argument against running many marathons per year even if each is a PR: doing so means never "getting the best out of yourself" in any single one, versus focusing training toward one performance — framed as a deliberate trade-off, not free lunch. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — implicitly pushes back against a common "run frequent marathons for practice/experience" approach)
- Fitness is explicitly framed as non-linear: after a big peak/race, there's a necessary "letdown"/detraining period; failing to recognize this leads athletes to panic that they've "lost it" when workouts feel harder a few weeks post-peak. (SCIENCE — framed as general knowledge, not citation-backed) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if periodization/detraining is already covered)
- Explicit recommendation: after a big "command performance" race, take 2-4+ weeks of real psychological/physical decompression (post-race letdown, "party," rest) before resuming build — failing to do this (chasing goals back-to-back without recovery, described via an anecdote about an athlete named Colin Bell) can lead to burnout/breakdown. (PRACTICE)
- Explicit case example: an athlete who missed a Boston Qualifier by <1 minute was refused permission by her coach to attempt another BQ race just weeks later, on the grounds that both physical and mental recovery from a maximal effort takes real time, and that her long-term potential (much faster than the BQ standard) made chasing an immediate makeover unnecessary. (PRACTICE)
- On fear of commitment/failure: reframes "failure" using the aphorism "failure is success in disguise" (attributed loosely, "I think even George Carlin...") — arguing runners must be willing to fail (i.e., commit fully to one goal, accepting the risk) in order to ever truly succeed; a coach who fears athlete failure "can't clean up a mess that was never made." (PRACTICE/ANALOGY — "shit sandwich vs. ice cream sandwich" analogy for accepting bad outcomes as the price of pursuing big goals) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if fear-of-failure/big-goal-setting is already covered generally)
- Principle 5: Group training is described as close to mandatory for reaching potential — for two reasons: (1) accountability (showing up because others expect you), and (2) "jumping levels" — training alongside faster/more experienced athletes lets people realize psychological/self-imposed limits ("the limits...are really between your ears") faster than solo training or generic plans would allow, because they have living proof/models of what's achievable nearby. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — "group training lets you bypass normal progression by exposure to a model of what's possible" is a specific psychological mechanism claim, distinct from generic "find a training partner" advice)
- Also emphasizes coaching value specifically as removing "blind spots" (an individual cannot see all their own issues, analogized to physically not being able to see behind your own head) and reducing athletes' cognitive/decision load ("it means you don't have to think as much") — plus the idea that trusting a coach fully ("cede power") lets an athlete "run free" without the burden of self-doubt about programming decisions. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- Culture as a distinct third benefit of group training beyond accountability/pushing: a strong training-group culture ("athletes are a reflection of their coach," quoting a University of Texas football locker-room sign) creates community identity and a sense of not wanting to let training partners down, described as one of the "principal aspects of being human." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — team/training-group culture as its own pillar, distinct from accountability, may be underserved on a site focused on physiology/individual training)

## Episode #150: Mental Tips and Tricks for Race Day
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Solo episode — Chris, Rogue Running head coach, speaking from personal coaching experience and his own marathon racing history (multiple marathon PRs referenced).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Structured framework of pre-race mental prep, presented as four sequential steps: (1) identify your specific "purpose" for the goal (the one core reason you want it, distilled from a longer list via reflection or by visualizing crossing the line with/without the goal achieved); (2) "set your intention" via repeated positive self-talk/affirmation (writing the goal statement down, saying it aloud daily, e.g., posted on a mirror) to shift the ratio of negative-to-positive internal dialogue; (3) develop "mantras" in two categories — "rhythm mantras" (a word/phrase/image to stay relaxed and efficient early in a race, e.g., "smile," "smooth like butter") and "fight mantras" (for the late-race suffering phase, tied back to your core purpose, e.g., "every second counts"); (4) full sensory visualization of the entire race from waking up through finish, done in the weeks beforehand. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — visualization and positive self-talk are probably covered in general sports-psych content already, but this specific 4-step sequential framework with the rhythm/fight mantra taxonomy is a structured, reusable synthesis that may be worth adding verbatim)
- During-race tools (four described, all explicitly presented as personally battle-tested by the host across many races): 
  - "Relaxation routine" / moving meditation — a body-part-by-body-part relaxation scan (face → shoulders → arms/hands ["potato chip test" grip tension check] → legs), sometimes done with eyes briefly closed on a straightaway; described as most useful early-to-mid race. Personal anecdote: a marathon PR where the first 8 miles felt unexpectedly hard, resolved via this technique until things "clicked." (PRACTICE/ANALOGY — "potato chip test" for hand tension is a memorable, specific teaching cue) (POSSIBLE GAP — a structured, ordered body-scan routine specific to mid-race execution, as opposed to general pre-sleep relaxation techniques, may not be covered)
  - "Going fishing" — picking a runner ahead (e.g., a bright-colored singlet) as a target to reel in, then repeating with a new target once you pass them; used to dissociate from pain and to sanity-check that you're not slowing down (if you're not passing people, you probably are). Used for as long as 4 miles at the end of a race. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
  - "Counting" — counting to 30 or 60 repeatedly late in a race ("push for 30 seconds, then do it again") as a dissociative, chunked-suffering technique; claims that pushing harder sometimes feels better than continuing to suffer at a slower pace. (PRACTICE)
  - "Knowing your course" / using landmarks — pre-identifying physical landmarks (streetlights, light poles) along the last mile(s) of a specific course (researched in advance via Google Maps) to break the finish into small, countable chunks rather than fixating on the distant finish line; specific example given from a PR race in Houston (counting 12 street lights across downtown). (PRACTICE)
- Pre-race nerves guidance: nerves are reframed as a positive signal ("your goal is big enough") rather than something to eliminate; coping strategies are split into "associative" (channel nervous energy into race-prep activity — studying the course map, reviewing pace plan, visualization) and "dissociative" (deliberately distract yourself — movies, non-running-related socializing, fiction reading). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — the associative/dissociative coping-strategy framework is a recognized sport-psychology concept, though no specific researcher is named here) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if general race-anxiety coping is already covered, though the explicit associative/dissociative taxonomy applied to *nerves* specifically, as distinct from in-race pain dissociation, may be a useful structural addition)
- Personal pre-race sleep-aid tips: a single glass of white wine the night before (calming effect, avoids red-wine headaches) and/or melatonin supplementation to manage pre-race sleep difficulty; also reassurance that poor sleep the night immediately before a race is normal and not performance-limiting — the two nights *before* that (e.g., Thursday/Friday for a Sunday race) matter more. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "sleep 2 nights out matters more than the night before" framing plus the wine/melatonin specifics could be a useful, concrete addition if general sleep-and-performance content doesn't already include this)
