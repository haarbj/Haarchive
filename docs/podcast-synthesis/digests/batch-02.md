## Episode #12: All Things Running Form
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Dr. Kim Davis, DPT (physical therapist), co-founder of RunLab, an Austin sports-medicine clinic focused entirely on runner gait analysis and biomechanics (bills insurance, does full-body gait assessment with slow-motion video). Also an experienced adventure racer. Interviewed by hosts Chris and Steve (Rogue Running coaches).

**Concepts & claims:**
- There is no single "correct" running form — form-prescription systems (Pose Method, Chi Running) wrongly assume everyone should move the same way despite differing structure, strength, and range of motion. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site already covers running economy/form, but the specific critique of Pose/Chi Running is a distinct angle — POSSIBLE GAP)
- Detailed critique of the Pose Method's core research claim: the often-cited Romanov/Noakes 2004 study abstract shows Pose reduces knee power absorption/eccentric work by ~50%, but the same abstract (rarely quoted) shows a compensating *increase* in power absorption/eccentric work at the ankle — load doesn't disappear, it shifts, per F=ma (mass constant, so force can't just vanish). This is why Pose practitioners commonly develop Achilles/ankle problems. Pose's own marketing selectively cites only the knee-reduction half of the finding. (SCIENCE — study and researchers named, host actually pulled and read the abstract) (POSSIBLE GAP — a case study in critically reading cited "research" behind popular running-form systems)
- A Japanese study of 200 elite runners found ~75% were heel strikers, a smaller share midfoot, and an even smaller share forefoot — undercutting the "midfoot strike is optimal" claim. (SCIENCE — study named but not fully cited/sourced) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Rear-foot (heel) striking is not inherently inefficient; the problem is *overstriding* (foot too far ahead of body, straight/extended knee at landing), not heel contact per se. The calcaneus (heel bone) is built to absorb large shock loads. Landing further back on the foot shifts loading toward the ankle/Achilles; landing more forward shifts it toward the knee — runners need to find the "happy medium" they can tolerate. (SCIENCE, physiologically grounded but not citation-backed) (POSSIBLE GAP — nuanced foot-strike guidance beyond "heel strike is bad")
- Form cannot be meaningfully changed by consciously thinking about it during long runs — running mechanics are largely subconscious/automatic, and conscious cueing for extended periods is energetically inefficient and unsustainable. "Fixing it in your mind is worse than just doing what you're doing" in the short term. (PRACTICE)
- Analogy: Tiger Woods rebuilding his golf swing multiple times never fully recovered because ingraining new motor patterns requires thousands of repetitions; elite runners like Galen Rupp show minimal mechanical change across race distances (1500m to marathon) — just stride length/power differences, not a different "gait" per event. (ANALOGY)
- "The best time to work on your run is while you're not running" — form work should happen via drills, strength work, and barefoot practice, not by consciously altering stride mid-run. Analogy: repeatedly walking a different path through a grassy field never wears a groove (neural pathway); you must repeat the *same* movement pattern deliberately, off the run, to build automaticity. (ANALOGY/PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Pure strength training (squats, core work) doesn't automatically improve running form/efficiency — strength must be trained in the functional context of the movement pattern itself, or it won't transfer. Analogy: "if everybody that was strong was a good runner, Crossfitters would be amazing" — but they don't win marathons because raw strength doesn't equal functional running strength. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if site covers strength training for runners, but this specific "strength doesn't transfer without functional/gait training" framing may be a gap — POSSIBLE GAP)
- The "180 steps per minute" cadence myth traces to Jack Daniels' observation of elite 5,000m runners at the 1984 Olympics running above 180 spm — a single data point from elite racing pace that got misapplied as a universal prescriptive target by shoe companies (Newton), wearables, and coaches. Cadence is a useful *cue/tool* (e.g., increasing cadence can trick the body into reducing overstride) but is not itself the mechanism — it's a proxy for foot-landing position, and optimal cadence varies hugely by leg length/build (one athlete cited at 200+ spm as normal for her). (SCIENCE — source named/critiqued) (DISAGREEMENT with popular cadence dogma) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Common RunLab clinic findings and their real causes: (1) Knee pain — usually from overstriding/straight-leg landing (quads can't eccentrically absorb shock) or weak hips causing pelvic drop/valgus stress; beginners get it more because they lack strength "load capacity," and mileage above ~40 miles/week is a common tipping point where mechanics issues surface if strength work is neglected. "It's not running that's bad for your knees, it's bad running that's bad for your knees." (PRACTICE/SCIENCE mix) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC generally, but the 40mpw tipping-point observation is a specific practical detail — POSSIBLE GAP)
- (2) Hip drop / weak glute medius — often masked by compensation patterns that hide on fresh legs but appear late in long runs (e.g., after 18 miles); runners commonly overestimate their own hip stability because compensations disguise weakness until fatigue strips them away. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- (3) Crossover step (feet crossing the midline) — linked to the same weak stabilizing muscles as hip drop; there's a normal sex-based biomechanical explanation (men tend toward genu varus/"cowboy legs" causing later crossover, women toward genu valgus/"knock knees" causing earlier crossover, plus narrower male pelvises) — so crossover onset speed differs by sex, and problematic crossover can contribute to IT band syndrome from the added lateral stretch. (SCIENCE, presented via clinical experience, not citation) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- (4) "Tight hamstrings" are frequently a false diagnosis — most runners don't need 90-degree hip flexion (only hurdlers do), and static flexibility testing often shows normal range even in runners who complain of tightness. The felt "tightness" is often actually an anterior pelvic tilt caused by tight hip flexors/quads (from sitting) pulling the pelvis forward and creating functional tension on the hamstring — the real problem is the front of the body, not the hamstring. Also distinguishes "static range of motion" (touching toes) from "dynamic range of motion" (leg swinging to 90° while running) — elite milers can't touch their kneecaps yet run with full dynamic hip range. Distance runners rarely truly strain/pull a hamstring (unlike sprinters) because marathon running doesn't require large ROM. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — distinction between static and dynamic flexibility is a genuinely useful, possibly missing framework)
- Nerve-based "hamstring" pain: sciatic nerve tension from anterior pelvic tilt can masquerade as a hamstring strain/tear — "nerves hate to be stretched" and will fire pain signals (sciatica-type symptoms) that feel muscular but originate from the hip/piriformis/low back. General injury principle stated: "where the pain is, is probably not the problem — that's just the weak link in the chain." (PRACTICE/SCIENCE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Five "no-brainer" ways to improve form: (1) professional gait analysis; (2) run more, with varied terrain/elevation — trail running builds lateral stability and shortens stride beneficially, hills temporarily force excellent mechanics (high knee lift, full glute extension) that can then transfer to flat running, "hills are speed work in disguise"; (3) weekly strides, ideally barefoot on soft surface when possible, using cues like "sneak up like an Indian" (quiet feet) and "push off the ground" (not butt-kicking); (4) running drills (high knees, skipping, bounding) that break the stride into components for motor learning — awkwardness during drills signals inefficiency that's also present, less visibly, in the full running stride; (5) dynamic single-leg balance/stability work over generic strength work — "running is really just balance over time with speed," and single-leg squat tests quickly reveal instability that shifts load onto the knee. Highly cushioned/heavy shoes can worsen poor loading mechanics rather than help knee pain. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly, but the specific "hills = disguised speed work" and single-leg-squat-as-diagnostic framing are more specific — mixed)

## Episode #13: Creating a Vision and Goals for Your Running
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris and Steve (Rogue Running coaches), continuing a "mental training" series (episodes 6 and 11 preceded this one).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Third installment of a mental-training framework: motivation/feeling (ep. 6) → purpose/statement of why (ep. 11) → vision (ep. 13, "how you see it") → goal-setting (also covered here). (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if site covers sports psychology generally, but this specific staged framework — feel/say/see/do — may be a useful structural gap-filler) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Vision board" exercise: create a physical/digital collage (poster board, cork board, or Pinterest) representing "the runner you want to be," using cut-out images, handwritten statement-of-purpose excerpts, heroes/heroines (or even villains/rivals as negative motivators), and non-literal/mythological imagery. Process guidance: gather more raw material than needed, work in a distraction-free "set and setting," stay flexible before committing (pin before gluing), and place the finished piece somewhere it will be seen regularly (or photograph it for a phone background). (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — vision boards specifically, as opposed to general visualization, may not be covered)
- Historical framing for vision-board-style visualization: cave paintings (~32,000-year-old French cave art depicting successful hunts, believed to be created as part of the process of achieving a hunt, not just a record of one), Egyptian hieroglyphics, and grimoires are cited as long-standing precedents for "seeing" a desired outcome before achieving it. (ANALOGY — asserted, not evidence-backed)
- Not all motivational material needs to be positive — Matt Fitzgerald's book "How Bad Do You Want It" is cited for the idea that some elite athletes are driven by anger (being underestimated, past slights) as much as by aspiration; vision boards/goal material can include "villains" or negative motivators. (PRACTICE, citing a named author/book) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Kill Plan B, or it will kill you" — goal-setting philosophy drawn from Stan Beecham's book "Elite Minds": describes a sales-training exercise where a group iteratively rewrites their goal to have decreasing certainty of success (100% → 90% → 80% → 70% → 60%), with the point being that a goal you're 100% sure to hit isn't a real goal ("no better than a pep rally") — a worthwhile long-term goal should carry real risk of failure and provoke nervous/adrenaline energy. A Plan B is framed as "not a safety net, it's a noose" — hedging bets is preparing to fail. (PRACTICE, citing named author/book) (POSSIBLE GAP — good candidate for a "goal-setting" section if not covered)
- Process-oriented vs. outcome-oriented goals: cites Hank Aaron reportedly not setting a numeric home-run target because too many variables (weather, ballparks, pitchers) were out of his control — instead he set process goals (hours of batting practice, strength/flexibility work) that indirectly produced the outcome. Host applies this to a runner client who, when coached to focus on process rather than a time goal, ended up far exceeding his own expectations (2:00 half-marathoner improving to sub-1:30) — outcome goals can artificially cap potential when true fitness ceiling is unknown. (ANALOGY/PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if general sports psychology is covered, but the Hank Aaron framing specifically may be new)
- Coach Bev Kearney (University of Texas) analogy: a coach/training process can typically get an athlete to about 90% of their capability through physical/mental/spiritual preparation; the remaining 10% gap to 100% is "the magic" — but that magic only appears if the athlete trained to the full 90%, not 85%. Framed as reconciling "no Plan B" with the reality that outcomes still involve some luck/uncertainty on race day. (ANALOGY)
- Personal host anecdote as a cautionary tale: articulated a long-term marathon goal (2:40) 15 years prior but let life circumstances (work, kids, other business) become an "excuse" to quietly abandon Plan-A-level commitment to it, instead accepting a series of softer Plan-B outcomes; co-host argues the athlete never did the purpose/vision-board work to anchor the goal, so when race-day stress hit there was no psychological "anchor" to return to. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — illustrates internal tension in the framework: goals need both belief and Plan-A commitment) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general goal-setting)

## Episode #126: Core Training Principles with Brent Stein
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Brent Stein — longtime (8-year) Rogue Running coach, coach of the "Killer Bs" training group, newly appointed Rogue Program/Curriculum Director; himself a sub-2:50 marathoner (46 years old) who returned from a serious ankle surgery (broken bone, damaged tendon/ligament, 2015) via a 3-year comeback. Formerly 23 years in corporate tobacco-industry sales/marketing before transitioning to coaching full-time.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Coaching tenet 1 — Consistency and patience: fitness cannot be "microwaved"; coaches must set realistic athlete expectations (especially for new marathoners — advises no time goal at all for a first marathon, just "finish, smile, want to do another") and use interim races/time trials to maintain motivation during a long buildup. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general training philosophy)
- Illustrative case: athlete "Mike" was stuck around 3:03–3:10 marathon for years despite fitness gains, until coach and athlete reviewed years of training logs together and identified that reducing recovery time *between* training cycles (rather than just changing one cycle) was the missing piece — he then ran an 8–9 minute PR and BQ'd. Point: cumulative multi-year "body of work," not just the most recent cycle, produces breakthroughs. (PRACTICE)
- Host's personal data point: took 10 years (2004–2014) between marathon PRs due to grad school, kids, career, and a string of bad-luck race days (weather, illness) — argued that runners should think about their progression on a "decade-wide," not single-cycle, timescale. (PRACTICE)
- Coach quote (attributed to "another coach," via Twitter): "I would rather give my athletes to a start line undercooked and healthy than overcooked and on the edge." Host's CIM buildup exemplified this — dropped weekly mileage from ~70 to ~55 mi/wk specifically to arrive at the start line "essentially 100% healthy" for the first time, and it produced a PR. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with high-mileage-is-always-better instinct) (POSSIBLE GAP — the explicit "undercooked and healthy vs. overcooked and injured" framing as a stated tradeoff)
- Coaching tenet 2 — Understand the athlete as an individual/whole person ("total stress," attributed to sports scientist Jason Brooks, discussed elsewhere in the podcast's "Human Performance Project" series): coaches should track non-running life stressors (career, family) alongside training load, ask "who's got stuff going on this week?" before hard workouts (attributed to coach John Shrupp), and hold structured 1:1s (goal-setting early in a cycle, pre-race check-in) supplemented by informal ongoing communication (Strava monitoring, texts). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if site covers total-stress/life-load concepts, but the "total stress" framing specifically may be a gap) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Case example: athlete "Stacey," a teacher, repeatedly got injured every August/September despite being highly consistent in her running — root cause identified only after tracking her non-running step count: back-to-school prep had her at 10,000+ steps/day on her feet before her run even started, and her training load wasn't adjusted for that occupational stress. Once mileage was adjusted around the school calendar, she broke through and got a BQ. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — occupational/on-your-feet stress load as an underrated injury factor)
- Coaching tenet 3 — "Miles matter, but there are limits": goal is to prescribe the *minimal* mileage/workload needed to achieve an athlete's specific goal, not maximum tolerable mileage — echoing another Rogue coach's line, "if you can run one mile a week and get your goal, that's what you should do." Explicit pushback on a commonly cited rule that "long runs should be 20–30% of weekly mileage," calling it an arbitrary number lifted from elite 100+ mile/week training that doesn't scale down proportionally to recreational mileage. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT with the 20-30% long-run-percentage rule)
- Case example: athlete "Kate Barrett" ran an Olympic Trials Qualifier marathon (2:43) off only ~55 miles/week — a volume that, viewed without context, would look inadequate for that goal — because her plan was individually weighted toward long-run experience (she lacked 20+ mile run history) rather than generic high mileage. Illustrates that total volume matters less than whether the specific miles address the athlete's specific gaps. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — good counter-example to "more miles = better" thinking)
- Cautionary counter-example ("Sally," anonymized): a rapidly improving athlete who kept moving up pace groups and hit a BQ, but sustained an injury from cumulative volume that never fully healed before Boston, effectively ending her involvement with the team — presented as a "too much too soon" cautionary tale contrasted with a patient athlete (Wesley Wheeler, ex-competitive swimmer, bonked to a 3:10 debut marathon on no training) who built mileage slowly (~55 mi/wk) and kept PR'ing (2:50s) without injury. (PRACTICE)
- Coaching tenet 4 — Recovery is king: cites coach Phil Wharton (via a Jay Johnson podcast) that if he could give one universal piece of advice to get faster, it would be "rest more" — framed as the physiological reality that adaptation/fitness gains happen during rest after a training stimulus, not during the training itself. (PRACTICE, citing named coach) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery)
- References Christy Ashwanden's book "Good to Go" (discussed elsewhere on the podcast) as having found limited scientific validity for most recovery modalities (massage, compression, etc.) beyond sleep — but argues subjective/placebo benefit may still matter, and recovery is highly individual so experimentation is warranted. Non-negotiables per the hosts: sleep, easy/active-recovery running (can't run fast every day), and a reasonably balanced (not perfect) diet. Everything else (massage, float pods, contrast baths, infrared sauna, foam rolling) is optional/personal experimentation. (SCIENCE, citing a named book/author) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery, though the "non-negotiable vs. experimental" framing is a useful structure)
- Specific recovery practices discussed: post-marathon recovery period (2–4 weeks) should be treated as sacrosanct — case example of athlete "Emily" who took a full month of totally unstructured, no-workout running during a 2-month post-marathon vacation and came back to a ~20-minute PR in her next race, illustrating that a real recovery block doesn't cost fitness long-term. Elite masters runner Bernard Lagat's annual 5-week off-season break is cited as a professional-level example of the same principle. Napping discussed as a recovery tool (including napping in a parked car during a kid's soccer practice) and meditation/"quiet mind" time (via apps like Headspace/Calm, or simply sitting without a phone) as an underused recovery practice. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly)

## Episode #130: First Marathons with Emily Sisson
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Emily Sisson — elite American professional distance runner (training partner of Molly Huddle under coach Ray Tracy), 2x NCAA champion (Providence College), 2017 World Championships team member, at time of interview coming off a marathon debut of 2:23 in London (2nd-fastest American debut ever) and a series of American-competitive results across 5K to marathon in the prior ~10 months.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Marathon-specific training adaptation from 10K/track training: reduced track work from twice weekly to once every 7–12 days, increased weekly mileage from ~90 to ~110–115 mi/wk (average, since her training used a 10–12 day "microcycle" rather than a 7-day week, with a scheduled ~5-6 mile "easy day" every cycle that resets/recharges). She felt *better*, not worse, doing less track work and more volume/tempo work, attributing it partly to being naturally stronger at tempo-style running. (PRACTICE, single elite athlete's experience, not generalizable science) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/specificity, though the 10-12 day microcycle structure (vs. standard 7-day week) is a specific structural detail) (POSSIBLE GAP)

Coach's rationale, per athlete: total time-on-feet during a 22-mile long run was nearly equivalent to the marathon race itself, so distance-matching wasn't necessary — what mattered was time on feet, not literal mileage matching. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — coach's stated rationale, not independently verified) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — long run periodization, but the specific "time on feet vs. exact race distance" framing is a nice explicit articulation) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Recovery day" structure: either a medium-long run (14–17 miles) or a double (e.g., 10+4, 10+5 miles) rather than a full rest day; she never took a complete off day during the London buildup. (PRACTICE)
- Strength training: added a twice-weekly (roughly) strength routine starting 2017–2018, mostly bodyweight plus some kettlebell work (squats, single-leg RDLs). Explicitly frames the goal as *not* directly "getting faster" but working on weaknesses/durability — "if you break down less, that's what will help [you get faster]," i.e., strength training's value is injury-resilience, not direct performance enhancement. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race mental strategy generally, but the specific water-bottle-chunking technique is a good concrete example)

- On marathon unpredictability: describes the marathon as her least favorite event structurally because of the training-to-race ratio — you train ~3 months and forgo other racing opportunities, then "anything could happen" (illness, bad food, extreme weather) on a single day, unlike shorter races you can simply repeat soon after a bad one. Cites Molly Huddle's brutal-weather 2018 Boston Marathon as an example of conditions "no one's ever seen before" that no amount of preparation could offset. (PRACTICE/opinion) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general marathon-specific psychology)

(PRACTICE, single-athlete anecdote)
- General consistency/longevity philosophy from her coach: prioritizing a "long, conservative approach" over chasing short-term results, explicitly for career longevity — described as the throughline of what she's learned from her coach across college and pro years. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- On enjoyment as a durability factor: attributes long-term consistency partly to deliberately "keeping it fun" — taking short breaks (even just running every other day for a week) to preserve motivation/enjoyment rather than pushing through burnout. ## Episode #131: Performance Engineering with Sasha Gollish
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Sasha Gollish — Canadian elite marathoner (2:32 PB, Olympic hopeful at time of recording), holds a PhD, self-described civil engineer applying an engineering/systems mindset to translate sports-science research into practical tools ("performance engineering"). Recently changed coaches (from Brad Hudson to a Toronto masters track-and-field program) and discussed working directly with a sleep-science expert (Amy Bender).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Sleep should be tracked and budgeted *weekly*, not just nightly: target ~56 hours/week (8 hrs/night × 7) as an aggregate goal rather than a strict nightly minimum — a short night (e.g., 5 hours) can be compensated later in the week via naps or extra sleep, similar to how missing one training session isn't catastrophic if the week's total volume is maintained. Marathon-training-volume athletes may need more like 70–75 hours/week. (SCIENCE — presented as expert/sleep-scientist guidance, source named (Amy Bender) but not an independently cited study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "weekly sleep budget" framing, as opposed to nightly-hours advice, may be missing from standard site content)
- Naps count toward total sleep at roughly a 1:1 ratio and are not simply "worse" than nighttime sleep for recovery purposes — even a short (~20-30 min) nap where the person doesn't fully fall asleep still provides recovery benefit by lowering heart rate/reducing stress hormone load, distinct from a full ~90-minute nap that completes a sleep cycle. (SCIENCE, attributed to a sleep expert but no study cited) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Physiological rationale for sleep given: deep/REM sleep clears "brain toxicity" (metabolic waste); insufficient sleep raises cortisol, reduces stress-coping capacity, and impairs memory consolidation/deep learning of skills or work done the prior day (framed as applicable to both athletic and cognitive/professional performance). Claim that stress is "the number four killer" in the US is asserted without a specific citation. (SCIENCE — mechanism asserted, partially unsourced) (POSSIBLE GAP — brain-toxin-clearance/REM framing)
- Circadian rhythm can be shifted gradually with age/practice (unlike teenagers, who are said to have the least flexibility) — recommends shifting bedtime by only 10–15 minutes at a time over multiple weeks rather than attempting an abrupt change ("you can't microwave sleep"). Practical tips: reduce blue light exposure before bed (blue light — from screens, sun, and household lighting generally — suppresses melatonin secretion; blue-light-blocking glasses suggested as a workaround if screen time can't be cut), stop caffeine intake by roughly midday (caffeine and melatonin secretion work in opposition via the adenosine pathway), avoid using alcohol as a sleep aid (disrupts sleep architecture despite subjective drowsiness), keep the room cool and fully dark (cover small LED lights on electronics). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE mix) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if general sleep hygiene is covered — but the specific "adenosine/melatonin" framing and circadian-shift pacing advice may add detail) (POSSIBLE GAP for the gradual-shift protocol specifically)
- Recommends tracking sleep like training: journaling bedtime, wake time, and activity in the hour before/after sleep to identify patterns and opportunities (a "sleep log" analogous to a training log). Wearables (Apple Watch + "AutoSleep" app, or Garmin) can estimate sleep depth via heart rate and movement, though acknowledged as imperfect without EEG. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general self-tracking, minor gap for sleep-specific tracking)
- Nutrition framed as highly individualized ("science experiment of one") — no universal "winning" diet; emphasis on identifying personal food sensitivities (not just allergies) since GI distress during running is common and often diet-specific. Discusses her own iron supplementation as a female athlete with a regular menstrual cycle whose ferritin needs exceed what's practical from red meat alone. Recommends short-term (2-3 day), not ongoing, food tracking via an app (cited: "My Fitness Pal," referred to on-air as an Under Armour app) as a diagnostic checkpoint rather than a permanent habit — 4 months of continuous tracking was "too much." Notes that non-dairy milks (e.g., almond milk) are often mistakenly assumed to be protein-equivalent to dairy milk and are not — a specific, checkable nutrition-label literacy point. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly — individualized fueling, though the almond-milk-protein misconception is a nice specific, fact-checkable detail) (POSSIBLE GAP for that detail)
- Goal-setting should not happen in life "silos" (running goals separate from career/family goals) — races should be chosen with awareness of surrounding life context (e.g., she avoids racing marathons in January because the holiday season, including her birthday and Christmas, undermines the buildup; recommends against scheduling key training blocks during back-to-school, spring break, or other known high-stress life windows). Goals should be actively communicated to partners/family rather than kept as "secrets," since uncommunicated goals create friction and unaddressed uncertainty, though oversharing (not all goals need to be public) is also cautioned against. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if general goal-setting is covered, but "goals-in-context-of-life-silos" and partner-communication-of-goals are specific angles) (POSSIBLE GAP)

Race goal reframed as: "I'm never going to give up" as the unifying standard regardless of A/B/C framing. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT with the common practice of nested A/B/C outcome goals) (POSSIBLE GAP)

- Habit-building/behavior-change approach: don't try to fix everything at once (echoes "you can't microwave training" applied to lifestyle habits); do a self-assessment exercise (10 minutes/day for a week journaling strengths/weaknesses, then identify the most-repeated weakness) and commit to changing just one small habit for three weeks (e.g., "go to bed 15 minutes earlier on short-sleep nights") rather than overhauling sleep, nutrition, and recovery simultaneously. ## Episode #132: Missing Goals — Let's Talk About Why with James Dodds
