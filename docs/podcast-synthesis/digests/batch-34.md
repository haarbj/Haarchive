## Episode #45: Performance Nutrition (+ Sleep)
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Megan Lyons, founder of Lyons' Share Wellness (Dallas). Self-described health coach (unregulated field, distinct from a licensed nutritionist/therapist), former strategy consultant turned nutrition/wellness coach since 2014. Not a doctor, dietitian, or exercise physiologist — background is personal/coaching experience plus client work, not clinical training.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core nutrition philosophy is a Michael Pollan quote: "Eat food, not too much, mostly plants," broken into four principles: more vegetables, more water, less added sugar, macronutrient balance. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling)
- Claims ~71-72% of packaged grocery items contain added sugar, and 75% of Americans are chronically dehydrated. (SCIENCE — asserted stat, no study/researcher cited) (POSSIBLE GAP if not covered — dehydration/added-sugar prevalence stats)
- Hunger/satiety mechanism: ghrelin signals hunger, leptin signals satiety; nutrient-poor processed food fails to raise leptin, so the brain keeps signaling hunger even when calories are sufficient — vegetables/micronutrients help raise leptin and reduce cravings. (SCIENCE — named hormones but no specific citation) (POSSIBLE GAP — practical hunger-hormone framing tied to running nutrition)
- Practical portion-sizing system using body parts: one palm-size of protein per meal for casual exercisers, two palms for daily trainers, up to three palms (~12 oz) for 80-100 mile/week marathoners; two fist-sized vegetable servings per meal; two-thumb-size portion of healthy fat 1-2x/meal; grain/starch servings scaled to training volume (zero if untrained, one if training, two if in heavy training). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete, runner-scaled portion heuristic)
- Recommends against long-term calorie counting (mentally exhausting, "miserable existence"), but endorses short 2-4 day tracking stints as a "gut check" for elite-level athletes working with a professional. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on both extreme calorie-counting and the "just eat whatever, you're a runner" belief) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, but the "short calibration stint" nuance may be a gap)
- Hydration formula: bodyweight (lbs) ÷ 2 = base ounces of water/day, +16 oz per hour of exercise. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete formula)
- Electrolyte self-test: salt your food heavily for a day; if you feel great next day you needed it, if bloated/puffy you didn't. Recommends against electrolyte products with artificial food dyes/colorings. (PRACTICE)
- On carb-loading: reframes it as relative to one's current diet rather than "big pasta meal for everyone" — a fat-adapted person's "carb load" might just be one sweet potato; a sugar-burner may genuinely need the big pasta meal. Warns carbs are hydrophilic and can cause "slushy belly" if you load beyond your norm. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on universal carb-loading advice) (POSSIBLE GAP — individualized carb-loading framing)
- Endorses periodic fasted training runs (moderate distance, not races/hard intervals) to build fat-adaptation and make race-day bonking "less painful"; suggests easing in via a greens-powder drink or (less preferred, causes her GI issues) bulletproof coffee. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — fasted-run protocol specifics)
- Recommends most people should be able to run 2 hours without any fuel (water only); start in-run fueling around the 2-hour mark for most, earlier (~1 hr) for those with poor baseline nutrition habits. For marathon fueling, start early (6-8 miles) in small, steady doses (e.g., half a gel every 4-5 miles) rather than big blood-sugar spikes; pair carbs with some fat when possible (e.g., Larabar, Huma gel with chia). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, though the "small steady doses to avoid a spike-and-crash" framing is a specific angle)
- Recovery nutrition: protein + non-spiky carbs (sweet potato, whole grains, vegetables) within roughly 60 minutes post-workout (concedes the "30-minute window" is optimal-but-not-mandatory); body is especially insulin-sensitive post-workout, "so if you're ever going to have an ice cream sundae, do it then" (not as a routine encouragement). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery)
- Skeptical of intermittent fasting for endurance athletes: fine for sedentary/HIIT-only populations, but impractical for runners doing significant daily volume because it's hard to fuel workouts and get enough micronutrients in a short eating window. (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on trendy IF enthusiasm) (POSSIBLE GAP — direct IF-vs-endurance-training discussion)
- Sleep: recommends 7-8 hours as average optimal, emphasizes quality over quantity. Practical quality levers: magnesium supplementation (claims ~70% of athletes are deficient — asserted, uncited), reducing screens/blue light before bed (avoid phone in bed, use blue-light-blocking glasses or f.lux app), avoiding sugar ~1 hour before bed, cool bedroom temp (~67°F), and blocking ambient light sources (electronics' standby LEDs) for full "blackout." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — sleep is only lightly covered by "general recovery" typically; this concrete quality checklist may be a gap)
- Magnesium is described as self-limiting for overdose (excess causes diarrhea/GI upset — relevant for runners' "runner's trot" risk). (SCIENCE — asserted, no citation) (POSSIBLE GAP)

## Episode #46: Race Strategy from 5K to Marathon
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris McClung and Steve Magness (both Rogue Running coaches; Steve is a former collegiate coach at University of Texas and elite 800m runner). Intro contains a NYT-study critique (skip) and Ironman/marathon news recap (skip); race-strategy content begins roughly a third of the way in.

**Concepts & claims:**
- 5K race plan: treat it as one of the hardest race distances precisely because it's short and intense; get out 5-10 seconds/mile faster than goal pace for mile 1 (citing "science" that early adrenaline provides free energy — vaguely referenced, not a named study), hold goal pace mile 2, empty the tank mile 3. Going out even 8 seconds too fast beyond that margin can "put the nail in the coffin." (PRACTICE, one claim tagged SCIENCE but uncited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general race pacing, though the "adrenaline gives free time in mile 1" claim is a specific, checkable angle)
- 5K/10K "in over your head" recovery: unlike the marathon, a 5K/10K runner who goes out too hard can back off, recompose, and reconnect with target pace — the shorter distance is more forgiving of an early mistake than a marathon. (PRACTICE)
- 10K strategy is explicitly NOT even-split by design: recommends a repeated "press to the edge, back off for ~60 seconds, press again" surge pattern rather than locking into one per-mile pace, because "the mind can't stay maximally focused for the whole 10K" — locking into one number causes mental fatigue/fade around miles 4-5. Uses an anecdote of forcing an athlete to use a 1-minute interval beeper during a race to train this. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific pacing-psychology tactic beyond generic "run negative splits")
- Warm-up "secret": before a 5K/10K/half, insert 5-7 minutes of tempo/half-marathon-pace running into an otherwise easy warm-up jog — anecdotally reduces the "shock" of race-pace lactate at the start, no scientific proof offered, explicitly framed as experience-based only. (PRACTICE, explicitly not backed by science) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete, unusual warm-up protocol)
- Half marathon is described as the most physiologically "forgiving" of the standard distances — doesn't push the athlete past aerobic/anaerobic thresholds into the pain most associated with the 5K/10K/marathon, so runners can dig out of an early pacing mistake more easily and it's a good distance for athletes to test/find their limits. Recommended structure: start 5-10 sec/mile slower than goal for first 2-3 miles, even splits to mile 9-10, then negative split the final 5K. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general pacing)
- Marathon philosophy, repeated mantra: "the marathon always wins" — suffering late is inevitable regardless of training/pacing, so the goal is to optimize how you feel late, not eliminate suffering; frames this as fundamentally a psychological-preparation problem, not just physiological. Cites elite Jordan Hasay's post-Chicago quote (paraphrased) that "her job is to suffer" and she takes a kind of pleasure/acceptance in that rather than panicking. (PRACTICE/quote worth preserving) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — mental prep for suffering, general sports psych, but the explicit framing may add value)
- Explicit debunking of "banking time" (running the early marathon miles fast because "it's easier early" to create a cushion for the end): called "the silliest approach ever... pouring jet fuel on a fire." Offers a "rule of three": being 1 minute ahead of goal pace at the half predicts being roughly 3 minutes behind pace by the finish. (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly rebuts a common runner belief) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general marathon pacing, but "rule of three" ratio is a specific quotable heuristic)
- Applied 1-on-1 coaching example (Chris's own "Run for the Water" 10-mile hilly race): coach reframes pacing decision around the athlete's stated "purpose" (inspiring others who've faced setbacks) rather than pure physiology — argues that knowing your psychological "why" should determine risk tolerance in the closing miles, using a basketball "ask for the ball / take the last shot" analogy: be aggressive in the middle of a hilly race (not the start) so you're positioned to "take the last shot" at the finish rather than hoping to hang on. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY — "ask for the ball" as a race-strategy metaphor tied to a stated purpose) (POSSIBLE GAP — tying race-strategy decisions explicitly to a pre-defined "statement of purpose")
- Course-specific tactic: on a hilly course, don't try to hold one pace-per-mile; instead "take the top" of each hill (don't surge at the bottom, push hard cresting the hill) then let the downhill carry you — an old cross-country coaching cue attributed to the host's father. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — hill-specific pacing cue)
- On pacers: recommends near-total distrust of official race pacers unless verified in advance (checking their pace philosophy/experience at the expo) — pacers often blow up, go off pace, or run a philosophy (e.g., strict even splits) that doesn't fit a hilly course. Only endorses using a pace group deliberately picked 5-10 sec/mile slower than goal, purely to stay calm early. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT)

## Episode #48: Seven Pillars of Running Wisdom with Alex Hutchinson
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Alex Hutchinson — PhD in physics (University of Cambridge), former NSA nanocomputing researcher, longtime "Sweat Science" science-of-running columnist for Runner's World (now Outside), author of forthcoming book *Endure: Mind, Body, and the Curiously Elastic Limits of Human Performance*. Competitive runner (1500m specialist in college, one marathon). This is the single richest science-forward episode in the batch.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Pillar 1 — "Moderation is more than you're doing": epidemiological evidence does not show running increases osteoarthritis risk (if anything, regular runners show lower or equal risk vs. non-runners); the "running ruins your heart/knees" narrative has a long history of being overstated. There IS legitimate emerging evidence that lifetime ultra-high-volume endurance athletes (hundreds of ultramarathons/Ironmans) may show cardiac changes (arterial plaque, atrial fibrillation risk) — but Hutchinson stresses this applies to a tiny extreme-volume population, not typical marathon-a-year runners, and society is "far better off" erring toward more running not less. (SCIENCE — named research area, hedged appropriately as inconclusive on outcomes) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — but the calibrated "who does this actually apply to" framing is a useful nuance)
- Physiological basis for "more running = faster": training grows heart stroke volume, new blood vessel networks, and increased mitochondrial density; VO2max is limited by multiple linked systems (breathing muscles, lung-blood gas exchange, cardiac output, muscle oxygen extraction) with genuine scientific debate over which is most limiting — all of them adapt with training. Rough heuristic: volume trains the cardiovascular delivery system more, intensity trains muscle-level adaptation more, but there's heavy crossover ("mileage is for the heart, intervals are for the muscles" as an oversimplification). (SCIENCE, general research area not tied to a single named study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base / VO2max)
- Notes there is essentially NO good research correlating health outcomes with stress/rest balance (hard-day-hard-day-hard-day vs. periodized training) — existing epidemiological data comes from crude decades-old self-reported exercise questionnaires. Cites Stephen Seiler (Texas-born, Norway-based exercise scientist) and the "polarized training" model (also referenced via Matt Fitzgerald's *80/20 Running*) as the best evidence-based framework: most volume easy, a small proportion hard. (SCIENCE — names a specific researcher, Seiler, and a specific book) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — polarized/80-20 training, but worth checking if Seiler is already cited)
- Pillar 2 — "No magic in a bottle": after ~10 years covering supplement studies, concludes the vast majority never replicate and don't have repeatable effects beyond tiny underpowered studies; describes supplement-seeking as a "psychological shortcut" that substitutes for the harder work of eating whole foods, potentially counterproductive. Multivitamins specifically: large-scale studies (hundreds of thousands of subjects) suggest no benefit or even mild harm. (SCIENCE/DISAGREEMENT — explicitly debunks a widespread practice) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "supplement psychology as an out/excuse" framing specifically)
- Beet juice/nitrates: mechanism — dietary nitrate → converted to nitrite by oral bacteria → converted to nitric oxide in the body → vasodilation. Evidence: taking beet juice ~2-3 hrs before exercise (or loading over ~12 hrs) improves running economy (oxygen cost of a given pace) by a couple percent — one of the few supplements with real repeatable evidence, though effects fade for elite/already-optimized athletes. Side effects: GI upset, discolored urine; concentrated "beet shots" (brand Beet-It) are easier on the stomach. Nitrates are also found in leafy greens (arugula, spinach) and rhubarb, with possible blood-pressure/health benefits independent of running. (SCIENCE — specific mechanism and named product) (POSSIBLE GAP — likely worth checking if beet juice/nitrate mechanism is covered)
- Caffeine: recent research suggests habitual coffee drinkers get roughly the same performance boost from race-day caffeine as non-habitual drinkers — challenges the common practice of a pre-race caffeine "detox" week. Effective dose range ~3-6 mg/kg bodyweight, with little extra benefit above the low end (~one strong coffee equivalent). A caffeinated gel taken 2/3-3/4 through a marathon can give a late boost. Individual variability in caffeine metabolism means some people get little or even adverse effects — recommends testing in training first. (SCIENCE, general research area cited but not single-study-named) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, but "detox week is unnecessary" is a specific, checkable, possibly-surprising claim)
- Pillar 3 — "Best technology is the device between your ears": GPS/heart-rate garment over-reliance can prevent runners from developing internal pacing sense/intuition. Personal anecdote: coach Matt Centrowitz Sr. would get furious and make Hutchinson throw his watch in the infield if caught checking splits too often during intervals. Practical protocol suggested: use the device purely to COLLECT baseline data for an extended period (don't let it dictate effort) before using it to guide training decisions; recalibrate this way periodically. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY — "throw the watch in the infield") (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific "collect-data-only phase" protocol for GPS watch use, and the coach term "geek-o-meter" is a nice line if wanted)
- On intuition vs. data: intuition is essential for racing (can't fully outsource real-time pacing decisions to a device) but not everyone's intuition is trustworthy — some personality types instinctively push too hard; an external voice (coach or objective data trend) can catch this before it becomes injury/overtraining. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psych, though framed distinctly around data-vs-intuition tension)
- Pillar 4 — "You probably got injured from doing too much too soon": cites a University of Calgary line of research attributing ~80% of running injuries to rapid load increases (asserted with a named institution, not a specific paper/author). Individual variability is huge — some can increase 20%/week safely, others get hurt at 5%. The classic "10% rule" is an imperfect but reasonable heuristic; a more modern approach is the "acute:chronic workload ratio" — compare the current week's training to the trailing 4-week average; if current week is ~15-20%+ above that average, injury risk rises. (SCIENCE — names an institution and a specific ratio-based framework) (POSSIBLE GAP — the acute:chronic ratio concept specifically, if not already covered, is a more modern/precise framing than the 10% rule)
- Counterintuitive point: consistent higher mileage over time may LOWER injury risk (not raise it) because the body adapts, running economy improves, and biomechanical inefficiencies get ironed out with volume — meaning sometimes an athlete's problem is "did too little in prior weeks" rather than "did too much now." Ties into acute:chronic ratio — someone detrained after time off has a lower chronic baseline, so a "normal" load for them now reads as a spike. (SCIENCE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the assumption that more mileage always means more injury risk) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Pillar 5 — variety/staleness: recommends periodically adding one new workout format/style per season, partly for physiological novelty but explicitly more for mental engagement — describes a specific hybrid workout (track 1200m fast-start simulation → shoe change → tempo run to a hill → 5x hill repeats → tempo back → track 1200m) as an example of a workout so structurally engaging it prevents "autopilot." Balances this against keeping some "classic" repeatable benchmark workouts (e.g., 5x1000m) for longitudinal comparison. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a fully worked example of a "mixed-modality" workout as a training-variety technique)
- Pillar 6 — running form: repeated finding across studies — if you consciously instruct a runner to change ANY specific form element (arm carriage, stride rate, etc.) mid-run, efficiency immediately WORSENS (body burns more oxygen); the "it'll get more efficient after 6 weeks of adaptation" claim is popular but Hutchinson notes no study has actually demonstrated this long-term adaptation benefit — it remains "an article of faith." The evidence-backed path to better form is simply running more, because the body self-selects efficient patterns through accumulated volume (though he shares an anecdote of watching NYC Marathon back-of-pack runners with clearly inefficient, "glued-arm" form, suggesting some basic coaching cues might still help). (SCIENCE/DISAGREEMENT — explicitly flags a popular claim as scientifically unproven) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — running economy/form, but the "consciously fixing form makes you less efficient short-term, and long-term benefit is unproven" nuance is a specific, useful, checkable claim)
- Pillar 7 — "you're capable of more than you think": previews his book's core theme — the brain/central governor plays a much larger, more "curiously elastic" role in setting physical performance limits than commonly assumed, but explicitly NOT "if you believe you can achieve" simplism; self-belief and control of internal monologue/self-talk measurably affect performance ceiling. Personal reflection: as a natural skeptic he wishes he'd been better at silencing doubt and trusting his coach/training plan rather than over-analyzing during his own racing career. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — describes an area of legitimate emerging sports-science research on CNS/perceived-effort limits) (POSSIBLE GAP — central-governor/CNS-limits-of-performance theory may be worth checking against existing site content on sports psychology, since this is a more evidence-grounded, less "woo" version of mental-limits framing)
- Side rant (hosts, not Hutchinson): critiques a Runner's World article ("The Ultimate Guide to Not Running") for irresponsibly implying 3 consecutive days of running causes stress fractures, and for recommending video games as a recovery activity; hosts' counter-framing: "rest is not doing nothing... movement equals blood flow equals healing," and true rest should include active-recovery modalities (foam rolling, cross-training, light movement) rather than total inactivity. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery, but "movement=blood flow=healing" is a clean quotable framing)

## Episode #49: Dealing with Injury
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts only (Chris McClung, Steve Magness), both Rogue Running coaches, explicitly caveat they are not doctors/not discussing specific injury diagnoses, only the injury-management process.

**Concepts & claims:**
- "Four-day rule" for triage: don't panic about a new ache/pain/niggle until it persists 4 days; many things that flare up resolve within 2-3 days on their own. If it persists past 4 days, see a medical professional promptly. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, memorable triage heuristic)
- Complementary "pain scale rule": if pain is 3/10 or less on a 1-10 scale AND it doesn't worsen as the run progresses, it's generally safe to keep running on it; attributed partly to a local sports-therapy provider's guidance. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Repeated framing: "movement equals blood flow equals healing" — for soft-tissue (non-bone) injuries, continued appropriate movement is often better than total rest because rest without movement lets tissue get tight/stagnant; "stop running" is characterized as actively bad/counterproductive advice for soft-tissue issues (as opposed to bone stress injuries, where rest is correct). (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — explicitly rebuts "just stop running" as a default answer) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery, though the sharp distinction between soft-tissue vs. bone-injury rest protocols is a specific, useful nuance)
- Kinetic-chain compensation concept: the first injury site is rarely what eventually sidelines a runner — compensating movement patterns create secondary/tertiary problems elsewhere in the chain (e.g., "if your ankle hurts, expect the opposite hip to start bothering you"), and by the time a 3rd or 4th-order compensation issue appears, it's hard for a provider to trace back to the original cause. Plantar fasciitis specifically is cited as often caused by tightness ABOVE the foot (calf/Achilles or upper-body mechanics) rather than a foot-local problem. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — kinetic-chain compensation as an explicit injury-progression model)
- Choosing a medical provider: prioritize someone who is BOTH a general-practice AND sports-medicine trained doctor for a "wide lens" view; absent that, ask local running specialty stores or university track/cross-country coaches who they refer athletes to (since university medical staff usually refer out rather than treat in-house). Orthopedic surgeons are explicitly flagged as often poorly suited to running-specific soft-tissue issues since their training centers on acute trauma/surgery, not overuse injuries. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete provider-selection heuristic, useful for a site not otherwise likely to cover "how to find a sports medicine provider")
- Red flag: if a provider's first-line answer is "just stop running for six weeks" and there's no bone-stress diagnosis, seek a second opinion — this is characterized as a common default from providers unfamiliar with runners. Give any single provider 2-3 sessions and full compliance with their prescribed exercises before concluding they aren't working; if there's no change/movement after that fair trial, move on ("it takes a village sometimes to heal an injured runner"). (PRACTICE)
- Encourages openness to modern chiropractic care, noting it has shifted substantially from pure spinal-adjustment practice toward PT-like functional treatment over the last 10-15 years, contrary to older stereotypes. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Active-recovery principle: almost no soft-tissue injuries heal well from "doing nothing" — active recovery (physical therapy exercises, targeted strengthening, foam rolling ~20-30 min/day) is core to fixing the root cause, not just resting; the rule of thumb given is "you should be sick and tired of doing your PT work — if you're not, you're not doing enough of it." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery, but the "if you're not sick of your PT work you're not doing enough" framing is quotable)
- Cross-training during injury: aqua jogging, elliptical, and stationary/arm bike are viable but mentally grueling; for shorter windows (2-5 weeks) these can preserve some fitness, but for longer layoffs (6+ weeks) coach explicitly recommends NOT trying to maintain aerobic fitness at all — take the downtime, shift focus to strength/weakness work, and let fitness detrain, because doubling down on cross-training can leave athletes aerobically ahead of what their tendons/ligaments/muscles can handle on return (creating re-injury risk), and because most of the aerobic-specific adaptation (mitochondrial etc.) is highly running-specific anyway. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the assumption you must always cross-train to "not lose fitness") (POSSIBLE GAP — the "sometimes it's better to just let fitness go than to over-cross-train" argument is a specific and somewhat counterintuitive angle)
- AlterG anti-gravity treadmill described in detail as a valuable tool for returning to running mechanics at reduced body-weight percentages (e.g., 60% or 40% bodyweight) while injured; anecdote of a collegiate athlete with a known stress fracture who used the AlterG to get 2nd at NCAA Championships in the 5000m, then had to shut down the rest of the season — explicit acknowledgment this was a risky, atypical call. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — AlterG/reduced-bodyweight training as a specific injury-return tool)
- Stress fractures specifically: coach's strong personal opinion is true rest (nothing, for 6+ weeks) is usually correct, contrasted with a personal anecdote of doing a 3-hour "20-mile run" on an elliptical with an undiagnosed stress fracture, which worsened the injury and extended recovery. (PRACTICE, personal-experience-based)
- Mental/emotional side of injury: framed as a normal, expected cost of training at the edge of one's capability, not a personal failure; recommends acknowledging emotions (anger, fear, depression) rather than suppressing them, temporarily stepping back from one's training group/community (to avoid comparison-driven frustration) while still allowing occasional brief social check-ins, and reframing the injury as a source of lessons (what caused it, what routine changes prevent recurrence) — cites Shalane Flanagan's 2017 stress fracture/comeback-to-New-York-win story as a model of injury-enforced rest leading to a breakthrough result. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology, though the specific injury-mourning framing is a nice angle)
- Return-to-running protocol: "add days first, then add distance, then add speed" — rebuild frequency before volume before intensity. Recommends switching from mileage-based to TIME-based progression (e.g., 10% increases in minutes, not miles) during return, since 10% of a small mileage base is a trivially small/demotivating number. Don't select a comeback race/goal until back to full normal training load — picking a race target too early causes premature rushing. Explicit fitness-retention claim: consistent-year-over-year runners never fully "reset to zero" — detraining caps out around 25-30% loss of capability and rebounds quickly to within ~10-20% of prior fitness; give it "4 weeks not enough, 6 weeks starting to feel normal, 8 weeks before it's reasonable to set a new goal." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the day/distance/speed sequencing rule and the time-based-percentage progression trick are concrete, useful, and likely novel relative to generic "ease back in" advice)
- Diet/weight management during injury (flagged by the coach himself as "a little controversial"): athletes often keep eating at their in-training caloric level while activity drops sharply during injury, and the resulting 10-15+ lb gain can itself become the limiting factor in a successful comeback (added bodyweight stresses the same still-healing structures); recommends consciously adjusting intake during injury layoffs while not swinging to extreme restriction. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — an intentionally provocative, less commonly stated piece of injury advice) (POSSIBLE GAP — likely not covered elsewhere; directly ties body-composition management to injury-recovery success)

## Episode #5: Marathon Race Planning
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts only (Chris McClung, Steve Magness), Rogue Running coaches. Early-numbered episode (recorded near the show's launch); intro banter/current-events section is comparatively short.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Presents a structured 5-part marathon race-planning framework meant to be written down as a checklist: (1) logistics, (2) pace strategy, (3) nutrition/hydration, (4) gear choices, (5) mental preparation. Explicit reasoning: writing it down "internalizes" the plan and makes execution more likely, and having a checklist reduces the number of "variables" that can go wrong on race day. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the 5-category checklist structure itself, as a reusable framework, may be a good organizing device even if individual components overlap existing topics)
- Logistics category: research race-specific quirks in advance (bib pickup timing/location, transport to start, corral entry timing, porta-potty locations) by interviewing others who've run the race or reading race-review sites (mentions MarathonGuide.com); plan a specific physical meetup spot with any running partners BEFORE the crowds build (personal anecdote of missing a training partner at the Chicago Marathon start due to a vague "meet at a tree" plan). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — pre-race logistics planning generally underdiscussed relative to training-specific content)
- Pace-strategy category: negative splits recommended ~90-95% of the time; course profile should dictate the specific split strategy (e.g., Boston's downhill-heavy start suggests a slight positive split is fine, since effort stays even even as pace numbers vary; a hilly local course like the Austin Marathon demands aggressive negative splitting). Recommends breaking the race into "sections" that follow the course's natural terrain breaks (e.g., 6 uneven chunks for a hilly course) rather than forcing a rigid mile-by-mile split target, since "I've never had an athlete run marathon goal pace for 26.2 miles... but I've had them finish the marathon at their goal pace." Bill Bowerman's "the hay is in the barn" quoted re: taper mentality (work is done, don't try to add fitness late). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general marathon pacing, though the terrain-based "sectioning" alternative to mile splits is a specific technique)
- "Rule of three" on banking time (also stated independently in ep 46): if you're 1 minute ahead of goal pace at the half, expect to be roughly 3 minutes behind by the finish — explicitly used as a deterrent against early over-pacing. (PRACTICE, repeated across episodes — cross-episode consistency, not a disagreement)
- Distrust of official race pacers (also echoed in ep 46): recommends checking a pacer's stated philosophy/experience at the expo before trusting them, and treating even a "trusted" pacer as only ~50% reliable; the only endorsed use is a pace group deliberately 10-15 sec/mile SLOWER than goal, purely to feel calm and controlled. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT)
- Nutrition/hydration category: recommends starting "hyper-hydration" (up to 1 gallon of water/day) a full WEEK before race day (not just 1-2 days before), stopping around noon/1pm the day before the race to allow normal bladder function and better sleep that night; explains that starting hyper-hydration too late can flush electrolytes and cause you to feel "washed out" by mile 6-7. Also recommends deliberately eating saltier foods in the week before a race (pretzels, salty carbs) as an easier way to load electrolytes than loading carbohydrates.
