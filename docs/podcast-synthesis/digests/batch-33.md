## Episode #396: What is Aerobic Strength?
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — hosts only: Chris McClung (Rogue Running head coach) and James Dodds (Rogue coach), discussing their own coaching book chapter-by-chapter.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Taper reminders (intro segment): don't introduce anything new during taper; keep the same routine/frequency of runs but reduce volume; simple taper math — cut back 1 mile per run per week (3-week taper for marathon, 2-week for half, 1-week for 5K/10K). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — tapering)
- "Taper madness" — anxiety/fear during taper is normal even for veteran marathoners and never fully goes away, you just get better at managing it. Two coping strategies named: associative (channel anxious energy into race prep — visualization, nutrition planning, logistics) and dissociative (distract yourself — movies, books, friends) (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the associative/dissociative coping framework specifically)
- Aerobic strength ≠ muscular strength — it's "staying power"/endurance, the ability to sustain a hard-but-still-aerobic pace long enough to unleash a finishing kick; framed as the "middle gears" of the aerobic engine (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic threshold/lactate threshold, though the "aerobic strength" phase terminology itself is a distinct coaching framework, POSSIBLE GAP)
- Guiding Principle 1: effort trumps pace in this phase — aerobic strength develops in the window between aerobic threshold and lactate threshold, which is an effort-based zone; identical effort on a hot/humid day vs. a cool day can mean very different paces but the same physiological benefit. Recommends training in pace *ranges*, not pinpoint paces (SCIENCE, asserted without a named study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Guiding Principle 2: control and relaxation ("smooth and relaxed") — the improvement vector in this phase is making a given pace/effort feel *easier*, not finding a new edge. McClung's anecdote: in a 10K he learned to consciously relax into an uncomfortable pace and it became effortless; compares to throwing a ball or golf swing — trying "as hard as possible" shortens the throw, while relaxing lets it fly further (ANALOGY, PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit teaching of "relaxation as a trainable skill, not just an outcome" is a distinct, actionable angle beyond generic race-effort advice)
- Alan Couzens (endurance coach) quote, approvingly cited: "If someone tells you that you need to do high-intensity interval training in order to improve your VO2 max, then you should jog slowly away." (DISAGREEMENT with the common belief that VO2 max requires HIIT)
- Guiding Principle 3: "when in doubt, be conservative" — a coach's own psychological framing: pushing too hard/too soon signals your body prematurely that "something big is coming" and can compromise the ultimate peak. Physiological training load and psychological signaling are treated as two separate levers on peaking (PRACTICE, somewhat SCIENCE-flavored but not study-cited) (POSSIBLE GAP — "signaling your body via training decisions" as a distinct peaking mechanism)
- Volume is still king in this phase — target is to go from ~80% of peak weekly volume (end of base phase) to 100% of peak volume by the end of aerobic strength phase, prioritized above adding quality workouts. If an athlete is behind schedule, the fix is to de-prioritize/flatten quality work, never to skip volume-building to "catch up" on speed (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodized volume progression)
- Frank Shorter quote (attributed): hills are "speed work in disguise" — ramps up intensity without the same musculoskeletal pounding as track speed work, while also building aerobic strength (ANALOGY/quote) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — hill training)
- Named example workouts for this phase: "Lumberjack Twist" (hill sprints + continuous AT-pace running + more hill sprints — a combo hitting endurance, hills, and neuromuscular recruitment in one session), "Hills Plus Tempo" (repeated short hill reps immediately into a tempo loop with minimal recovery), and the "Julia" workout (named after elite Julia Stamps Malone via a coaching lineage — Julia → coach Carmen → Rogue): a 1-1 or 2-2 fartlek "appetizer" (16-20 min) generating fatigue, followed by a continuous 16-20 min tempo effort (PRACTICE, concrete session designs) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific named workout templates)
- "Fit enough to train" concept: athletes must earn the right to add quality/intensity by first building an adequate volume base — echoed via a general-contractor/remodeling analogy (don't paint cabinets before the walls are up) (ANALOGY, PRACTICE)
- Down weeks (recovery weeks) are used to keep short, sharp "priming"/sharpening work (e.g., 5K-pace strides or short reps) alive even while overall volume drops 15-25%, so speed doesn't atrophy during a volume-focused phase (PRACTICE)

## Episode #397: Getting Race Specific
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — hosts only: Chris McClung and James Dodds.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Race-specific phase has four goals: (1) dial into race pace, (2) sharpen speed so race pace feels easier, (3) ride the edge of fitness while staying healthy, (4) prepare the mind (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race-specific/peak training block)
- Faster-than-race-pace prescriptions are relative to event distance: marathoners work in half-marathon/10K pace ranges, half-marathoners work in 5K-10K pace ranges, to make race pace "feel easier" without excess injury risk — deliberately avoiding pushing all the way to 5K/mile pace for marathoners ("greedy" pacing) (PRACTICE)
- Guiding principle reversal from the prior phase: in aerobic strength, effort trumps pace; in race-specific phase, **pace trumps effort** — a direct, explicitly stated contrast in the same coaching framework depending on training phase (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT/contrast worth flagging — same coaches use opposite defaults by phase, useful nuance if the site discusses "effort vs. pace" training)
- Guiding principle reversal #2: "when in doubt, press" (take the more aggressive choice) — contrasts with the aerobic-strength-phase default of "when in doubt, be conservative." Framed as earned permission ("fit enough to make those choices") rather than an arbitrary rule change, since it follows ~15 weeks of base + volume work (PRACTICE)
- "Minimize noise and distractions" — in the final 5-7 weeks, avoid not just obviously bad choices (late nights) but also *new "good" things* (starting yoga, changing diet, adding strength training) because there's no time to safely assimilate a new stimulus this close to race day (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicitly warning against introducing new "healthy" habits during a taper/peak window is a more specific and counlitive angle than generic taper advice)
- Recommends "doubling down" on already-established recovery precision during this window — faster post-workout rehydration, hitting the ~90-minute post-run nutrition window consistently (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — post-workout nutrition timing)
- Race-pace workouts should use short, frequent segments (2-5 miles for marathon pace, 2-3 miles for half) rather than very long continuous stints — rationale: "finding the pace" repeatedly is as important a skill as holding it (PRACTICE)
- "McClung Special" — named marathon race-simulation long run: 5-mile progression from marathon pace+30s down to marathon pace, short recovery, a 3-mile hard 1-1 fartlek at 5K effort to generate fatigue, another short recovery, then a 5-mile "finish simulation" holding then dropping below marathon pace — designed to let an athlete rehearse both the start and the finish of a marathon without running 26 miles (PRACTICE, concrete workout) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, well-reasoned race-simulation workout design)
- "McCoba" workout (a portmanteau of McClung + Renato Canova) — a 4-3-2-1 mile progression (marathon pace+, then marathon pace, then faster) tacked onto the end of a 20-mile long run to simulate a strong marathon finish on tired legs (PRACTICE)
- "Long Run Quality" (LRQ) progression system — a structured 3-step escalation used across a training cycle: LRQ1 = simple race-pace segments with recovery; LRQ2 = adds in-and-out/variable-pace segments; LRQ3 = a full race simulator (start + finish elements). Athletes are meant to build up to LRQ3 over multiple training cycles, not attempt it cold (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a systematic, named progression framework for long-run quality workouts, more specific than "do some long runs with pace work")
- Coaches say they don't mind when race-specific-phase workouts "go poorly" — it forces the athlete to practice the mental side (fighting through adversity) before race day, and a single bad workout is "one data point," not predictive of race outcome (PRACTICE, mental-training angle) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psych, though the specific framing of coach-endorsed workout failure as valuable is a nice angle)

## Episode #40: Becoming a [Road] Warrior
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — hosts only: Chris McClung and Steve Sisson (Rogue co-founder/coach, former University of Texas collegiate coach). This is the 8th and final episode in their mental-training series.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Note: roughly the first third of this episode is pure current-events banter (US road racing results, Alberto Salazar/Galen Rupp speculation, a tribute to the death of elite runner David Torrance) — discarded as non-generalizable.
- Four-stage mental-skill-acquisition model, explicitly presented as sequential and non-skippable:
  1. **Develop the skill in isolation ("learn")** — requires a quiet, repeatable, distraction-free setting (explicitly borrows the psychedelics-culture term "set and setting"); practice sessions break into input (listening/reading), processing (writing, reflecting), and output (e.g., producing a purpose statement) (ANALOGY, PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a structured input/processing/output practice model for mental skills)
  2. **Develop the skill in the mind ("knowing")** — a checkpoint distinct from learning: you must believe the tool will work for you and understand how it applies to your specific situation before moving on; guitar-chord analogy for "knowing how" vs. merely having been exposed to a concept (ANALOGY)
  3. **Develop the skill in the body ("living it")** — road-test the skill in real training: start with small, selective applications (e.g., using self-talk only twice per run), then move to deliberately "successful scenarios" designed so the athlete tastes success without needing full completion (concrete example: the "Aussie 5K" workout used to road-test self-talk in early reps before shifting full focus to execution), then to genuinely challenging scenarios (goal race, hard workout); expect and accept failure as part of this stage; take a deliberate break after a big test regardless of outcome, since sustained focus on one skill leads to burnout (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the graduated "safe → successful → challenging" scenario design for testing mental skills is a specific, actionable coaching technique)
  4. **Integration/mastery ("being")** — the skill becomes automatic and unconscious; illustrated via a John Mayer interview anecdote about songwriting and guitar practice ("I played with the Grateful Dead last night, I'm good") (ANALOGY)
- Concrete example applying the model: coaching self-talk into a hot, tactically awkward relay leg — build a rehearsed self-talk phrase into the race plan itself (deploy right after the first 800m); "I think I can... I know I can" rhythm-matters-more-than-words anecdote (PRACTICE)
- General framing: mental skills training should be treated as its own disciplined practice regimen with the same rigor/periodization mindset as physical training — you don't "wing it" on race day (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly for sports psychology/self-talk/visualization, but this specific staged pedagogical model for *how* to acquire a mental skill is more specific than generic sports-psych content) (POSSIBLE GAP)

## Episode #42: The One Percents
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — hosts only: Chris McClung and Steve Sisson (former University of Texas collegiate coach).

**Concepts & claims:**

- "Turning the lights on/off" — warm-up philosophy: before running, do a body check-in (sit, feel feet/calves, notice tight spots — described as "stretching without stretching"), then light skipping drills/leg swings, then a minimum 20-minute easy warm-up jog, then a set dynamic-drill routine (heel-toe walks/skips, high knees, butt kicks, skipping, carioca) plus strides before any quality workout (PRACTICE, ANALOGY)

- Claimed optimal timing for supplemental strength work is roughly 6-8 hours after a hard workout (or immediately after if that's not feasible), attributed to post-exercise hormone/recovery timing, so that hard training days stay hard and easy/recovery days stay genuinely easy (SCIENCE claim, asserted as "research has told folks," no study named) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, uncommon timing recommendation)

## Episode #43: Running and Pregnancy
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Karen Rueff — perinatal fitness specialist and personal trainer (certified ~10 years), former college professor, lifelong runner who has run a 3:44 marathon eight months postpartum; draws on her own three pregnancies (including a home birth with a postpartum hemorrhage) and years of coaching pregnant/postpartum clients. Hosts also reference Kara Goucher's Boston Marathon PR run seven months postpartum.

**Concepts & claims:**
- This entire episode covers a topic area (pregnancy/postpartum running) that is plausibly outside the scope of the site's existing exercise-physiology/training/nutrition/recovery/sports-psych content — flag the whole episode as a likely content gap domain. (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Framing: pregnancy is "a health condition, not a medical condition" — guest asserts (without citing a specific study) that active mothers have healthier babies both immediately and in long-term outcomes (SCIENCE claim, asserted)
- No universal trimester-based cutoff for when to stop running — guest ran 2 miles on her own due date in one pregnancy, but developed shin splints and had to stop running at 26 weeks in another; framed as purely individual, day-to-day (PRACTICE/anecdote)
- Choosing a supportive OB/midwife matters because runner-specific baselines (naturally low resting heart rate/blood pressure) can look abnormal against standard prenatal metrics if the provider doesn't know the patient is an athlete (PRACTICE)
- Myth debunked: pregnant women should keep heart rate under 140 bpm — guest calls this false; the actual concern is *sustained* time at the top of one's target zone (risk of oxygen-depletion dizziness), not a hard ceiling (DISAGREEMENT with a popular belief, SCIENCE claim asserted)
- After ~15-16 weeks, stop supine (on-back) exercises — a growing uterus can compress the vena cava and disrupt blood flow; substitute standing core work for crunches (SCIENCE claim, asserted)
- Round ligament pain (from the stretching ligament supporting the growing uterus) is commonly misattributed by runners to hip flexor/psoas tightness (PRACTICE/clinical note)
- Later-pregnancy relaxin hormone increases joint laxity, which can manifest as sudden looseness in ankles/wrists — raises fall risk while running and should be treated as a monitoring flag (SCIENCE claim, asserted)
- Recommends avoiding trail running and treadmills during pregnancy due to reduced visual field (can't see feet later in pregnancy) and balance changes (PRACTICE)
- Changing center of gravity: keep torso tall and chest open rather than hunching forward, both for pelvic alignment/efficiency and because hunching further compresses already crowded lungs (PRACTICE)
- Red flags requiring immediate care-provider contact: any bleeding, dizziness (especially on standing) (PRACTICE/safety)
- Heat management: hydrate aggressively; because the fetus cannot dissipate heat via sweating the way the mother can, elevated core temperature is a distinct pregnancy-specific concern — use run/walk intervals and active cooling (ice, cold towels) (SCIENCE-based practice)
- C-section is major abdominal surgery (cuts through rectus and transverse abdominis); rushing back before proper healing undermines strength recovery, regardless of how routine the procedure has become (PRACTICE)
- Standard postpartum running clearance is the 6-week OB/midwife check (internal exam), though guest personally (with midwife clearance under close postnatal monitoring) ran at 2 weeks postpartum in two of her three pregnancies (PRACTICE/anecdote — explicitly not a general recommendation)
- Postpartum hemorrhage explained (placenta fails to fully detach in the "third stage of labor"); guest's own case required 8-12 weeks of very low activity and, due to associated anemia/fatigue, close to two years before running felt normal again (PRACTICE/anecdote, medical)
- Pushes back on the "get your body back" framing — postpartum body is a "new normal" (pelvis position changes even with C-section), not a return to a prior state (PRACTICE/philosophy)
- Common postpartum weakness pattern: weak glute medius (key to pelvic stability while running) and general glute underactivation — targeted strength/cross-training is often needed after birth (SCIENCE/PRACTICE claim, asserted from clinical experience)
- Urinary leakage while running postpartum is common but explicitly framed as "not normal, and fixable" through pelvic-floor/core-firing rehab — applies whether postpartum by 6 weeks or decades (PRACTICE, notable reframe) (POSSIBLE GAP — pelvic floor rehab is not typically part of general running-education content)
- Prolapse defined (bladder or vaginal-canal descent) as a distinct, serious red flag beyond ordinary postpartum discomfort; usually treatable via pelvic-floor physical therapy rather than surgery; running's repetitive impact can aggravate an existing prolapse if pelvic floor/core aren't functioning well (PRACTICE/medical)
- Diastasis recti (abdominal muscle separation) is very common in pregnancy and, per the guest, paradoxically *more* likely in women who were very fit pre-pregnancy (a strong rectus resists stretching until it suddenly separates); described as repairable through targeted rehab restoring functional/global strength, not just cosmetic appearance (SCIENCE/PRACTICE claim, asserted)
- Breastfeeding-and-running logistics: nurse before running rather than after (more comfortable); lactic acid buildup can flavor breast milk and cause some babies to refuse post-run milk; remove a sports bra immediately after running to avoid mastitis and reduced supply signaling (PRACTICE)
- Jogging stroller fit to the runner's own frame matters (a poor fit can distort natural running posture/gait); defer to manufacturer and pediatrician guidance on minimum baby age (PRACTICE, minor point)
- Coaching philosophy note: coaches report that guiding athletes through pregnancy made them better, more athlete-centered coaches generally, and several athletes reported becoming more body-attuned runners after going through pregnancy (PRACTICE/coaching philosophy)
