## Episode #10: Usain Bolt + Running by Feel
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts Chris and Steve (same as Ep. 1).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Opening Bolt-retirement discussion is mostly fan commentary/banter (stats, doping speculation, GOAT debate) — not much generalizable coaching content; skippable for synthesis purposes. Nothing tagged here.
- Four energy systems framework, explicitly defined and mapped to %-effort zones: (1) Aerobic conditioning (easy/recovery/long runs/steady runs, ~50-80% effort, "brewing" — no significant lactate accumulation unless glycogen runs out); (2) Aerobic power/capacity/strength (VO2max-type work, 5K–10K pace intervals, recovery time ≈ work time, ~80-90% effort); (3) Anaerobic conditioning (3K–1mile pace repeats with short rest, ~90-100% effort); (4) Anaerobic power (all-out lactate-flooded efforts, "100%+", rarely trained by distance runners). (SCIENCE — presented as established exercise-physiology framework but no specific researcher/study cited; general effort % figures are Sisson's own heuristic) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — energy systems/VO2max/threshold are probably already covered, but the specific %-effort mapping and "muscle system" analogy may be additive)
- Analogy: energy systems described as different "muscles within your aerobic toolkit" (like quads vs. biceps) — used to explain why different workout types train different physiological capacities. (ANALOGY)
- Distinction between "threshold/tempo" runs (defined strictly as half-marathon pace ±10 sec, pace-locked) versus "steady" runs (same effort zone/pace range but explicitly NOT pace-focused — "comfortably hard," feel-based). Coined phrase: "steady is as steady does." (PRACTICE)(ANALOGY: Forrest Gump reference) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific terminology distinction between "tempo" and "steady" runs at the same pace range is a nuanced, possibly novel framing)

(PRACTICE)(DISAGREEMENT — implicitly pushes back against standard pace-chart-driven training culture)
- GPS/heart-rate tech guidance ("Geek-O-Meter"): use it, but review data post-run rather than watching it live; using it live turns runners into "slaves" to the device and erodes trust in internal effort perception. Suggests occasionally running completely without a watch during base-building/reset phases. Crowbar analogy: technology is a tool being misused as a weapon against oneself. (PRACTICE)(ANALOGY: crowbar) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "use it post-run only" prescription and no-watch reset periods are a concrete, actionable practice)
- Practical pace-calibration tool: take recent marathon time and goal marathon time, generate equivalent-pace tables for 5K/10K/half/marathon for both (e.g., via McMillan calculator), then run any given workout somewhere between the "recent" and "goal" pace bands depending on how you feel that day — "run down to but no faster than" goal pace on a good day, "no slower than" recent-fitness pace on a bad day. Uses a self-rated effort scale of 5–10 (10 = hardest possible, 5 = easiest sustainable effort past 30 min). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific dual-pace-band practical protocol is concrete and actionable)
- Weekly training structure: four run types — recovery runs (easiest, blood flow/clearance), easy/filler runs including a "medium-long run" (roughly half long-run distance, run at easy pace but longer than typical easy days), one workout/quality day per week (for most Rogue athletes — advanced Team Rogue athletes do two), and the long run (20-40% of weekly mileage). Claim: the medium-long run is an underrated differentiator — "the difference between the ones that hit their goals and the ones that don't are the ones that do their medium-long days," because it's unsupervised/unglamorous (no group, no crowd) and requires self-motivated discipline. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the medium-long run as a distinctly named, emphasized workout type with claimed outsized impact)
- Microcycle/macrocycle periodization structure: 21-day microcycles (2 hard weeks + 1 down/easy week) as the "optimal" default for most runners; some elite programs use 10/14/28-day cycles instead of calendar weeks. Macrocycles (6-8 months, sometimes 4 for advanced/short-turnaround athletes) broken into four blocks: base+speed development, strength (hills, aerobic power), race-specific speed/pacing, and taper. "Global cycle" (multi-year, e.g., 18 months to 4 years) is the top-level framework for big goals like Boston Qualifiers or Olympic Trials — described via banana-split analogy (base=banana, strength/race-specific=ice cream/whipped cream, taper=cherry on top). (PRACTICE)(ANALOGY: banana split) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization structure generally, though the specific 21-day/banana-split framing may be additive)
- Explicit claim: an 18-months-out Boston Qualifier chaser needs roughly 3 full macro cycles — cycle 1 to adapt to increased training stress, cycle 2 to realize the stress is beneficial, cycle 3 to actually capitalize on the fitness gained. (PRACTICE)
- Claim/argument for cross-training race distances: distance runners (half/marathon focused) should periodically train and race shorter distances (5K/10K) even if marathon is the "A" goal, because it's the only way to engage the anaerobic power/capacity systems, and because 5K-pace discomfort is physiologically/psychologically similar practice for marathon-pace suffering ("the 5K and marathon are almost the same race" in terms of pacing discipline and end-race pain management). Explicitly ties to Austin's marathon-heavy culture as something the hosts push back against. (PRACTICE)(DISAGREEMENT against marathon-only training culture) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Aside: marathon distance (26.2 mi) has no inherent physiological basis — it's a historically arbitrary distance (Pheidippides legend + 1908 London Olympics royal-box finish placement), unlike the half marathon which sits cleanly within the "aerobic threshold" training zone. (SCIENCE/trivia, weakly evidenced — presented as established history) (POSSIBLE GAP — mildly interesting factoid, low priority)
- Response-regulated training / knowing when to back off: recommends tracking resting heart rate 3x/week upon waking as an objective readiness signal; gives personal example (45-48 bpm = well-recovered, 50-53 = normal hard-week stress, 55+ = red flag to back off, possible illness/overtraining). (PRACTICE)(SCIENCE — plausible physiological basis, not study-cited) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete self-monitoring protocol)
