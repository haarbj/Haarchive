## Episode #357: Seeing the Forest, not the Trees
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode. Host Chris McClung, owner/coach at Rogue Running (Austin, TX), 20+ years coaching experience, running since 2000.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Aerobic capacity/system development takes 10-15 years (sometimes longer) of consistent training to reach full potential, regardless of the age at which someone starts. (SCIENCE — asserted as established fact, no study or researcher named) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base training)
- "Aerobic baby / aerobic toddler" analogy: a new runner's aerobic system is developmentally young regardless of the runner's chronological age; throwing "adult" training loads at an aerobic toddler (high mileage, intense pace work too soon) is developmentally damaging, like expecting a 6-month-old to walk. (ANALOGY)
- Long-term planning should happen in 12-18 month windows (not week-to-week or single-cycle), deliberately blocking time for marathon-specific, half-specific, and 5K/10K-specific training within that window, plus planned volume increases and life events (vacations, deload windows). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — most sites cover periodization within a single training cycle; explicit multi-cycle 12-18-month "meta-planning" framework may be a distinct addition)
- Volume-increase rule of thumb: don't increase peak weekly mileage by more than ~20% from one training cycle's peak week to the next cycle's peak week (this is cycle-to-cycle, not week-to-week). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — mileage progression, but the cycle-over-cycle framing rather than week-over-week is a specific nuance worth checking against existing content)
- First marathon (or any first-time distance) result should prioritize smart, conservative execution and a strong finish over the finishing time itself — this builds "muscle memory"/belief in negative-split execution that carries forward to future races; total time will be dwarfed by later PRs anyway. (PRACTICE)
- "Chase one rabbit" principle (attributed to Eliud Kipchoge): pick a single most-important goal and treat all other races/goals as supplemental to it, because chasing two goals simultaneously often yields neither. (PRACTICE/quote)
- Coaching observation: most athletes underestimate their potential rather than overestimate it (author claims in 20+ years of coaching he can count on two hands the number who overestimated). A coach's key job is often to tell someone they can do more than they think. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — goal-calibration/confidence coaching angle)
- Failure and injury are framed as inherent, valuable parts of the process, not obstacles to it — injuries reveal weaknesses/mistakes and generate a personal "encyclopedia" of prehab/rehab knowledge that prevents recurrence. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — injury/recovery, but the explicit "injury as informative, not just setback" framing plus building a personal prehab catalog may be a useful specific angle)
- "No one workout matters" — body of work across an entire cycle (and prior cycles/years) matters far more than any single session; a coach values a long run that goes badly (dress rehearsal for mental toughness) similarly to one that goes perfectly. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training consistency, general)
- Puzzle analogy: a marathon performance is like completing a jigsaw puzzle made of many different-looking pieces (easy long runs, recovery runs, workouts, sleep, fueling, strength) — no single piece (e.g., "I must practice marathon pace") completes the picture alone. (ANALOGY)
- Fundamentals (easy-effort volume, gradual mileage growth, stress/rest balance, periodization within and across training cycles/distances) always beat social-media/influencer training fads. (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general coaching philosophy)

## Episode #358: Building the Confidence to Chase Big Goals with Listener Maeson

(PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific cross-domain-resilience mapping technique for building start-line confidence)

## Episode #359: Building Your Routine (aka Planting the Trees)
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Kipchoge quote: "Champions are not made when he wins races. It's the seconds, minutes, hours, weeks, and months when he prepares that matter." Used to argue that 20+ years of Kipchoge's year-round consistency (not any single race) explains his record. (quote)
- Explicit critique of US fitness culture for glorifying single, extreme, soreness/vomit-inducing efforts over unglamorous daily consistency (e.g., banking 2,000 easy miles a year), arguing the latter is what actually builds long-term potential. (DISAGREEMENT — direct pushback on "harder/more extreme = better" fitness culture norms) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit naming/critique of this cultural bias)
- Football "base offense/defense" analogy: a runner's routine functions like a team's foundational scheme — the identity from which all game-day variation and adjustment is built. (ANALOGY)
- "Home base" analogy: a routine is the place a runner returns to for comfort/orientation, from which they can confidently venture into race-specific variation. (ANALOGY)
- "Three pillar runs" framework: long run, medium-long run, and one quality/speed workout per week — these should exist in some form year-round, not just during race-specific cycles. Only one quality workout per week is recommended for most runners; a second quality day is deemed a rare, advanced tactic that mainly benefits athletes nearing the ceiling of their aerobic development. In lieu of a second quality day, the medium-long run is prioritized because aerobic-capacity building is argued to matter more than additional speed work. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training structure/periodization, though the explicit "pillar run" naming and the MLR > 2nd-quality-day priority ranking may be a specific, useful addition)
- Car-engine analogy: easy running builds "more cylinders" (aerobic capacity/engine size); fast running "fine-tunes" the existing engine. A big engine tuned well beats a small engine over-tuned. (ANALOGY)
- Pillar runs should be spaced at least one day apart (never back-to-back) because doing hard/long runs back-to-back without an easy day between is described as the single most common injury pattern the coach sees. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery spacing, general)
- Recovery runs are the "glue" of the routine — should be easy enough that the runner feels better afterward than before; typically 3-5 miles as a starting point. General recommendation to keep one full day off running per week, with rare exceptions for experienced/adapted runners doing 7 days. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery running)
- Post-workout/post-long-run fueling: eat a balanced carb/fat/protein meal within roughly 90 minutes of finishing a pillar run, framed as the optimal window for glycogen restoration ("carbo-loading actually happens most effectively post-workout") and for supplying "building blocks" for muscle repair; hydration should accompany this meal. (SCIENCE — reflects the general post-exercise glycogen-resynthesis window concept from sports science, but no specific researcher/study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — post-workout nutrition, general fueling; specific 90-minute framing may be a useful addition)
- Sleep described explicitly as "your number one recovery tool." (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery/sleep)
- "Other supplemental" routine elements (foam rolling, mobility work, meditation, sauna, cold plunge, yoga, easy cross-training) are personal and should feel enjoyable, not burdensome; if something feels like a burden it likely doesn't belong on that list. (PRACTICE)
- Habit-formation timelines given explicitly: roughly 3-6 months to fully embed a complete routine so it runs "without thinking," and roughly 4-6 weeks specifically for the body to adapt to a new strength-training schedule slot. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete habit-formation timeframes for routine-building, distinct from training-adaptation timelines)

## Episode #361: Principles on Strength for Runners
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung. References informal collaboration with Jonas at Infinite Fitness (Austin strength gym) but Jonas is not directly interviewed.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central principle: runners should generally never be sore from strength training. Soreness (beyond the first 2-3 weeks of a brand-new routine) signals too much weight or too many reps, not a sign of an effective session; the "no pain no gain" mentality is called an "American myth" that doesn't serve runners. (DISAGREEMENT — explicit pushback against bodybuilding-style "you must be sore to have worked hard enough" culture) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a fairly strong, specific, and possibly counter-to-mainstream claim worth flagging for the site)
- Specific rep/weight guidance: never more than 10 reps per set, typically 4-6-8 reps, at a weight manageable enough that a spotter shouldn't be needed; not training to failure or testing one-rep maxes. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — many general strength-for-runners resources recommend higher-rep muscular-endurance work; this specific low-rep/moderate-weight, never-to-failure prescription is a distinct and possibly conflicting stance worth checking against existing site content)
- Strength training is explicitly NOT the same category as "cross-training" (cross-training = low-intensity aerobic work); strength is its own "supplemental training" category that supports running but should never become primary. (DISAGREEMENT/clarification of terminology) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Timing for adding strength training: only add it during a base/priming phase or in the post-race recovery window before ramping into a new cycle — never mid-cycle while training for an imminent A-race. Full adaptation to a new strength routine takes roughly 4-8 weeks. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strength training for runners generally, though this explicit "only add during base phase" periodization-timing rule may be a specific addition)
- Five core movement categories for runner-specific strength work: push, pull, hinge, squat, and core (including rotational/anti-rotational, not just static planks); avoid "vanity muscle" isolation work like biceps curls. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — functional strength training)
- Strength training should be periodized in parallel with run training phases: priming phase (rebuild routine/reintroduce exercises), strength phase (raw strength/volume focus), race-specific phase (power/coordination/plyometrics, moving weight explosively), taper (deload to bodyweight/mobility only, possibly dropping strength entirely in the final week). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — an explicit strength-periodization framework mapped one-to-one onto standard run-training phases is a structured, potentially missing tool)
- Placement rule (echoing ep. 359): ideally do strength sessions on the same days as medium-long-run and quality-workout days (right after or later that day); if not possible, pick two other fixed days and stay consistent. Adjust exercise selection (e.g., de-emphasize leg work) when strength days fall close to a big long run late in a marathon cycle. (PRACTICE)
- "Consistency over brute force" — long-term view for strength training mirrors the long-term view for running; strength gains and injury resilience compound over years/decades of small, sustainable sessions rather than occasional maximal efforts. (PRACTICE)
- Advice on vetting a strength coach/personal trainer: get local recommendations from runners/running stores/clubs, and explicitly interview prospective trainers about their philosophy (supplemental-to-running vs. general fitness/bodybuilding-oriented) before committing. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #363: Nailing Race Day (+ Bonus Houston Tips)
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung, drawing on personal experience racing the Houston Marathon (2018 PR there).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Detailed marathon pacing template: start mile 1 about 30 seconds/mile slower than goal marathon pace (GMP); progressively descend ~10 sec/mile until reaching GMP by mile 4; hold GMP from roughly mile 4 to mile 21; then progressively pick up pace in two-mile increments (~10 sec/mile faster) through miles 22-25; empty the tank in the final mile. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — negative-split pacing is a known concept, but this specific numeric template is a concrete, actionable protocol that may be worth adding verbatim)
- Parallel half-marathon pacing template: mile 1 at goal half-pace +15-20 sec, mile 2 at +5-10 sec, on goal pace by mile 3, hold through mile 10, then progressive pickup of 5-10 sec/mile from mile 11 to the finish, aiming for the final mile to be the fastest. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific template)
- "If you miss early, miss slow" — if early miles come in slower than planned, don't panic or overcorrect; gradually work back to goal pace over the next few miles rather than surging. (PRACTICE)
- Car-accelerating-from-a-red-light analogy: it costs more energy to accelerate up to speed than to maintain that speed once already moving — explains why the first 3-4 race miles burn more relative energy than later miles at the same pace, justifying a deliberately slow start. (ANALOGY)
- "Shortening the race" mental technique: treat the first 3-4 miles as a warm-up, mentally reframing a marathon as a 22-mile race that starts once goal pace is reached. (ANALOGY/PRACTICE)
- Practical caution: adrenaline distorts perceived effort at the start of a race, making an actually-too-fast pace feel appropriately easy; suggested heuristic — "if you're not being passed by others early in the race, you're probably going out too fast," and "you should be passing others, not being passed, late in the race." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific perceptual/adrenaline-calibration heuristic)
- Explicit stance that official race pace groups/pacers are unreliable ("more often wrong than right" even at major races) and should never be blindly followed — usable only as a loose positional reference near the start. (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back against the common practice of trusting official race pacers) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Weather-adjustment rules of thumb: add ~10 seconds/mile to goal pace for every 5°F the start-line temperature is above 55°F (i.e., 60°F=+10s, 65°F=+20s, 70°F=+30s, etc.); for wind, add ~2 seconds/mile for every mph of headwind above 10 mph (e.g., a 20mph headwind ≈ +20 sec/mile). (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — presented as coach-derived rules of thumb from observation, not attributed to a specific study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — heat's effect on pace is generally known territory, but these specific quantified adjustment formulas for both heat and wind may be a useful, possibly-missing concrete tool)
- Race-day nutrition/hydration protocol: never introduce anything new on race day; start fueling ~45-60 minutes in, then repeat every 30-45 minutes (matched to whatever interval was practiced in training) regardless of how good the runner feels early on, because fueling maintains glycogen stores for later, not immediate need; for marathons, skip the first water stop, then take fluids at every subsequent stop (alternating water/electrolyte drink), switching to "drink to thirst" in the final ~5 miles; half-marathoners (under ~2 hours) can generally rely on thirst cues throughout. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general race-day fueling, though the specific water-stop-skipping/alternating protocol is a concrete detail that may be missing)
- Mental toolkit: three distinct categories of mantras mapped to race segments — a "start-line" calm mantra, a "rhythm" mantra for the early/middle miles, and a "fight" mantra for the final miles; recommends preparing 2-3 options in each category in advance. Visualization of the entire race (start-line logistics, pacing execution, nutrition stops, difficult moments, finish) is framed as producing a "neurological dress rehearsal" effect similar to actually performing the action. Additional specific tools: the "going fishing" technique (mentally picking off runners ahead one at a time late in a race so you are the one passing, not being passed); counting (e.g., 1 to 60 repeatedly) as a neutral mental-distraction technique; and pre-memorizing course landmarks (e.g., counting streetlights on the final straightaway, done via Google Street View recon the night before) to structure a late-race mental push. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general race-day mental-toughness/mantra content is likely covered by sports psychology material, but the three-tiered mantra structure and the specific "fishing"/landmark-counting techniques are concrete, possibly-missing tactics)
- Uses quotes from Jimmy Carter (the future as "a gift" that is nonetheless "cloaked in mystery") to frame how to approach race-day uncertainty by focusing on what's controllable rather than the unknown outcome. (ANALOGY/quote)
- Uses a Mike Tyson quote ("everybody has a plan until they get punched in the mouth" / even a good plan will face "the wrath" at some point) to argue every race, even a well-executed one, will include an inevitable difficult patch, and mental preparation should anticipate this rather than expect a flawless race. (ANALOGY/quote)

## Episode #364: Find Your Inner Superhero
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung, adapted from a pre-race talk he gave to the Rogue Renegades virtual group in Columbus.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Tom Hanks quote: "A hero is somebody who voluntarily walks into the unknown" — used to reframe the act of stepping onto a race start line (regardless of outcome) as itself a heroic act, since the definition centers on the act of facing the unknown, not on results. (ANALOGY/quote) (POSSIBLE GAP — reframing race-day uncertainty explicitly through a "heroism" lens is a distinctive sports-psychology angle)
- Cites psychologists Philip Zimbardo and Zeno Franco and their paper "The Banality of Heroism," which argues heroism should be conceived as a trainable, universal human attribute rather than a rare trait limited to an elite few, making it feel achievable to ordinary people ("perhaps inspiring more to answer the call"). (SCIENCE — named researchers and a real academic paper/book, a genuine citable source unlike most other claims in this batch) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit academic grounding for a self-concept/confidence-building technique)
- Four proposed characteristics for self-identifying as a "hero" applicable to runners: (1) heroes come in all shapes and sizes, not just physical elites; (2) genuine heroes typically don't call themselves heroes; (3) heroes run toward the challenge/unknown rather than away from it; (4) every hero has an "origin story" from humble beginnings worth reflecting on. (PRACTICE/framework) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Five practices for embodying this mindset around a race or training cycle: (1) stay calm at the start line — introduces a third mantra category ("start-line mantra") beyond the rhythm/fight mantras from ep. 363; (2) identify and lean into a personal "superpower" (e.g., consistency, calm, grit, positivity, being a good teammate) without dwelling on personal weaknesses/"kryptonite"; (3) expect to get "knocked down" during the race/cycle and plan to get back up rather than expecting a flawless experience; (4) identify a "sidekick" — a real or imagined person mentally channeled for support at specific race moments (e.g., at each timing mat) — as a reminder that racing needn't be experienced as solitary; (5) deliberately "engage the heroic imagination" via visualization, potentially including choosing a personal superhero name/outfit as a psychological cue. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a distinct, structured psychological framework combining identity, imagined support figures, and visualization)
- Brene Brown quotes used to support the framework: "you can choose courage or you can choose comfort, but you cannot choose both, not at the same time," and courage is defined as "not the absence of fear, but rather the ability to face it." (ANALOGY/quote) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports-psychology content on courage/fear likely exists already; these specific quotes may or may not already be used)
- Theodore Roosevelt's "Man in the Arena" quote is used to reframe falling short of a goal (erring, coming up short "again and again") as still heroic provided the athlete dared greatly, contrasted with never having tried at all. (ANALOGY/quote)
