## Episode #11: Why Do You Run?
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris (Rogue Running head coach) and Steve Sisson (longtime Rogue coach, former post-collegiate elite coach) discuss their own mental-training framework. Part of a multi-episode mental training series (references episode 6 as the framework intro).

(PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the site's sports-psych content may cover goal-setting generically but this specific "battle plan" structure with defined vocabulary is a distinct coaching framework worth considering.)
- Steve draws a three-way distinction: volition (the decision/choice), intention (converting decision into action), will (consistent application of the decision over time). (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY)
- Motivation is split into extrinsic (rewards, praise, scholarships, "the carrot and the stick") vs intrinsic (love of the process, sense of accomplishment as its own reward, testing personal limits, connection to nature). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology, but the specific intrinsic/extrinsic framing tied to athlete retention is a useful angle.)

(ANALOGY)

## Episode #110: The Science of Recovery with Christie Aschwanden and Alex Hutchinson
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Christie Aschwanden — science journalist (FiveThirtyEight), author of *Good to Go: What the Athlete in All of Us Can Learn from the Strange Science of Recovery*; former competitive runner/cyclist/cross-country skier. Alex Hutchinson — Outside Magazine's "Sweat Science" columnist, author of *Endure*, PhD physicist by training, recurring guest.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Recovery science is inherently hard to study because the human body is highly adaptable/noisy, sample sizes in sports science are small, and "recovery" itself has no single agreed-upon metric — different measures give different answers. (SCIENCE — Aschwanden, drawing on her own beer-and-recovery study.)
- Publication bias: many null-result studies never get published or even submitted, skewing the visible evidence toward positive findings for a given modality. (SCIENCE — general methodology point, not tied to a specific citation.)
- Alex's evolved approach to evaluating single studies: don't trust any isolated small study — look for convergence across multiple studies/labs before updating belief; described science as "a process of uncertainty reduction," not a source of final answers. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE) — useful media-literacy framing for how the site should treat cited studies generally.
- A methodology-reform group (referenced as "STORC" — likely SIPS/Society for Transparency, Openness in Sports and Exercise Science Research) is pursuing pre-registration and multi-lab collaborative studies to fix small-sample-size problems in sports science. (SCIENCE — named group referenced, details fuzzy/mis-transcribed.) (POSSIBLE GAP — meta-commentary on how to read sports science literature could be a useful "how to evaluate research" sidebar.)
- Core reframe: recovery's actual goal is "rest and relaxation," and psychological/emotional stress (work deadlines, relationship stress, busy "rest days" full of errands) taxes the body physically just like training stress does and can prevent true recovery. (SCIENCE — Aschwanden's synthesis of interviewed researchers.) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "rest days aren't rest if you're stressed doing errands" framing is a genuinely underused angle vs. generic recovery advice.)
- Most popular recovery modalities (massage, ice baths, cryotherapy, foam rolling, infrared sauna) lack strong scientific support for direct physiological recovery benefit — but if they produce genuine rest/relaxation/placebo-driven confidence, that is a legitimate benefit in itself, not something to dismiss. (SCIENCE) (DISAGREEMENT with any content implying strict evidence-only recovery guidance — book explicitly argues placebo-driven routines still "work" if defined by subjective outcome.)
- Best subjective indicator of recovery status found in the research: simply asking "how do you feel?" — outperforms wearables/heart rate data according to Aschwanden's reporting. (SCIENCE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC in general terms, but worth citing specifically against overreliance on recovery-tracking gadgets.)
- Float tanks (sensory deprivation/floatation therapy) singled out by Aschwanden, alongside sleep, as one of the few recovery modalities with genuinely strong subjective benefit — described as "forced meditation." (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — float tanks are a fairly niche/underrepresented recovery modality that may not be on the site.)
- Sleep: chronically sleep-deprived people lose the ability to perceive their own impairment — self-reports of "I only need 5 hours" are usually wrong; those people are still cognitively/physically impaired, just unaware. Comparisons made to alcohol-level impairment at ~5 hours of sleep. (SCIENCE — researcher interviewed but not named in transcript.) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sleep is probably covered, but the "impairment awareness gap" specific claim may be a useful addition.)
- Naps vs. continuous sleep: evidence is thin/poorly controlled; Alex's view is continuous nighttime sleep is likely better when achievable, but napping helps because most people carry a chronic sleep debt, so any extra sleep — nap or otherwise — helps. (SCIENCE, appropriately hedged by both guests.)
- **Hydration — major discussion, contains explicit DISAGREEMENT:** Aschwanden argues drinking-to-thirst is well-supported even in ultra-endurance events (cites an "Ultra Science Research" group led by Martin Hoffman with formal guidelines endorsing drink-to-thirst); argues the sports-drink/hydration industry has oversold dehydration danger and conflated heat illness with dehydration — she could not find a documented case of a runner dying purely from dehydration in a sporting event, and cites a large military study where only ~20% of heatstroke cases involved dehydration. (SCIENCE, researcher and study named — moderately strong sourcing.) (DISAGREEMENT — directly conflicts with traditional "stay ahead of your hydration" coaching advice that host Chris admits he was trained to give and still gives athletes, especially in Texas heat; also somewhat in tension with Ep. 114's coach Marilyn not addressing hydration planning explicitly.)
- Aquaporins: the body has fluid-regulating channels in the kidneys that adapt to typical hydration levels; chronically over-hydrating (e.g., "hydrate the day before a marathon") may reduce the body's readiness to conserve fluid when it's actually needed, per researchers Aschwanden interviewed. (SCIENCE — plausible physiological mechanism cited, not a landmark/named study.) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific claim about pre-race over-hydration being counterproductive is a fairly novel/counterintuitive angle.)
- Functional dehydration: losing 1-2% body mass during exercise may not represent true cellular dehydration because glycogen is stored bound to water and that water becomes available as fuel is burned. (SCIENCE — Hutchinson explaining accepted exercise physiology.) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if general fueling/hydration content exists, but this specific mechanism might be missing.)
- Counter-caution from Hutchinson: performance-relevant dehydration risk is real and distinct from heat-stroke risk; a meta-analysis (co-authored by Martin Hoffman, referenced as newly published) comparing drink-to-thirst vs. pre-planned drinking schedules in events up to 2 hours found no significant performance difference, though drink-to-thirst athletes drank about half as much. (SCIENCE, study and author named.)
- Case study/anecdote: Haile Gebrselassie lost 10% body weight during a marathon world record using an aggressive planned hydration/fueling schedule (not drink-to-thirst) — but he had previously bonked using a casual drink-to-thirst approach in his first marathon loss to Tergat/Kanuchi. Used by both guests as an example of how confounded the "does hydration strategy actually cause performance outcomes" question is. (SCIENCE, named athlete example, appropriately caveated as inconclusive.)
- Practical guidance from Hutchinson: drink-to-thirst only works if (a) you're well-practiced at reading your own thirst signals and (b) fluids are actually available when you're thirsty — in a race with aid stations every 5K, "pure" drink-to-thirst isn't really achievable, so some pre-planning is still recommended for competitive racing even if the underlying physiological argument favors thirst-based drinking. (PRACTICE)
- Personal anecdotes from both journalists: neither carries water on runs under ~90 minutes even in serious training; Hutchinson explicitly cautions that his own hydration writing is shaped by living in cold-climate Toronto and shouldn't be extrapolated to hot climates like Texas. (PRACTICE, self-caveated as anecdotal/non-generalizable.)
- Massage: no strong evidence of physiological recovery benefit per the book's research, but both guests still get massages regularly and consider it close to "the definition of recovery" (enforced stillness + relaxation + psychological benefit). (SCIENCE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC.)
- Active recovery / "movement equals blood flow equals healing" — host's personal practice of light movement post-marathon and post-hard-run; Aschwanden agrees exercise is literally the simplest way to increase blood flow, no gadget needed. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC.)
- Individual variability in recovery capacity is likely genetically influenced (example cited: ultrarunner Camille Herron's unusually fast recovery); the single most important recovery "skill" an athlete can develop, per Aschwanden, is learning to read and trust their own body's signals — developed only through experience plus close attention, not from data alone. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE)

## Episode #114: Get Started, Running for Beginners
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Marilyn Faulkner — Rogue Running coach for 8+ years, coaches the Couch-to-5K/10K beginner program and half/full marathon programs; also specializes in coaching pregnant/postpartum runners; personal experience running through pregnancies including two unmedicated births.

**Concepts & claims:**

- Common beginner mental block ("I'm not a runner") should be treated as a dismissible story, not a fact — Faulkner uses a literal physical gesture (push the pile of excuses aside with your hand) as a coaching technique to help clients let go of self-limiting narratives before starting. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY)
- Direct pushback on "running is bad for your knees": claims exercise/mechanical load makes joints, bones, tendons, ligaments, and muscles stronger over time; inactivity is worse for joint health than running-related "pounding." (SCIENCE — asserted by host as established science, no study/researcher named in the episode.) (DISAGREEMENT with the common "running destroys your knees" belief.) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if injury-prevention content exists.)
- New runners will likely feel some soreness/twinging in the first ~4 weeks as a normal response to novel structural loading — not a sign to quit. (PRACTICE)
- "Motion is lotion" — Faulkner's phrase for the idea that easy movement helps clear soreness better than complete rest; paired with the host's own mantra "movement equals blood flow equals healing." Recommendation: when sore, still go out, just start at an easier effort/walk. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY)
- Redefining "running" broadly: coach explicitly does not care about the walk/run distinction — if forward movement is happening, it counts; the program's stated goal is "positive forward progression" across three domains simultaneously — physical, mental, and spiritual/emotional. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY)
- Conversational pace test for beginners: you should be able to hold a normal conversation while running; open-mouth breathing at 80–85% max effort is a physical cue you're going too hard and should dial back. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — conversational pace / talk test is common but worth confirming site coverage.)
- Practical beginner run-walk protocol: don't prescribe a fixed run/walk interval upfront (e.g., "run 5, walk 2") — instead have the athlete run until uncomfortable, walk until recovered, repeat, log the actual times, then average those numbers into a personalized starting interval if structure is desired. - Recommended beginner frequency: minimum 3 running days/week (4 if lifestyle allows), plus cross-training/restorative activity (yoga, walking, stretching) on off days; Rogue's own beginner program starts long runs at 1–2 miles. (PRACTICE)
- Recommended starting session length for absolute beginners: 20 minutes (10 out/10 back) as day one baseline. (PRACTICE)
- "Something is always better than nothing" — if the planned 2 miles or 30 minutes isn't feasible on a given day, doing a reduced version (1 mile / 20 minutes) still delivers a training stimulus and preserves consistency/habit; better than skipping entirely. (PRACTICE)
- Consistency, not pace, is described repeatedly by both host and guest as the single most important variable for improvement at any level, beginner through veteran. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — likely well covered on the site already as a general principle, though the specific beginner framing may add value.)

No fixed distance or precise pace required. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site covers strides, but the specific "relaxed thirds" cueing technique may be a useful addition.)

(PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — running economy is probably covered, though the "introduce speed early for the psychological win" framing is a distinct angle.)

## Episode #115: Workout Management with James Dodds
**Guest(s) and credentials:** James Dodds — Rogue Running coach, recurring podcast guest (previously on episodes 8, 37, 107); coaches at Rogue and does personal training/CrossFit-adjacent work.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Direct answer to a listener question ("if easy days are easy, when do you get faster?"): adaptation/fitness gains occur during recovery/rest, not during the hard workout itself — the workout provides the stress stimulus, but the body improves while healing. Analogized to weightlifting: you don't max out your bench daily; you build volume at submax loads so that periodic max tests show improvement. (SCIENCE — physiology 101 framing, not tied to a specific citation, but standard supercompensation concept.) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training stress/adaptation cycle is presumably already well covered.)
- Jack Daniels' three core workout variables: frequency (how often), volume (how much), intensity (how fast/hard). Layered against this, Dodds independently learned a "Gray Institute" framework of ~10-12 "observational essentials" (direction, plane of movement, environment, weight, duration, etc.) for thinking about biomechanical variables within a single workout. (SCIENCE — named source, Jack Daniels' published training system.) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site already covers Daniels' training principles.)
- Key coaching insight: the same nominal workout (e.g., "4x800") can be tuned toward completely different training purposes by adjusting just one variable — recovery duration (standing vs. jogging/floating recovery), interval distance, total volume, or pace. Given as concrete example: shortening recovery from 2 min to 1 min turns the same workout into a harder session without changing the "headline" structure. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this "workout construction levers" framework, explained with this level of granularity, may be a genuinely useful addition beyond generic "vary your training" advice.)
- Claim: "there's no such thing as a perfect workout" — a coach could substitute 10 different workout structures on a given day and achieve the same training purpose; this is framed as both humbling for coaches and reassuring for athletes (imperfect execution of a single session doesn't ruin the training effect). (PRACTICE)
- Distinguishing when pace matters more than recovery vs. vice versa: if a workout's purpose is developing raw speed, hitting exact pace matters more and recovery is less critical; if the purpose is aerobic strength/endurance, recovery duration (getting back to work quickly) matters more than nailing exact pace. Athletes are encouraged to ask their coach (or themselves) which variable is the priority for a given session. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific pace-vs-recovery prioritization heuristic is a useful, somewhat novel coaching tool.)
- Concrete example: taper-week workout of "3x1 mile at goal race pace" the Wednesday before a Sunday race is intentionally designed with unlimited recovery between reps — the sole purpose is grooving pace/rhythm ("making the pace feel sticky"), not physiological benefit; you can only hurt yourself, not help yourself, that close to race day. (PRACTICE)
- Contrasting personal race-week philosophy from Dodds: rather than race-pace repeats close to race day (which can feel harder than expected during taper due to the body adjusting, causing athlete anxiety), he prefers short, easy, high-recovery intervals (e.g., 10x400m building from half-marathon to 10K to 5K pace with long rest) purely to keep legs "moving" without psychologically "freaking out" an athlete pre-race. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — Dodds explicitly frames this as differing from the host's/Rogue's more common race-pace-based taper workout; presented as two legitimate but different taper philosophies, not a right/wrong.)
- Extended example from *Running with the Buffaloes* (referenced from the host's separate interview with Adam Goucher, previewed here): Coach Mark Wetmore had his team run the same "mile repeats" workout multiple times across a season, but shifted the pace (from near-half-marathon pace early season to near-threshold pace late season) and recovery duration to change the training purpose from aerobic-strength-building early to peak-sharpening late — an explicit periodization example. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization broadly, but this specific "same nominal workout, evolving purpose across a season" illustration adds texture.)
- Workout-routine/ritual concept: minimizing decision fatigue and cognitive load around big workouts by establishing consistent pre-workout routines (same dinner, same bedtime, laying out gear, consistent warmup/drill sequence) — explicitly compared to golfers' and baseball players' pre-performance rituals, and framed as "priming the brain" for performance. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC in general sports-psych pre-performance routine terms, though this running-specific version could be useful.)
- "Enclosed cognition" concept (referencing Kara Goucher's book *Strong*): wearing a specific race uniform/kit or piece of jewelry functions as psychological "armor" signaling to the brain that it's competition time — example given of Adam Goucher wearing a necklace of beads representing defeated rivals only on race day. (SCIENCE/ANALOGY — term attributed to Goucher's book, not independently verified/sourced by the hosts; likely refers to "embodied cognition.") (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific psychological priming mechanism/vocabulary term may not be covered elsewhere on the site.)
- Recommended post-workout routine components: proper easy-paced cooldown, refueling within roughly 90 minutes, and deliberately "laying down" (even if via stretching with no proven physiological benefit) to let the central nervous system and heart rate fully return to baseline rather than rushing straight to the next task. (PRACTICE)
- Phil Maffetone concept cited: during warmup, the heart rate should progress gradually and continuously through every zone up to workout intensity rather than jumping abruptly — and the same gradual step-down should happen in the cooldown; skipping zones (going out too hard, or stopping abruptly post-workout) is suboptimal for both performance and recovery signaling. (SCIENCE — named source, Maffetone's heart-rate methodology, not independently verified against literature in the episode.) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if heart-rate training/Maffetone method is covered, but the specific "hit every zone on the way up and down" framing might be additive.)
- Workout-management decision tree for when things go wrong mid-workout: (1) Is there injury risk? If yes, stop — "live to fight another day." (2) What's the purpose of this workout? If it's an aerobic-strength/endurance-building session, grind through at reduced pace/effort even if precise paces are missed; if it's a precision speed-oriented session, it may be better to stop once form/pace control degrades rather than reinforce sloppy mechanics. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this explicit, actionable decision framework for "should I quit this workout" is more concrete than typical general perseverance advice and could be a valuable standalone addition.)
- Framing bad/failed workouts as more valuable in some cases than perfect ones — especially long-run workouts closer to race day, because struggling through adversity mid-workout builds the mental resilience needed for the inevitable hard patch in a race; a "perfect" workout can even breed overconfidence and skip that mental rehearsal. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — mental toughness generally, but this specific reframe of failed workouts as valuable training may be a distinct angle worth flagging.)
- Explicit guidance against being a "slave to your watch" during interval reps: check the watch only at the start/stop of a rep, not during it; run by feel/rhythm instead, especially for half-marathon/marathon-distance efficiency work, because obsessively checking instant pace mid-rep causes over/under-correction. Attributed quote: "the watch isn't running the race for you, your legs are." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site already discusses running by feel vs. GPS data, though this specific mid-rep guidance is more granular.)
- "When in doubt, lean conservative" — cited as attributable to Bill Bowerman (or a commonly repeated coaching maxim); the stated top reason athletes miss goals isn't underperformance but failing to reach the start line healthy, reinforcing conservative workout progression. (PRACTICE)
