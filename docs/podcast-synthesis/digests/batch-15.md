## Episode #251: Building and Executing Long Run Workouts
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode hosted by Chris McClung (founder/head coach, Rogue Running, Austin TX; personal marathon PR 2:45, has run 20+ marathons; coaches an in-person group and two virtual podcast-based training groups).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Negative-split marathon strategy is achievable even for first-timers, provided the goal pace is conservative enough and the athlete has a specific plan for executing it (not just the right target). Host cites his own 15-minute negative split in his first marathon (Chicago 2001). (PRACTICE)
- "Bank energy, not time" — start conservatively even if it means slower opening kilometers than feels natural; the host diagnosed a listener's fade in the final 8K of a marathon as due to too-fast early kilometers despite an already-conservative target. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race pacing strategy)
- Long-run "race simulator" workouts: 3–4 per training cycle where athletes rehearse gear, nutrition/hydration protocol, pre-race routine (dinner, sleep, breakfast), and course terrain (flat/hilly/downhill) as if it were race day itself. Framed as the primary place to experiment and "make mistakes" before race day. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — full-dress race-day rehearsal as a structured, recurring workout category, distinct from generic "practice your fueling" advice)
- "Anything workouts": these long-run workouts should be hard enough that the athlete genuinely doesn't know if they can complete it — "if that's not a question in your head, then the workout isn't tough enough" — but not so hard they bury themselves. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- Workouts across a training cycle should build progressively, with the hardest/most intense long-run workout scheduled right before the taper, functioning as a physiological and psychological "signal" to the body that it's time to peak. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/tapering)
- Claim: running at pure marathon goal pace (MGP) sits in a "physiological no man's land" — too fast to build aerobic capacity, not fast enough to build aerobic strength/threshold — so long runs done entirely at MGP are largely wasted miles from a physiological standpoint (though useful for pacing familiarity in small doses). (SCIENCE — asserted as physiological fact, no specific study or researcher cited) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Rule of thumb: never exceed ~10 miles of pure marathon-goal-pace running within one long run (6 miles for half-marathon pace); no more than ~75% of a long-run workout should involve "work" of any kind, no more than ~50% at goal race pace, and continuous work segments should be capped around 5 miles (marathon) / 3 miles (half) to preserve recovery within the workout. (PRACTICE)
- Don't add long-run distance and workout intensity in the same session — build to a new distance first, then add pace work at that distance in a later cycle. Combining both "is a recipe for injury." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — progressive overload, but the specific rule of sequencing distance-then-intensity is a concrete, actionable nugget)
- Space these hard long-run workouts at least two weeks apart (no more than one every three weeks). (PRACTICE)
- Three named categories of long-run workouts: (1) "pure race pace" segments with easy recovery jogging between (entry-level, used early in a cycle); (2) "in-and-out" workouts alternating faster-than-race-pace segments with race pace to build the ability to recover into goal pace under fatigue — e.g., the "Tiger Run" (90 sec at 10K effort / 5 min at marathon effort), inspired by a Bill Squires quote: "the long run puts the tiger in the cat"; (3) "progressive finish" workouts (the "McClung Special") that start easy, move through goal pace, and finish faster than race pace to train a strong close. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — named, reusable workout templates with explicit physiological rationale)
- These workouts are deliberately allowed to go imperfectly — coach says he actually prefers when they don't go perfectly, since dealing with unexpected fatigue/adversity in training builds the mental skill needed for race-day surprises; success is measured by lessons learned, not by hitting every prescribed split. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #253: Deciding What's Next for Your Training
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Heat-adjustment pacing rule of thumb: add 10 seconds/mile to goal pace for every 5°F the start-line temperature is above 55°F (e.g., 60°F → +10 sec/mile, 65°F → +20 sec/mile, 70°F → +30 sec/mile), with the adjustment held through roughly mile 21 before reassessing. (SCIENCE — presented as a reliable rule of thumb, not attributed to a specific study) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete, quantified heat-pacing adjustment formula)
- Before planning the next training cycle, fully process the prior one: explicitly grieve a missed goal or celebrate a win (roughly a 3-day emotional window, not a hard rule), and separately extract training/racing lessons without overanalyzing. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology, though the explicit "digest before you plan" step and rough 3-day guideline are a specific practice)
- Make only incremental changes between cycles — pick at most one or two variables to change, never more; overreacting to either a bad race (overhauling everything) or a good race (stacking on extra changes) is described as a common failure pattern leading to a harder subsequent season. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — implicitly pushes back against the instinct to make big changes after either success or failure)
- Recommends against "revenge racing" — signing up for a new goal race soon after a disappointing one to chase redemption — because it doesn't allow proper recovery from the prior peak or proper rebuilding, and tends to produce another bad outcome. General guidance: at least 5–6 months between peak marathon/half-marathon efforts; you can only meaningfully peak 2–3 times per year. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/peaking, but the specific "5–6 months, 2–3 peaks/year" figures and the "redemption race" framing are concrete and possibly missing)
- Seven variables to consider tweaking between training cycles (pick 1, max 2 per cycle): (1) stay the course — repeating the same volume/intensity is still additive to aerobic fitness, not stagnation, especially if coming off an injury-prone or over-the-limit cycle, or facing outside life stressors; (2) change speed/distance focus — almost universally recommended (e.g., after a marathon cycle, do a half-marathon or speed cycle next) since cross-training distances improves the target distance ("the faster you can run a 5K, the faster you can ultimately run a marathon"); (3) add volume — no more than ~20% increase in peak weekly mileage from one cycle to the next, appropriate for "aerobic toddlers" or marathoners not consistently running 40+ mpw / 2,000+ mi/year; (4) add intensity — should generally not be combined with a volume increase in the same cycle; (5) reestablish base — a deliberate no-goal cycle focused on consistent easy mileage to rebuild health/injury resilience and love of running; (6) add a strength program — takes an estimated 3 months to establish sustainably, should be the only variable changed if added; (7) double down on recovery — pick only 2–3 sustainable recovery modalities rather than trying to overhaul everything at once. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a specific, named 7-variable framework for cycle-to-cycle progression decisions that a site might not have organized this way)

## Episode #256: The Running Rogue Way
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode; McClung reads a 12-principle coaching philosophy document he originally wrote in 2016 for his in-person "Rogue Morning Show" training group.

**Concepts & claims:**
Training principles:
- Consistency is the #1 success metric in distance running; every run (easy or hard, long or short) matters roughly equally over the long term, since quality workouts and recovery runs are mutually dependent. Quotes Eliud Kipchoge: "champions are not made on race day. They are made in the months, weeks, days, hours, minutes, and seconds they have spent preparing." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — consistency/training philosophy, but the Kipchoge quote is a citable, near-verbatim line worth preserving)
- "Miles matter" — quotes Arthur Lydiard: "you don't make yourself great doing anaerobic training. You make yourself great doing aerobic training," building capillary density, mitochondria, blood oxygen-carrying efficiency, and lung capacity through easy-paced volume. Injury is attributed to running too fast on volume days, not to volume itself. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — Lydiard named) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base development)
- "Go slow to go fast" — the counterintuitive core principle that most weekly mileage should be run at easy conversational pace; explicit analogy to strength training (high reps/low weight most of the time, occasional max effort) to explain why the same logic should apply to running even though people resist it. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy-running/aerobic-base principle, though the strength-training analogy is a distinct teaching device)
- "Respect the purpose of each day and training phase" — periodization concept applied within a week, within a cycle, and across cycles (mixing distance focuses over a year, not repeating the same distance cycle after cycle). (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization)
- "Recovery is as important as the running itself" — beyond sleep and easy recovery running (the "top two" modalities), additional recovery "extras" (foam rolling, ankle mobility work, massage, etc.) are highly individual and best discovered through trial and error; practical tip to multitask recovery modalities into daily life (e.g., foam rolling while watching TV, ankle mobility during work calls) so they don't compete for time. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery, though the "recovery modalities are individual, find yours via experimentation, and stack them onto existing daily activities" framing is a specific practical nugget)
- "Find strength and accountability in the team" — "running is only a solo sport if you let it be"; explicitly encourages finding inspiration in everyday training peers/teammates rather than only elite athletes, since relatable stories of ordinary people balancing work/family with training are argued to be more motivating. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — team/community-based motivation and accountability as a distinct psychological lever, somewhat different from typical individual-focused sports psychology content)

Racing principles:
- "Trust in your training" — anecdote about elite marathoner Noah Droddy (per host, 9th-fastest American marathoner ever, at the time unsponsored and working full-time) being advised by Kara Goucher to bring his training log to the New York City Marathon to rebuild confidence by reviewing concrete evidence of completed work. (PRACTICE)
- "Execute the plan" — quotes Mike Tyson: "everybody has a plan until they get punched in the mouth"; cites real coaching examples of athletes skipping a planned mid-race gel because they "felt good," costing them fuel later in the race. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race execution/fueling discipline)
- "Race without fear of failure" — some fear is healthy (signals the goal is big enough); failure is reframed as only possible if you quit or fail to learn. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology)
- "Start slow, finish strong" — negative-split strategy for anything longer than a 5K; memorable framing: "if you're not getting passed at the start of a race, you're doing something wrong. And if you're not passing people at the end of the race, you're doing something wrong." (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — pacing strategy)
- "Leave it all on the course" — concrete mental technique: when at the edge of tolerable fatigue late in a race, count to 30 seconds and push for that chunk, then repeat, to find "a little tiny gear" incrementally rather than trying to sustain effort indefinitely. Personal anecdotes: walking the final 4.5 miles of the 2016 Boston Marathon due to a stress fracture (framed as "leaving it all on the course" even though the pace was a walk) versus gutting out 7:30–7:45 miles during a leg shutdown in a later Boston race. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the concrete 30-second counting/chunking technique for late-race fatigue is a specific, teachable mental tool)
- "Do it for you and for the team" — racing performance is framed as inspiration that ripples through a training group/community, reinforcing motivation to give full effort. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #257: Defining Junk Miles (+NYC and Shalane)
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode; substantial intro segment recapping the 2021 NYC Marathon results and Shalane Flanagan's "Project Eclipse" (six marathons in six weeks) is pure recap/banter and was discarded per instructions.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Reframes "junk miles" as "unproductive" (or "counterproductive") miles, rejecting the term itself because it implies miles are unsalvageable. Defines two root causes: (1) miles you can't physically recover from, and (2) miles that run counter to the specific purpose of that training day. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a distinct, well-articulated taxonomy/reframing of a commonly-misused term that a site might want to address explicitly)
- Explicitly disagrees with coaches who label slower-paced running as "junk miles" — argues miles can't be "too slow" outside of a specific workout context. (DISAGREEMENT)
- Macro-level unproductive miles: building weekly volume by more than ~20% from one training cycle's peak to the next risks miles the body can't recover from (this 20% figure repeats from episode 253/256). Also describes a personal "aha moment": the host plateaued and got injured trying to force 75–80 mi/week around his life constraints, but backed off to 65–70 mi/week and set a marathon PR in 2018 — illustrating that an individual's recoverable volume ceiling is set by life constraints (sleep, stress, family/work), not just training itself, even when all other training variables (pacing, workout structure) are correct. (PRACTICE)
- Micro-level unproductive miles: examples include running a normally-easy long run at the "wrong" time of day (e.g., shifting a 10-mile easy run from morning to late afternoon before a hard workout the next morning) — the same mileage becomes unproductive purely because there isn't enough recovery window before the next quality session; or over-extending a cooldown after a very hard quality workout just to hit a volume target, which can itself impair recovery. (PRACTICE)
- Defines the "purpose" of specific run types and what would make miles unproductive within them: medium-long/long runs (purpose = build aerobic capacity via easy pace; going too fast shifts you out of the aerobic-capacity-building zone; going too slow — below roughly 115 bpm — technically shifts you into a pure recovery zone instead) (SCIENCE — specific heart-rate threshold asserted without a named study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific 115 bpm cutoff is a concrete, actionable number); recovery days (purpose = active rest/blood flow/healing, not aerobic capacity building; recommends running at least 2 minutes slower than marathon pace or 2.5 minutes slower than half-marathon pace); quality/speed days (purpose = train the specific aerobic system the workout targets; running faster OR slower than prescribed effort both "waste" the day by training the wrong system). Cites guest Marilyn Faulkner's phrase "motion is lotion" for recovery running. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- Follow-up nuance (responding to a listener named Jonas): no single pace or effort is universally "useless" — productivity always depends on the athlete's broader context and goals. Also clarifies that improvising a different-but-still-purpose-matching workout (e.g., substituting a group track session that trains the same energy system as a prescribed workout) is fine and not "junk miles" — it's only counterproductive when a substituted session is tangential to or conflicts with the training block's actual purpose (example given: doing an all-out 5K speed workout three weeks before a marathon may build some fitness but risks derailing marathon-specific preparation). (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — nuances/softens the earlier hard-line framing)

## Episode #258: The Pain and Triumph of Running
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, first in a planned mini-series on emotions in running, inspired by a Taylor Swift documentary ("Folklore: The Long Pond Studio Sessions").

**Concepts & claims:**
- Frames pain in competitive running as "a privilege" — voluntarily chosen — explicitly distinguished from involuntary suffering/pain outside one's control. (PRACTICE)
- Taxonomy of pain types experienced in racing, illustrated with the host's own race history: pain that forces you to stop (2016 Boston Marathon stress fracture requiring a walk of the final ~4.5 miles; a 2005 marathon DNF at mile 18 from full physical/energetic shutdown); low-grade "controllable" pain the athlete has "dominion" over (2018 Houston Marathon PR, struggling from mile 16 but pushing through); overwhelming pain with no control over its performance impact (glute/hamstring lockup in the final 5 miles of the 2018 CIM Marathon; a full-body fatigue "meltdown" after mile 18 of a later Boston Marathon); emotional pain/grief accompanying physical pain; and the pain of facing something genuinely new (first-time distances or paces). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a granular, named taxonomy of pain types specific to endurance racing, going beyond generic "mental toughness" advice)
- Pain-processing techniques: (1) acknowledge/observe pain without self-blame and without treating it as part of one's identity; (2) use a head-to-toe "relaxation protocol" while running, on the premise that "fast running is relaxed running" and tension often amplifies pain — illustrated with an anecdote of using moving meditation during a cold-weather marathon (2013 Bryan-College Station) to salvage a PR after a rough first 8 miles; (3) mentally focus on one's purpose (e.g., picturing athletes he coaches) to stay on pace late in a race; (4) use both "rhythm mantras" and "fight mantras" (a named distinction) to dissociate from pain — examples given: "if not now, when" and "when it hurts, push harder." (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — the explicit rhythm-mantra vs. fight-mantra distinction and the head-to-toe relaxation-protocol technique are specific, teachable tools beyond generic "use a mantra" advice)
- Reframes "triumph" beyond simple "total victory" to include: "moral victories" (training went well and/or a PR was achieved even if the race execution or final result wasn't perfect); the triumph of giving everything you had even when that meant something ugly (walking the last miles of the 2016 Boston Marathon reframed as still "giving his all" that day); the "getting knocked down and getting back up" boxing analogy — triumph doesn't require 100% effort in every single moment, only responding and re-engaging after setbacks; and the "triumph of progress" — deliberately reflecting on long-term improvement rather than only judging a single race in isolation. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — this reframing of "triumph" into multiple discrete categories is a distinctive teaching framework)
- Processing-triumph tips: consciously pause and celebrate (type-A endurance athletes tend to skip past this and move straight to the next goal); accept that triumph and grief/pain can coexist simultaneously in the same race; keep a gratitude journal, citing Deena Kastor's book "Let Your Mind Run" and Kara Goucher's book "Strong" as examples of elite athletes using this practice. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — gratitude journaling/sports psychology, though the specific book citations are useful for fact-checking/sourcing)

## Episode #259: The Anxiety and Peace of Running
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, second installment of the emotions mini-series (continues directly from episode 258).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Explicitly scopes the discussion to "ephemeral" performance-related anxiety (nerves before a big workout or race) and distinguishes it from chronic/clinical anxiety as a mental health condition, for which the host recommends seeking a mental health professional. (PRACTICE — important scope/safety caveat)
- Taxonomy of anxiety types tied to training/racing: anxiety of anticipation (likened to the anxious climb up a roller coaster's first hill before the drop); start-line "frenetic energy" from crowd atmosphere; anxiety of the unknown (facing an unfamiliar distance or pace — illustrated by the host's first 50-miler, where his longest training run had only been 30 miles); anxiety of worry (catastrophizing worst-case scenarios); anxiety of outcomes (fixating on the result and others' judgment of it); and anxiety about "have I done enough" (retrospective doubt about whether training was adequate, illustrated by the host's own doubts before a Boston Marathon after a training cycle disrupted by food poisoning and a breakthrough COVID infection). (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — a granular, named taxonomy of race-specific anxiety types)
- Anxiety-processing framework: (1) notice and name the feeling without judgment or identity-attachment; (2) reframe it as a normal, adaptive stress response indicating the goal is meaningfully large, not a sign of weakness; (3) channel it via one of two named strategies — "dissociative strategies" (deliberate distraction: movies, hobbies, an easy shakeout run, time with friends who won't discuss the race) or "associative strategies" (channeling the energy into concrete race-specific preparation: reviewing training logs, visualizing the course, refining pacing/fueling plans, preparing mantras). (SCIENCE — the dissociative/associative coping distinction is real sports-psychology terminology, though not attributed here to a specific researcher) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general anxiety-coping strategies in sports psychology, though the specific dissociative-vs-associative framing and paired concrete examples may be a useful addition if not already covered this specifically)
- Peace types described: the "flow state" of effortless movement; the peace of connecting with nature (anecdote of stopping mid-trail-run to watch a sunset); the peace of camaraderie/group running, including bonding with a stranger while pacing together for several miles during the Boston Marathon; the peace of knowing you did all the training you could (pre-race, illustrated by the 2018 Houston Marathon PR); and the peace of a satisfying result. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — flow state/nature connection in running is fairly commonly covered)
- Processing-peace tips: name the feeling; deliberately linger in it rather than immediately moving to the next stimulus (a explicit call-out that modern life conditions people to rush past positive moments); extract the specific circumstances that produced the feeling so it can be recreated; and share it with others. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #26: Chasing Excellence with Coach Barnett
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Coach Mike Barnett — head cross country/track coach at Luling High School (small, rural, low-income Texas 3A school; over 60% of students on free/reduced lunch), in his 31st/32nd year at the school at time of recording; personal high school PRs of 1:58.5 (800m) and 4:20 (mile); team had won 7 consecutive Texas state cross country titles at time of interview. Cites Jack Daniels, Joe Vigil, and Arthur Lydiard as major influences (describes tracking down Vigil and having weekly hour-long calls with Jack Daniels early in his career). Co-hosted by Chris McClung and Steve Sisson (former Running Rogue co-host).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core coaching philosophy: "I individualize the team. I don't team the individuals." Every athlete on the team gets an individualized daily training pace/distance based on their current fitness, rather than the whole squad running the same prescribed pack pace or distance. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — an explicit, named philosophy for reconciling individualized training within a team/group coaching context)
- "No junk miles... we don't waste any days" — every run, including easy/recovery runs, has a defined purpose and a specific pace range (e.g., recovery runs are prescribed as 45 seconds to 1 minute slower than the previous day's tempo pace), never an undefined "just go run a few." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — cross-references and reinforces the "junk miles" framework discussed independently in episode #257)
- Describes the "PAVO" training system he uses: named in tribute to the competitive spirit of Paavo Nurmi (explicitly NOT based on Nurmi's actual training methods, addressing a common misconception) — a physiologically-grounded system built on incremental volume tied to individual recovery capacity (no fixed team mileage cap; volume increases only when an athlete shows they can recover), sequential progression that requires observable signs of physiological adaptation ("conversion") before advancing to the next training stimulus (aerobic base → tempo → aerobic-threshold intervals → anaerobic/lactate speed work only once threshold-interval recovery is demonstrated), and highly quantified goal paces (down to tenths of seconds). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a named, structured training methodology that may not otherwise be represented on the site)
- Relays a secondhand claim (sourced to Steve Magness's book "Peak Performance") that Bernard Lagat takes a complete 5-week break from running each year to reset before rebuilding for winter training — offered as a positive example of prioritizing recovery/off-season rest for career longevity. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — book and athlete named) (POSSIBLE GAP — an extended annual full-stop break is a specific, less commonly discussed recovery/periodization practice)
- Endorses (with Joe Vigil's explicit agreement, per anecdote) running easy/recovery days notably slower than is typical — Vigil is quoted as telling him "everybody in this country runs too damn slow... you nailed it," in apparent reference to the pace gap between hard and easy days, i.e., that most runners don't slow down enough on easy days relative to their hard-day pace. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — an explicit pushback, credited to a named sports scientist coach, against typical recovery-day pacing) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy-day pacing/polarized training, though the specific quoted anecdote adds color)
- Training benchmarks set relative to state-meet performance standards for larger school classifications (a small 3A school training to hit 5A/6A qualifying benchmarks) as a concrete, athlete-visible motivational and progress-tracking device; benchmarks are deliberately shared transparently with athletes rather than kept by the coach alone. (PRACTICE)
- Defines "excellence" operationally as "a quality effort against a quality standard," and frames motivation as a teachable skill: explicitly instructing athletes on the behaviors of "motivated people," then diagnosing whichever specific barrier (injury, home situation, illness, or fear) is preventing an athlete from reaching a benchmark they're capable of, rather than treating underperformance as a vague character flaw. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the explicit teach-motivation-as-a-skill, diagnose-the-actual-barrier framework)
- Notes explicit gender-based and socioeconomic differences in athlete training capacity: girls are progressed at lower mileage and more conservatively than boys in his program; and athletes from lower-income or single-parent households may have reduced training capacity/recovery time due to family work obligations (e.g., needing a job, or caring for siblings while a parent works), a context-of-life factor that has to be built into individualized training plans. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the socioeconomic/family-context factor shaping an athlete's realistic training capacity is an angle not typically covered in exercise-physiology-focused running content)
- Deterministic framing of training-to-race translation: "I can tell you what we're going to run" based on observed training data — race performance is treated as a near-direct extrapolation of demonstrated training paces/adaptation rather than something requiring separate "magic" or taper tricks. (PRACTICE/quote — "the magic is in the process")
