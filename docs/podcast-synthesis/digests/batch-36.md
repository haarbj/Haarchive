## Episode #63: Masters Running, Training with Age
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Carmen Troncoso, Rogue Running coach. Elite pre-masters career: four-time US World Cross Country Championships team member, three-time Olympic Trials qualifier, exhibition 5,000m runner at the Olympic Trials. Holds a master's degree in exercise physiology from UT Austin. As a masters athlete, made two more World Cross Country teams at ages 40-41, won multiple US masters national championships, and still holds the US masters (age-40) record for the 3,000m (9:28). Approaching 60 at time of recording.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Physiologically, VO2 max decline with age is driven more by peripheral factors (loss of muscle mass reducing oxygen-extraction capacity at the muscle) than central factors (heart rate max drops slightly, but stroke volume is largely preserved) — training cannot change the neuromuscular slowing (slower muscle contraction/relaxation signaling, slower cardiac relaxation/filling) that sets in around 40-55, but it can offset the muscle-mass and flexibility losses. (SCIENCE, presented as researched but not citing specific studies/researchers by name) (POSSIBLE GAP — masters-specific physiology of aging is a distinct topic from general aerobic-system content)
- Fast-twitch muscle fiber loss with age is described as irrecoverable ("if you lose it, it's gone"), whereas slow-twitch fiber loss can be partially offset by strength training — this is presented as the reason to start serious weight training in your 30s, before losses begin, rather than waiting until 45+. (SCIENCE, asserted, not literature-cited) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Observational/non-scientific claim from decades of coaching: most athletes have about "12 years of really, really hard work" in their legs regardless of starting age — meaning an athlete who starts serious training at 35 can still be setting PRs in their early 50s, while one who started hard training at 12-20 has likely used up that window earlier. Practical implication: masters PR potential depends heavily on training-age/background, not just chronological age. (PRACTICE, explicitly self-described as "non-proven," observational) (POSSIBLE GAP — a distinct mental model for coaching masters athletes' expectations)
- Three-factor framework for masters training changes, roughly constant in kind but shifting in proportion by decade: (1) rest/recovery becomes the top priority — spacing hard workouts from ~1 week apart to 1.5, then to 2.5 weeks as an athlete ages (same effort/pace-equivalent workouts, just far more days between them); (2) strength training, ideally started in the 30s before losses begin; (3) nutrition, both for general health and because metabolism/enzyme production slows, making weight easier to gain past 50. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for strength/nutrition generally, but the recovery-spacing specifics are POSSIBLE GAP)
- Concrete recovery-spacing guidance: in her 40s she needed 2-2.5 days between hard workouts instead of the day or two she needed in her 20s; in her 50s that stretched to 4-5 days; by her later 50s, the same three-workouts-per-week structure she used to fit in 7 days now gets stretched across 2.5 weeks. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — very specific, actionable age-based recovery spacing guidance)
- Volume doesn't need to drop nearly as much as people fear with age if an athlete has a long training history — the "banked" aerobic development from decades of running means a 55-year-old can extract more benefit from 30-35 miles/week than someone without that history, likened to compound interest/money in the bank. But the coaches note this is hard for athletes psychologically (they don't want to reduce volume) even when it's physiologically the right move. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY — "money in the bank"/accrued interest framing for aerobic development) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base, though the "banked development lets you train at lower volume later" framing is a nuance worth capturing)
- Same-effort-different-pace principle: masters athletes should keep doing the *same* workouts (same rep counts, same recovery, same relative effort ~95%) as they did when younger — only the absolute pace changes (roughly 1 minute/mile slower), not the structure or intensity of the session. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general workout-effort principles, though explicit masters application is a useful angle)
- Aging-specific strength/mobility additions: massage/foam rolling to flush waste products from legs; more emphasis on dynamic stretching/mobility (not static stretching) because tendon flexibility (not muscle) declines with age, shortening stride; basic bodyweight circuit training (jumping jacks, burpees, squats — likened explicitly to "CrossFit" but framed as what athletes did decades ago); jump rope recommended as an accessible plyometric substitute; occasional supervised plyometrics/bounding to maintain the "reflex" ground-contact power that produces bounding stride (vs. an aged running gait that "runs toward the ground" rather than bounding). (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific tendon-flexibility-vs-muscle-flexibility distinction and the "running toward the ground" gait-aging observation are fairly specific angles)
- Named workout example ("the cruise workout," attributed to Brazilian 800m world-record holder Joaquim Cruz and coach Téo Rivera at University of Oregon): alternating 100m fast strides/sprints with a calisthenic exercise (jumping jacks, etc.) in a circuit, used as a way to teach neuromuscular firing and confidence in racing sprint capability. (PRACTICE, attributed to a named coach/athlete) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific named circuit workout not typical in general training content)
- Cross-training substitution guidance: as volume necessarily drops with age, replace lost run mileage with cycling (acknowledges legs may lack power even if the cardiovascular system could handle more) and pool/aqua-jogging (with a flotation belt, to preserve running-specific form) and elliptical intervals (described as allowing near-original-speed "mile repeats" with no impact load, closely replicating the cardiovascular distress of hard running). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general cross-training, though the elliptical-for-speed-intervals angle is a specific tactic)
- Racing frequency recommendation for masters athletes: race roughly every 4-6 weeks if recovery allows, but always build in a deliberate post-race recovery period even if you feel great — many athletes skip this and pay for it. Also recommends racing multiple distances (not marathon-only) for long-term career longevity, citing her own 18-year, one-marathon-per-year career as a personal example of avoiding overuse. (PRACTICE)
- Coaching claim (attributed to unnamed "elite coaches," not further sourced) that even marathoners should race frequent 5Ks/8Ks because they function as "the ultimate tempo race" and "ultimate VO2 max" stimulus, and because the 5K and marathon are described as "sister races" — both involve the same psychological arc of pacing discipline (going out too fast in either distance is similarly unrecoverable), just compressed into a much shorter time window. (PRACTICE, attributed vaguely) (POSSIBLE GAP — the explicit "5K and marathon are sister races, same psychological arc, different timescale" framing is a distinct, quotable teaching analogy) (ANALOGY)
- Disagreement/pushback on common marathon-training structure: both coaches criticize the standard internet marathon plan that peaks mileage 4 weeks before race day, calling it "backwards" — peak mileage should occur ~5 months out, with volume brought down as intensity/race-specific work is added afterward. (DISAGREEMENT) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific critique of conventional marathon-plan periodization structure)
- Coaches note a current worry: many recreational marathoners now run very high weekly mileage (85+ miles/week) and are reluctant to cap long runs below 20 miles even when coaches suggest 16-18 would be safer/more sustainable long-term, given uncertainty about how many 20-mile efforts "we have in us" over a lifetime. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with athlete instinct) (POSSIBLE GAP — long-term "budget" framing for how many long, hard long-runs a runner's body can sustain over a career/lifetime)

## Episode #64: Endure with Alex Hutchinson
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Alex Hutchinson, science journalist (formerly Runner's World, then Outside Magazine), author of "Endure: Mind, Body, and the Curiously Elastic Limits of Human Performance." Former competitive middle-distance runner (trained under Matt Centrowitz Sr.). Was present at Nike's "Breaking2" sub-two-hour marathon attempt.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central thesis of the book: the brain, not just the muscles/cardiovascular system, is a major limiter of endurance performance — psychological interventions (self-talk, belief) are described as "a special case of physiology" because they demonstrably change measurable physiological output (e.g., core temperature tolerance), not just subjective feeling. (SCIENCE, citing a real body of research though no single study named in this exchange) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sports psychology/mental limits generally, but likely worth cross-checking granularity)
- Motivational self-talk is specifically validated by multiple recent research studies (unnamed by name in this conversation but described as "multiple studies... from different research groups"): identify negative in-race self-talk patterns, replace with realistic-but-positive phrases ("I've trained for this, I can do this" rather than grandiose claims), and pre-plan phrases for specific race points (halfway, three-quarters). Shown in lab settings to let athletes push core temperature roughly half a degree higher and access more physiological reserve, not just feel better. (SCIENCE, studies referenced but not named) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — mental training/self-talk, though the specific physiological mechanism claim — pushing core temp tolerance — is a fairly specific, useful detail)
- Framework distinguishing "true belief" from "justified belief": for belief to meaningfully improve performance it must be both actually true (you really can run the time) and justified (you have good evidence, e.g., a recent half-marathon time that predicts it) — explains why performance progress is typically incremental rather than a leap of pure faith. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, from the book's argument) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific epistemological framework for goal-setting/belief distinct from generic "believe in yourself" advice)
- Breath-holding safety-margin anecdote: elite breath-holder Brandon Hendrickson's body signals "involuntary breathing movements" (a hard physiological alarm, triggered by rising CO2, not falling oxygen) at about 4 minutes, yet he can push through to a US record of 8:35 — illustrating that the brain's perceived limit is roughly half of the true physiological limit, used as evidence that perceived limits in running (fatigue, "I can't go on") carry a large safety margin and can be pushed past. (SCIENCE, named individual/record cited) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific, vivid quantified example of perceived-vs-actual physiological limit could be a strong teaching tool distinct from generic "mind over matter" framing)
- Electrical brain stimulation (tDCS-type) research is described as increasingly supported (Hutchinson says his confidence in it "actually really does work" has grown since the book) for endurance performance by altering perception of effort — but Hutchinson frames this as double-edged: it demonstrates the size of the untapped physiological reserve, but he considers it "bad news" for what it implies about the primacy of pure competition/self-generated effort vs. external augmentation. He also cites a Red Bull-sponsored cycling time-trial study (cyclists Jesse Thomas and Tim Johnson) where, notably, the fastest times actually came on placebo/sham-stimulation trials — a cautionary example of how hard it is to separate a real brain-stimulation effect from belief/placebo. (SCIENCE, with explicit personal ambivalence, named study/athletes) (DISAGREEMENT — Hutchinson pushes back on treating this as an unambiguous good; philosophical tension flagged, not resolved)
- Discussion of "barrier-breaking" mythology (four-minute mile): breaking a barrier does not automatically cascade to mass replication — the first American didn't break 4:00 until three years after Bannister, second/third not until six years later. Circumstantial/historical factors (World War II interrupting a generation of milers, lack of structured pacing strategy before Bannister) mattered as much as any single "great man" breakthrough — cites War and Peace's "great man theory of history" debate as a framing device, and The Perfect Mile's account of Landy/Santee's less favorable racing circumstances vs. Bannister's. (SCIENCE/historical analysis, not a running-specific claim but a broader point about how innovation disseminates) (POSSIBLE GAP — the sociology of how a barrier-break disseminates (or fails to) through a sport is a genuinely different angle than typical training content)
- Pacing/pacemaker innovation: Bannister's sub-4 run was enabled partly by an even-pacing strategy (roughly 60-60-60-59) at a time when most milers ran erratic splits (fast-slow-fast); this even-pacing knowledge, not just "belief," was a disseminatable, practical takeaway that likely mattered as much as any psychological breakthrough. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — even-pacing strategy, general)
- Drafting/wind-shelter analysis from Breaking2: Hutchinson researched historical marathon-world-record photos and found that in every previous record, the record-setter ran to the side of pacemakers (avoiding literal drafting, using them only for pace), whereas Kipchoge's Breaking2 and Berlin approaches deliberately tucked him directly behind an arrowhead formation of pacers for wind-shelter — a small but real (some evidence suggests up to ~0.5%) aerodynamic advantage that may become a disseminated, systematized tactic in elite marathoning. (SCIENCE — some evidence cited, described as "reasonable evidence," not a definitive study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific historical-photo analysis of drafting position across marathon world records is a very specific, useful detail on race tactics)
- Belief transmission is not universal/transitive — a barrier-break by one elite (e.g., Kipchoge) does not automatically transfer belief to unrelated athletes (e.g., Galen Rupp), but is far more likely to transfer to people who can identify with the barrier-breaker (training partners, same region/background) — illustrated via a personal anecdote about a Kenyan runner motivated to start training seriously after hearing a countryman/near-peer (Eric Wainaina) won an Olympic marathon medal, and via Hutchinson's own reasoning that he chose 1500m as an event partly because two Canadian milers he could personally identify with (Graham Hood, Kevin Sullivan) had reached Olympic finals. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, with named individuals as case examples) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "identifiable near-peer" mechanism for belief transmission, distinct from generic "believe in yourself" messaging, and possibly useful for framing why training-partner/community culture matters for performance)
- The value of a coach-athlete relationship built on trust/confidence over rigid justification of every workout — Hutchinson recounts training under Matt Centrowitz Sr., a highly intuitive, non-explanatory coach, and how teammates who fully trusted the process ("just have faith") outperformed his own more skeptical, "why are we doing this" approach; concludes that picking a coach you can trust, then suspending the need to understand every workout, is itself a performance-relevant skill. (PRACTICE, personal anecdote) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — coach-athlete trust/psychology, general, though this specific framing (skepticism as a personal liability) is a nuance)
- Signature analogy: "it's not the cake, it's the icing" — physiological training fundamentals (mileage, consistent workouts) are "the cake"; scientific/mental performance tricks (beet juice, caffeine, brain stimulation, motivational self-talk) are just "icing" that should never substitute for doing the training work. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC territory, but a strong, quotable framing worth preserving near-verbatim)
- Practical example discussed by the hosts (not from Hutchinson's book) illustrating "belief is itself physiologically relevant": the hosts' own no-nutrition, ~30-mile training run — acknowledged by the coach as having no clear direct physiological benefit over a 24-26 mile run, yet retained because it builds athlete belief that they can handle "anything over 26 miles," which the coaches argue is not physiologically inert since belief itself changes performance capacity. (PRACTICE, explicitly framed by the coaches as belief-driven rather than physiology-driven) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC overlap — see also Episode 68's fuller discussion of no-nutrition runs)

## Episode #65: Health Maintenance for Athletes
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Dr. Cody Godfrey, family practice physician (Baylor Scott & White, Austin).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core message, framed via a real anecdote: an active, healthy-eating, longtime runner/cyclist in the hosts' community had 100% blockage of a main coronary artery discovered only because of an unrelated arm-tingling symptom — he was functioning normally only because of naturally-formed collateral blood vessels; fitness and "eating pretty well" do not substitute for actual medical screening and family-history awareness. (PRACTICE/case example, not itself a scientific claim but a framing anecdote) (POSSIBLE GAP — general "health maintenance beyond training" is a different topical lane than exercise physiology/training content)
- CDC-style baseline activity guideline cited: ~150 minutes/week of moderate-to-vigorous activity (e.g., 30 minutes, 5 days/week) is the general recommendation for an "active lifestyle" — offered as a floor, not a target, for the podcast's audience. (SCIENCE, general public-health guideline, not further sourced) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general exercise recommendations)
- Overdoing exercise physiologically is possible mainly via too-rapid escalation (e.g., going from little/no running straight to a marathon) rather than sustained high volume built up gradually — muscles and heart need conditioning time. (SCIENCE, general, not literature-cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training progression/overuse injury)
- Risk factors that can hide behind apparent fitness: genetic/hereditary conditions (high cholesterol, early heart disease, clotting disorders) and lifestyle factors that don't show up in day-to-day training performance (e.g., excessive drinking straining the heart without affecting how a run feels). Rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown leading to kidney damage) flagged as a specific risk of racing/running distances the body isn't trained for. (SCIENCE, general clinical knowledge, not citation-backed) (POSSIBLE GAP — rhabdomyolysis as a specific runner-relevant risk is likely under-covered in general training content)
- Concrete warning-sign guidance for athletes: chest pain that worsens with increasing exertion, shortness of breath, lightheadedness, and the described "tingling" symptom are heart-related red flags; difficulty breathing may indicate lungs or clots; persistent (non-resolving) localized pain in feet/shins may indicate stress fracture rather than normal training soreness. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE) (POSSIBLE GAP — differentiating "normal training soreness" from injury/medical red flags is a practically useful, distinct checklist)
- Concrete argument for maintaining a primary care physician relationship at every age (not just correlated with "getting older") — a doctor who already knows your history can triage faster and more accurately; the physician states roughly 50%+ of his patients only establish care after a health crisis, calling this rate "too high," and explicitly urges proactive relationship-building rather than reactive care. (PRACTICE, physician's own estimate, not literature-cited) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific advocacy for preventive primary-care relationships is a different lane than the physiology/training content)
- Family history guidance: focus questioning on ~2 generations (parents, grandparents, aunts/uncles), specifically age and cause of death/diagnosis (not just "heart problems" but at what age, e.g., heart attack before 40 is a major red flag) — a parent/grandparent heart attack at a young age (e.g., 45) would prompt the physician to order an earlier stress test even without symptoms. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete family-history interview guidance, a different lane than training-focused content)
- Genetic testing (23andMe-style) commentary: genes interact with environmental factors for most disease risk, so testing can be double-edged — useful if it prompts environmental/behavioral changes, but can also create anxiety over risks that may never manifest ("can open a can of worms you're not ready for"). (SCIENCE, general/cautionary, not citation-backed) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Sleep hygiene guidance: consistent sleep/wake routine, cool room (68-70°F), dark room, avoid screens ~1 hour before bed, low-dose melatonin can help and is described as "pretty benign"; poor sleep is linked to reduced exercise capacity/recovery and increased injury risk, and untreated anxiety/depression is flagged as an underappreciated cause of poor sleep. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, standard sleep-hygiene guidance, not deeply citation-backed here) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sleep and recovery, general, though specific temperature/timing numbers are a useful concrete addition)
- Screen-time commentary: no strong adult-specific research cited (physician explicitly notes society "cares less" about post-childhood screen guidelines), but suggests adults roughly mirror the pediatric ~2 hours/day guideline, and links heavy phone use to increased social anxiety and altered expectations around response time in relationships. (SCIENCE, explicitly flagged by the speaker as under-researched for adults) (POSSIBLE GAP — screen-time/mental-health connection specifically framed for adult athletes)
- Supplement stance: "in theory you shouldn't need any supplements" if eating a well-rounded diet; recommends running any supplement by a doctor first because of documented cases of liver/kidney damage from supplement overuse; performance troubleshooting should start with lab work (e.g., undiagnosed anemia or thyroid dysfunction can silently cap performance) before supplementing. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, general clinical stance, not deeply citation-backed) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general nutrition/supplements; POSSIBLE GAP for the anemia/thyroid-as-hidden-performance-limiter angle specifically)
- Meditation is described by the physician as having enough evidence behind it for mental health, focus, and body-awareness/breathing benefits relevant to performance athletes to be worth recommending. Hosts specifically endorse the Headspace app's free "10 days, 10 minutes" intro program as an accessible entry point. (SCIENCE, general assertion, not literature-cited in this conversation) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — meditation/mindfulness for athletes, general)

## Episode #66: Listener Questions, Round 3
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris and Steve (Rogue Running coaches) answer listener-submitted questions and give race commentary.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Statement-of-purpose methodology defended against listener pushback: a listener (a certified trainer/coach) argued the hosts conflated "why do you train" (general) with "what do you think about at mile 22" (in-race coping), comparing it to golf's "why golf" vs. "swing thought" distinction. Hosts push back that a marathon's mile-22 crisis is not equivalent to an in-the-moment golf swing thought — it is closer to a wilderness-survival "reason to live" scenario (explicitly citing the book Deep Survival by Laurence Gonzalez, which finds survivors of extreme situations disproportionately have "a reason to live") — arguing a crystallized statement-of-purpose/mantra is specifically what lets an athlete reach for a reason to keep going under extreme physical duress, distinct from simpler in-race technical cues (breathing, form) which also have their place. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — hosts explicitly defend their approach against a listener's critique) (POSSIBLE GAP — the wilderness-survival analogy/citation as a framework for marathon mental toughness is a distinctive cross-domain reference)
- Practical marathon-to-5K/10K transition plan (for a high-mileage marathoner, ~90 mi/week, wanting to extend into fast summer racing): take a full 3-week marathon recovery/rebuild (not skipped just because another race is coming), cap long runs at 14-16 miles rather than continuing marathon-length long runs, keep tempo/half-marathon-pace work in every 2-3 week cycle without exception, and add dedicated 3K-pace workouts roughly once every 2-3 weeks (e.g., 4x800m at 3K pace with generous 2-4 min recovery, or 8x400m for less-adapted athletes) to extend the athlete's top-end speed range beyond marathon fitness. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/race-specific training, general, though the specific "add 3K-pace work once every 2-3 weeks post-marathon" prescription is a concrete detail)
- Cramping/implosion troubleshooting framework for a marathoner who cramps consistently around mile 17-20: check (1) daily/weekly hydration and sodium intake in training, not just race-day salt tabs — a body's electrolyte status should be "topped off" well before race week, not corrected mid-race; (2) whether long runs actually simulate race-day fatigue (if cramping happens in racing but not training, training likely isn't specific enough — needs marathon-simulation long runs, not necessarily 26.2 miles but structured to produce similar late-race stress); (3) whether the race was started too fast relative to fitness (implosion by mile 13-15 suggests early pace was too aggressive, "burning matches" early). Posterior-chain/glute strength work is suggested as possibly helpful but explicitly framed as unlikely to be the root cause. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — cramping/hydration and pacing strategy, general, but the three-part diagnostic checklist is a useful structured framework)
- Masters-training follow-up nuance (responding to a listener who trains in a fixed weekly group schedule): group/team training programs are often already effectively running on a longer cycle than they appear (e.g., a "weekly" schedule that's actually two hard weeks + one down week averaged over 3 weeks = a ~20-day cycle), so masters athletes in group settings may already get more built-in recovery flexibility than they realize; athletes should mentally "circle" the 2-3 workouts per training block that truly matter and protect recovery around those specifically, treating other sessions as adjustable/optional-intensity. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC overlap with Episode 63, but the "identify and protect only the workouts that matter" heuristic is a specific, transferable practical tool)
- On aging past 60 specifically (from a listener comment, endorsed by the host): the psychological transition is described as often more pronounced at becoming a "senior" (60+) than at 40 or 50, evidenced by USATF Boston-Qualifying time standards increasing in 15-minute increments per age group past 60 versus 10-minute increments before that. Coach's proposed practical response: be "ruthless" about resetting what's reasonable to expect at each stage rather than clinging to previous-decade benchmarks, while not using aging as an excuse to reduce effort/commitment; cites the Walter Payton quote "Never die easy" as a mantra for fighting decline. (PRACTICE/quote worth preserving, host explicitly caveats he hasn't personally reached that age) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific BQ-standard-increment data point as objective evidence of an accelerating age-related decline past 60)
- Observation on why some heavily-decorated college runners fizzle post-collegiately while lightly-decorated ones (cited by name: Molly Huddle, Des Linden, contrasted with Shalane Flanagan's more decorated college career) become elite pros anyway: key factors proposed are coaching continuity (not jumping programs/coaches), adequate life support structure, and — most importantly — having found an intrinsic purpose for running beyond the external "carrot" of the college eligibility/season cycle, which many decorated college athletes lose once that structure disappears. (PRACTICE/observation, not research-backed) (POSSIBLE GAP — talent-development/longevity angle distinct from physiology or fueling content)

## Episode #67: Keeping it Real with Colleen Quigley
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Colleen Quigley, US steeplechaser — 2016 Olympian, three-time World Championship team member in the steeplechase (one indoor 1500m team as well), NCAA steeplechase champion at Florida State, member of the Bowerman Track Club (coached by Jerry Schumacher and Pascal Dobert).

**Concepts & claims:**

(PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this explicit two-axis (physical readiness x mental readiness) framework for post-race diagnosis is a useful, reusable coaching lens)

## Episode #68: Fueling Your Run
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris and Steve (Rogue Running coaches), a full topical/practical episode on running nutrition, prompted by a listener request.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Three framing principles stated up front: (1) fueling meaningfully affects marathon (and to a lesser extent half-marathon) performance because the body cannot store enough glycogen for 26.2 miles at race effort — this is explicitly why runners "hit the wall"; (2) fueling strategy is highly individual and must be personally experimented with in training, not copied from a training partner; (3) the gut can be trained to tolerate more carbohydrate over time through repeated practice, similar to how ultrarunners train their stomachs — cites Alex Hutchinson's Endure as covering this. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, general, cross-referencing episode 64's guest book) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, though the explicit "gut-training" framing as its own trainable adaptation is a useful specific angle)
- Carbo-loading myth-busting: casual "eat lots of pasta the night before" carbo-loading doesn't work and mainly risks GI distress ("a very, very large stool," in the hosts' words) — genuine, evidence-supported carb-loading requires one of two week-long protocols: (1) the classic 1970s-era glycogen-depletion-then-supercompensation protocol (avoid carbs for ~3-4 days about a week out, then load beyond normal for the remainder), or (2) a modern approach of precisely tracking and elevating gram-for-gram carbohydrate intake (relative to body weight) for the final 5-6 days — one host describes personally doing the second protocol for a marathon and finding it logistically taxing with unclear results. (SCIENCE, general, but not attributed to a specific named study — one protocol is attributed in passing to a former colleague with a master's degree, Jeff Knight) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — carbohydrate loading, general, though the concrete two-named-protocol structure is a specific, useful addition)
- Historical/analogy note: 1970s marathoners (cites Frank Shorter's Olympic silver-medal marathon) fueled during races with "de-fizzed Coke," functionally similar in composition (high-fructose corn syrup) to a modern energy gel — used to frame modern gels as not a novel invention so much as a more convenient packaging of the same basic sugar-delivery mechanism. (SCIENCE/ANALOGY, historical claim, not deeply sourced) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific historical de-fizzed-Coke-to-modern-gel lineage is a memorable, concrete detail)
- Week-before hydration guidance, explicitly contrasted with a common mistake: rather than "hyper-hydrating" only in the 24-48 hours before a race (which the hosts argue, citing Hutchinson's Endure discussion of hydration/nutrition, can flush the body's natural electrolyte balance and trigger cravings/imbalance right before race day), spread increased water intake across the entire race week (or from the moment of air travel to the race destination) to avoid this rebound-electrolyte problem. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, cross-referencing Episode 64's guest's book) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "over-hydrating only in the final 24-48 hours flushes electrolytes and backfires" mechanism is a concrete, actionable caution not typically spelled out)
- During-run fueling categorized into three practical buckets: (1) fast sugar (gels, chews, "real food" alternatives like Skittles or dates, described as workable options beyond standard branded gels); (2) slow-burning cornstarch-based products (e.g., UCAN) taken pre-run, providing multi-hour steady energy without the insulin-spike/crash cycle of sugar; (3) fat-metabolism-priming products (e.g., Vespa, an amino-acid/beeswax product) taken before a run to shift the body toward burning fat stores sooner, reducing reliance on glycogen. General timing rule: start supplementing only after the ~2-hour mark of a long run/race (the point at which the body's own glycogen typically runs low), then resume fueling roughly every 30-45 minutes to avoid the insulin-spike-then-crash pattern that occurs if sugar intake isn't sustained once started. (PRACTICE, brand names given as examples/options rather than endorsements framed as backed by trials) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race-day fueling generally, though the specific "why come back to sugar every 30-45 min" (avoiding the post-spike crash) mechanism, and the three-bucket categorization, are useful structuring devices)
- "Real food" alternative for gel-intolerant athletes: baby food pouches (not just applesauce — savory options like cauliflower and sweet potato are now available) recommended as a practical substitute for athletes, especially those with ultrarunning backgrounds, who struggle with sugar-heavy gels; described as a "game changer" for multiple coached athletes. (PRACTICE, from personal coaching experience) (POSSIBLE GAP — baby food as an in-race fuel source for road racers is a distinctly unusual, concrete tactic)
- Specific troubleshooting insight for gel/sugar intolerance: one in roughly ten athletes who suffer severe GI distress from race-day gels turn out to be skipping breakfast (or eating minimal food) before the race — introducing gels into an empty, "volatile" stomach can trigger explosive GI reactions; the fix was to add real carbohydrate (bread/bagel) the morning of long runs and races so gels are introduced into a stomach that already has food in it, which resolved a chronic problem for one athlete who improved from a 3:20 to a 2:54 marathoner over roughly 18-24 months, partly attributed to this fix. (PRACTICE, specific coached-athlete case example) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific "empty stomach + gel = GI explosion" diagnostic insight, and its fix, is a concrete and non-obvious troubleshooting tip)

(SCIENCE, attributed to a named science journalist relaying a study, not a fully cited paper) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — hydration during exercise generally, though the specific 90-minute drink-to-thirst-vs-proactive threshold is a useful concrete rule)
- Salt tabs (e.g., Endurolytes/Succeed brand-style) protocol given: one serving before the run/race, then one roughly every 45-60 minutes depending on sweat rate — recommended particularly for athletes with GI issues from sugary sports drinks or for anyone racing/training in heat. A non-pill electrolyte alternative (a liquid concentrate product, "Oral IV," taken in small break-open tubes) is also mentioned as a personal-use option for athletes who can't tolerate swallowing capsules mid-race. (PRACTICE, brand examples given) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — electrolyte/salt supplementation generally)
- Cramping-troubleshooting cross-reference (echoing Episode 66's listener question): late-race cramping in athletes who have already done multiple 20+ mile long runs is described as "almost assuredly" an electrolyte-imbalance issue, whereas cramping in athletes who haven't done enough 22-24 mile long runs (not just up-to-20) is described as a training-specificity issue, not a fueling issue — distinguishes the two root causes explicitly. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC overlap with Episode 66)

(PRACTICE, explicitly non-peer-reviewed and explicitly contested by a named exercise physiologist; also DISAGREEMENT in that the coach knowingly proceeds against a scientist's stated skepticism) (POSSIBLE GAP — the no-nutrition/fasted long run as a distinct, well-specified training tool, including its explicit tension with formal exercise science, is a genuinely different and detailed angle not likely covered by standard fueling content)
- Post-run refueling guidance: aim for a balanced meal (carbs + protein + fat) within about an hour of finishing a long run/race, when the body is more receptive to glycogen replenishment and muscle-repair building blocks — doesn't need to be precisely macro-counted, just balanced and "real food." Explicitly warns against using a hard run as license to eat purely sugary/inflammatory "junk" recovery food, and separately warns against drinking alcohol before eating real food post-race (alcohol impairs the refueling process even though beer itself contains some carbohydrate). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE, general, not deeply citation-backed) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — post-exercise nutrition/recovery window, general)

## Episode #7: Miles Matter
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris and Steve (Rogue Running coaches), part of a foundational series explaining the core tenets of their Lydiard-influenced training philosophy. Steve cites his own mentor relationship with Joe Vigil (Deena Kastor's longtime former coach) as a source for some of the ideas discussed.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core aerobic-development rationale: most adult recreational runners are "aerobic midgets" (host's phrase) — they lack the decade(s)-long accumulated aerobic training history that youth/collegiate athletes have, so building weekly mileage is about literally reconstructing the body (adding mitochondria, capillary density, red blood cells, and improving lung-to-blood oxygen transfer efficiency), a slow process requiring sustained, easy-paced volume over years, not weeks. (SCIENCE, general exercise-physiology claim, not literature-cited in this discussion but standard/well-established) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base development, core existing content, though the "aerobic midget" framing itself is a notably blunt, memorable phrase)
- Named mentor claim: Joe Vigil (Deena Kastor's former coach) reportedly held the view that all distance runners, from 1500m specialists up through marathoners, should be running around 100 miles/week for full aerobic development — presented as one influential coach's philosophy, not universal prescription, and explicitly tempered by the hosts as impractical for most recreational athletes. (PRACTICE, attributed to a named elite coach) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — high mileage philosophy, general, though the specific named attribution and number are a useful concrete data point)
- Two teaching analogies for why base-building at easy pace matters even though it feels counterintuitive to "run slow to get fast": (1) engine-size analogy — an untrained aerobic system is a "lawnmower engine" revved to its max but producing little power, versus a large-displacement "8- or 10-cylinder engine" built through mileage that can be "fine-tuned" later; running fast prematurely just "revs" an undersized engine rather than growing it. (2) Strength-training analogy — building a bigger bench-press max requires mostly higher-rep/lower-weight volume work, with periodic max-effort tests, directly paralleling why most running volume should be easy-paced with periodic quality testing. (ANALOGY, both explicitly built by the hosts as teaching devices) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base building, though these are strong, reusable, quotable analogies worth preserving verbatim if the site wants memorable framing devices)
- Named physiological principle: "you can't get fast and strong [i.e., aerobically developed] at the same time" — attributed to Joe Vigil's "multi-paced system" philosophy — meaning early-phase training should prioritize one adaptation (aerobic base) before layering in race-pace-specific quality work, since high-volume and high-intensity training pull the body's adaptive resources in different directions. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE, attributed to a named coach) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization principles, general, though the specific "can't build both simultaneously" framing attributed to Vigil is a useful, quotable, specific claim)
- Strength-is-speed argument, illustrated with a concrete question-based teaching device: ask a runner how fast they could run a single 400m (e.g., 90 seconds for an 8-10 min/mile runner), then ask if they could repeat that pace four times to run a 6-minute mile — they typically say no, illustrating that the limiter for most runners isn't top-end speed but the aerobic "strength" (endurance) to sustain a fraction of that speed over distance; used to argue why aerobic development, not raw speed work, is usually the correct target for most runners' plateaus. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY, a specific coaching interrogation technique) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific Socratic "can you repeat your fast pace four times" diagnostic question is a concrete, reusable coaching tool for explaining strength-vs-speed to skeptical runners)
- Concrete mileage-building protocol: build by no more than ~5 miles/week per increase, repeating a mileage level (or dropping back one level) whenever recovery signals are poor, rather than committing to a fixed timeline; build "days" (frequency) before building total mileage on those days (e.g., go from 3 runs/week to 5-6 runs/week of similar length first, then add distance, starting with the long run and one midweek medium-long run); map mileage goals out over multiple years/summers rather than a single season. Building back after a break follows the same 5-mile/week rule, but the starting point scales to the runner's established "sweet spot" (e.g., a 100-mile/week runner restarts around 50-55 miles/week post-break, not from zero, while a 45-mile/week runner restarts around 25-30). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — mileage progression, general, though the specific "5 miles/week ceiling, build days before miles, restart proportional to your established sweet spot" rules are concrete, actionable specifics)
- "Strides" defined precisely and distinguished from interval training: 6-10 second bursts at ~85-90% max effort (not sprinting), done with full recovery (walk back, 5-10+ min between if done post-run) — explicitly stated to train neuromuscular recruitment and ballistic push-off mechanics/tendon-ligament resilience, not aerobic or anaerobic energy systems; recommended year-round, at least once weekly, regardless of training phase, and described (along with "foot drills") as one of the two single highest-value resilience/injury-prevention tools available if an athlete had time for only two things. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the precise distinction between strides-as-neuromuscular-training vs. intervals-as-energy-system-training, and the specific "if you could only do two things" prioritization of strides + foot drills for resilience, is a useful specific detail beyond generic "do strides" advice)
- Easy-pace calibration guidance: prioritize the "talk test" (able to hold a full conversation without gasping) over heart-rate monitors or GPS pace as the primary tool for gauging easy effort, on the reasoning that external gadgets are a secondary/corroborating signal, not the primary one — body awareness (breathing pattern specifically: full, relaxed inhale/exhale) is described as more reliable and individual since "everybody's easy is different" even within pace-matched training groups. Also notes it's very difficult to run "too slow" to gain aerobic benefit — most runners' risk is running too fast on easy days, not too slow. (PRACTICE, coach's stated preference, not literature-backed) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy-pace/talk-test guidance, general, though explicitly deprioritizing heart-rate/GPS data below body-awareness is a slight disagreement with a data-driven training trend worth flagging) (DISAGREEMENT with heart-rate/GPS-first training culture)
- Direct critique of the "Run Less, Run Faster" (Runner's World-style, low-mileage/high-intensity) training model: hosts argue such programs can produce a short-lived performance peak (roughly 3-6 months, up to 12-18 months for athletes with an existing high-volume background) by "tearing away" at aerobic base to fund quality work, but are not sustainable for continuous long-term improvement — framed as effectively unsustainable "short-term borrowing against your base," with a value judgment that such programs are commercially motivated ("beholden to shareholders") rather than optimized for the individual runner's long-term development. (DISAGREEMENT, explicit) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/mileage vs. intensity debates, general, but the specific, named critique of "Run Less, Run Faster" and its commercial-motive framing is a pointed, specific disagreement worth preserving if the site engages with competing training philosophies)

Illness specifically requires a full return to 100% health before resuming hard training — no "in-between" state — with mileage dropped back (e.g., roughly 75% of normal, rebuilding via the same 5-mile/week rule) proportional to how much training time was lost. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general training-adjustment/illness-return guidance, though the explicit "no make-up workouts, no in-between health state" framing is a clear, quotable rule)
