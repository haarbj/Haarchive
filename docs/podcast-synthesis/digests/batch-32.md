## Episode #389 (audio intro states "episode 388"): What is Quality Work (aka Speed Work)?
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris McClung (founder/head coach, Rogue Running, Austin/Dallas run coaching company; author) and James Dodds (Rogue coach) discussing their own coaching book, chapter 5.

*Note: this file is labeled Episode #389, but the host verbally introduces it as "episode 388." Every other file in this batch has its spoken episode number match its filename number, so this appears to be an isolated off-by-one (either a misspoken intro or a mislabeled file), not a batch-wide pattern.*

**Concepts & claims:**
- FVI framework (Frequency, Volume, Intensity): quality/speed work should only be added once frequency and volume are established; rule of thumb is a 10-mile long run as the threshold for being ready to add quality workouts. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — base building/periodization)
- Paraphrased quote attributed to coach Alan Cousins: "You don't do quality or speed workouts because you're bored. You do them because you're aerobically ready." (PRACTICE, quote)
- Four-category taxonomy of "quality" workouts: (1) running economy/speed-development, (2) VO2 max, (3) threshold/aerobic-strength, (4) race-specific. Framed as a comprehensive organizing system for all non-easy running. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific 4-bucket taxonomy and the "quality" vs. "speed" terminology distinction, even though the underlying physiology zones are standard)
- Strides defined precisely: 80–100m controlled sprint, build for a third, hold 85–95% of peak speed for the middle third (~30m), ease off the final third, full walk-back recovery, 4–6 reps, done at the end of medium-long or easy runs, never after long runs or other quality sessions. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this level of prescriptive execution detail)
- Analogy: strides as "allergy shots for speed" — small weekly doses accumulated over months/years produce gradual, durable adaptation. (ANALOGY)
- Coaching cue: running-economy work is "salt and pepper" — a small dose, not the main meal of training. (ANALOGY)
- Straights-and-curves workout (12–14 laps, stride straights/jog curves) as an advanced stride progression; named common mistake: running the curves too fast turns it into an aerobic workout instead of a neuromuscular/economy workout. (PRACTICE)
- Hill strides (60–80m, 4–6% grade): lower impact than flat strides (shortened stride cycle) while still recruiting more force/glute activation; form cues — eyes up to hilltop, quicker turnover, drive knees/arms. Quote attributed to Frank Shorter: "hills are speed work in disguise." (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- VO2 max explained as maximal oxygen uptake per kg body weight (range cited ~35–75); workouts are deliberately done just *below* true VO2 max effort because max effort itself is too taxing/risky to train at directly. Jack Daniels credited with popularizing the 2-mile/3000m time trial as a practical field-test proxy for VO2 max. (SCIENCE — named researcher) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Named VO2 max workout templates: 200s/400s ladders, 8x800 progressing from half-marathon pace → 10K pace → 5K pace, and the "Texas 10K" ladder (4x400, 2x800, 1xmile, 2x800, 4x400, all at 10K pace). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific named/branded workout templates)
- Threshold terminology: aerobic threshold (AT/LT1, roughly a 2-hour race effort) vs. lactate/anaerobic threshold (LT2, roughly a 1-hour race effort), with "tempo" as the equilibrium zone in between. (SCIENCE, standard physiology) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Analogy: the lactate-processing zone between AT and LT described as a bank's "revolving door" — lactate coming in and being recycled/cleared; going too hard "jams the door." Credited to a visual James Dodds learned from someone else ~10–12 years earlier. (ANALOGY)
- Effort-word cues: aerobic threshold = "comfortably hard"; lactate threshold = still in control but on the edge, "and I'd like to be done." (PRACTICE)
- Threshold progression example: 4x8–10min at AT progressing toward LT → 2x15–20min → a single continuous 35–40min effort late in the training block — deliberately capped below the theoretical 60-minute race-effort equivalent so as not to "spend all their savings." (PRACTICE, financial analogy)
- Claim: threshold workouts should not be done more than every other week, even during the dedicated aerobic-strength phase. (PRACTICE)
- Race-specific workouts: two main forms — continuous race-pace repeats, and "in-and-out" workouts alternating faster-than-race-pace segments with race pace so race pace "feels like recovery." Marathon pace is described as a physiological "no man's land" — limited standalone physiological benefit, so marathon-pace-specific work is deliberately confined to the final 2–8 weeks. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE claim) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific argument against extensive marathon-pace training outside the race-specific window)
- Explicit pushback: running "20 miles at race pace" in marathon training is called overdoing it — equivalent to "racing the workout." (DISAGREEMENT with a common practice)
- Overall philosophy: "When in doubt, choose easier" — healthy and slightly undercooked beats injured or overcooked; no single quality workout matters as much as the cumulative body of work ("seeing the forest for the trees"). (PRACTICE)

## Episode #390: The Why and How of Running by Feel
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris McClung and James Dodds, continuing their book series (chapter 6).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core argument: pace alone is not a reliable measure of effort — temperature, humidity, altitude, terrain grade, stress, sleep, and fueling all change what a given pace "costs" physiologically; the same 10-minute mile can represent very different efforts. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — effort vs. pace is a common aerobic-training theme, though depth here may exceed a typical treatment)
- RPE (rate of perceived exertion) presented as a legitimate 1–10 scientific construct; claim that "people are actually pretty good" at rating RPE relative to true effort zones, if they pay attention — asserted without naming a specific study. (SCIENCE, vague citation)
- Practical tip: log RPE (1–10) in a training log/Strava notes for every run as a low-effort way to build feel/intuition over time. (PRACTICE)
- Wrist-based heart rate flagged as unreliable (can sync to cadence instead of true HR); chest strap recommended for genuine accuracy. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — practical HR-monitor accuracy caveats)
- Observed inconsistency: runners readily slow down for altitude (a direct, felt effect — less oxygen) but resist slowing down for heat/humidity (an indirect effect on chemistry/muscle friction), partly attributed to ego and the need for pace-based validation. Rough heuristic mentioned: adjust pace roughly 20–30 sec/mile per meaningful increase in heat/humidity. (PRACTICE insight, psychological) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific heat pace-adjustment heuristics)
- Philosophical framing of intuition: cites Dr. Iain McGilchrist's hemisphere theory (right brain = meaning/intuition, left brain = logic/calculation) and Socrates' "daemon" (an inner voice that tells him only what *not* to do) as grounding for trusting felt effort over calculated pace. (SCIENCE — named real researcher/philosopher, though the application is soft/tangential) (POSSIBLE GAP — this philosophical framing of intuition-based training is unlikely to be in a physiology-focused site)
- Concrete effort-zone cues: easy runs = full-sentence conversational pace, can sing a song without gasping; recovery runs = "glacially slow"/"pitter-patter," should feel better at the end than the start; aerobic threshold = "comfortably hard," broken conversation possible; tempo = "fidgety" — in control but tempted to bail, likened to being stuck in a middle airplane seat or a room at an uncomfortably even temperature (ANALOGY, coined by James Dodds); lactate threshold = on "the edge of control." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for the underlying zones; POSSIBLE GAP for the specific "fidgety" teaching language)
- Practical technique: cover/ignore the GPS watch during threshold-type workouts to force feel-based pacing, reviewing the data only afterward. (PRACTICE)
- Coaching trick (James Dodds): prescribe threshold work as time-based rather than distance-based, removing the "mental reward" of speeding up near the end of a fixed distance and thereby preventing athletes from blowing up the workout late. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, underused coaching technique)
- Technique attributed to a coach named John/Juan Trump: execute every rep/workout as a slight progression (start a touch slow, build incrementally) rather than trying to hit exact target pace immediately; more dramatic progression early in a training cycle, subtler as race day nears. Helps calibrate feel and prevents overshooting pace on the first rep. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Post-run and next-day feel as a calibration tool: recovery runs should leave you feeling better than when you started; being flat/unable to do the next day's easy run signals overreach. (PRACTICE)
- Anecdote: runner Carolyn Coffin reportedly ran the Boston Marathon with her watch covered, running entirely by feel — cited as an aspirational example of leveling up felt-effort skill. (PRACTICE anecdote, named real person)

## Episode #391: Long-Term Planning for Max Success
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris McClung and James Dodds, book chapter 7.

**Concepts & claims:**

- Recommendation: within a 12–18 month window, sequence 2–3 distinct macro cycles of different race distances (e.g., marathon block, half-marathon block, speed/5K-10K block) rather than repeating the same distance back-to-back, to avoid plateauing or becoming "one-dimensional." Repeating the same race distance is explicitly named as a common cause of plateaus. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT with the common pattern of chasing marathon after marathon)

Paraphrased quote attributed to Eliud Kipchoge: "you can't chase two rabbits" (i.e., you can't simultaneously optimize for "just having fun" and for a secretly-desired performance outcome). ## Episode #395: How Big is Your Base?
