## Episode #348: The (Rational) Irrational Fears Episode
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode with host Chris McClung, owner/head coach of Rogue Running (Austin, TX), competitive marathoner (PR 2:45:36, 22 marathons run), ~20+ years coaching experience.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Fear #1 — "I'll never be in that shape again" post-peak: post-race detraining/flatness is a *necessary* physiological reset, not a sign fitness is gone; the body needs a lull to rebuild to a higher peak. Framed via periodization: after peaking/tapering/racing, you return to base/priming phase and must "stay present" in that early stage rather than judging it against peak-fitness paces. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/base-building, but the specific psychological reframe of post-peak detraining as necessary-not-alarming is a good practical angle)
- Fear #2 — "If I train for a different distance I'll forget how to race my main distance": claims cross-distance training is mutually reinforcing — 5K/10K speed improves half/marathon potential and vice versa, and volume built for shorter-distance training (e.g., 12-16mi long runs while training for a 5K/10K) keeps a runner "one step away" from marathon-length long runs. Also claims each distance builds a distinct *mental* skill (5K = pain tolerance, 10K = sustained focus, half = relaxed efficiency at the edge, marathon = grinding through late fatigue) that transfers across distances. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "train for X without racing X" concept and cross-distance mental-skill transfer framing goes beyond generic "vary your training" advice)
- Fear #3 — "I'll never be healthy again" (injury): claims injuries are frequently "blessings in disguise" that reveal an underlying weakness (e.g., knee pain revealing weak/inactive glutes) which, once addressed, produces a stronger, faster, more injury-resistant runner. Recommends a "bimodal" mental approach: dream about the big goal periodically, but stay focused day-to-day on "what's the next small step." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general injury/recovery psychology, though the "reveal a weakness" framing and bimodal dreaming/present-focus technique are a specific angle)
- Fear #4 — "Going slow on easy days will make me slower": reiterates polarized-training doctrine — slow easy/recovery runs enable higher sustainable volume and better quality-day performance; claims comfort/efficiency at slow paces transfers to efficiency at faster paces ("your ability to be smooth at all paces helps you be smoother at all other paces"). Recommends committing to extreme slow-down for a full 6-month cycle to see results. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base/polarized training is presumably already covered)
- Fear #5 — "There's no way I could hold that pace for the whole race" (only having run segments in training): Puzzle analogy — race-day performance is a completed picture assembled from many different unique training "pieces" (easy runs, medium-long runs, long runs with workouts, nutrition/hydration practice, mental prep), and running a few miles at goal pace in training is only one piece, not the whole picture. Also: "focus on what you do know, not what you don't know" — you don't need to know you can hold pace for 26 miles, only that you can hold it for one mile, and the race is run one mile at a time. (ANALOGY) (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the puzzle-piece framing and "you only need to know you can do one mile" is a distinct, quotable mental-performance tool for race-day doubt)
- Fear #6 — "X goal isn't possible for me": claims most runners under-dream because they lack a reference point for what's possible; coach's role is to reveal higher potential ("you are worthy of big goals" regardless of starting point). Recommends positive-affirmation reinforcement (write the goal on your mirror, dashboard, password, etc.) to reprogram self-doubt into belief. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology / goal-setting, though coach's specific "worthiness" framing and physical-reminder tactic add color)

## Episode #349: Athlete Questions on Bad Days, Paces, Taper Length, Dogs, and More
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo Q&A episode with Chris McClung (see above).

**Concepts & claims:**
- "You can't go too slow" on easy runs: rule of thumb is easy runs at marathon pace +1 min/mile (or half-marathon pace +1.5 min/mile) *or slower*, recovery runs at +2 min (or +2.5 min half) *or slower*; explicitly says runners should NOT speed up easy runs just because their fitness/workout paces improve — stay conservative on easy days even as quality paces progress. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy pace prescriptions are standard, though the "don't scale up easy pace with fitness gains" nuance is a useful specific)
- Bad workout days: distinguishes one-off bad days (partial miss ~80-90%, or a near-total miss ~15%) from chronic bad-day stretches (3-4+ consecutive bad long runs/workouts over 1.5-2 weeks). Claims from ~20 years/thousands of coached runners there is low correlation between isolated bad training days and race-day outcomes — sometimes a bad workout precedes a great race and vice versa. Recommends never trying to "make up" a missed/bad workout by repeating it later in a race-specific block. (PRACTICE) (SCIENCE — presented as evidence from personal coaching-database experience, not a cited study/researcher) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general "don't panic about one bad workout" is common coaching wisdom, but the specific chronic-vs-isolated framework and recommendation against "making up" workouts is a concrete decision rule)
- If bad days chain together (2+ weeks), recommends: significantly reduce intensity, consider interventional blood work (anemia, iron, etc.) assuming training variables (easy pace, fueling, stress/rest balance) are already sound. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overtraining/underrecovery, though the "get bloodwork if training checks out" recommendation is a specific actionable step)
- Dog-safety strategy for runners (from a listener question): assess context (urban vs. rural/feral dogs — feral dogs relying on humans for food tend to be safer than owned dogs "protecting territory/owner"; predatory dog packs in some regions should simply be avoided entirely, e.g., use a treadmill). Tactical protocol: give leashed dogs wide berth regardless of owner reassurance; for an off-leash dog encounter — stop, don't turn your back, don't make direct eye contact (viewed as aggressive), speak calmly, slowly back away (compared explicitly to bear encounters — "hey bear, hey dog"); don't run away (triggers chase instinct); pepper spray as last resort (practice using it first), tasers not recommended. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — highly specific, non-physiological safety topic almost certainly not covered by an exercise-physiology-focused site)
- Marathon/half taper length: standard rule of thumb is 3-week taper for marathon, 2-week for half, appropriate for ~90% of runners. More experienced athletes can test shorter tapers (2-week marathon / 1-week half) if they empirically "do better at higher mileage" — recommends testing this via how one responds to down-week volume reductions (do you rebound stronger after a 20-25% down-week cut vs. a 10-15% cut?) as a lower-risk proxy since full tapers can't be tested often. Explicitly separates "mental" taper-crazies from real physiological signals — shortening taper to address taper anxiety alone is discouraged. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — tapering is presumably covered, but the down-week-reduction-as-taper-length-proxy testing method is a distinct practical tool)
- "Anything workouts": deliberately very hard, but always-prepared-for workouts (e.g., their point-to-point "Boston Simulator" long run mimicking Boston's downhills/hills + track mile repeats) used to build race-day confidence ("if I can do that, I can do anything"); coach's rule: never assign anything he wouldn't do himself or that lacks explicit purpose. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY — "anything workouts" as a coined term) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — confidence-building hard workouts is a known concept, though the specific naming/framing may be new)

## Episode #350: 10 Tips to Get Your Mind Right for Race Day
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode with Chris McClung, recorded on-site at the Boston Marathon.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Tip 1 — Purpose/intention: distinguish "why you run" (general benefits) from "why you want THIS specific goal" (personal, often not examined); visualize the finish-line moment and the emotion it represents to clarify purpose before race day. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — goal-setting/motivation is standard sports psych territory)
- Tip 2 — Review training wins pre-race and journal them regardless of race outcome, because "training results and racing results" don't always align — a bad race day doesn't erase real training progress (longest long run ever, new workout paces, improved effort-based pacing, etc.). (PRACTICE)
- Tip 3 — Be present / run it "one mile at a time": don't let the mind project forward to mile 20-22 (marathon) or mile 11-12 (half); acknowledge intrusive thoughts about the finish, then redirect to the current mile. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — present-moment focus is common in sports psychology)
- Tip 4 — Channel pre-race anxiety into concrete preparation tasks (studying race plan, writing nutrition plan, studying course map, logistics) rather than suppressing it; if that fails, dissociate/distract (TV, books, friends). Frames nerves as a normal, useful evolutionary/physiological response (adrenaline priming the body), not something to eliminate. Also: don't worry about a sleepless night before the race — very common and not predictive of poor performance; prioritize sleep in the days *before* the final night instead. (PRACTICE) (SCIENCE — evolutionary/physiological framing of nerves is asserted, not sourced to a named study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — pre-race anxiety management is standard sports psych, though "don't panic about night-before sleeplessness" is a specific reassurance point)
- Tip 5 — Reframe "what if negative" into "what if positive": cites psychological principle that telling someone to avoid an obstacle makes them more likely to hit it (framed as "psychology tells us"); recommends acknowledging negative race-day scenarios but then deliberately shifting mental share toward positive outcomes. (SCIENCE — psychological principle asserted without naming a specific researcher/study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general reframing technique)
- Tip 6 — Mantras: two categories — "rhythm mantras" (early race, for staying relaxed/efficient, e.g., "light and relaxed," "smile," "breathe") and "fight mantras" (late race, for pushing through pain, e.g., "every second counts," "when it hurts, push harder," "I am strong, I am fast"). Cites Alex Hutchinson's book *Endure* as support that positive self-talk/affirmations measurably improve endurance performance (Hutchinson previously a skeptic). Recommends athletes develop 2-3 personal mantras per category rather than borrowing others' verbatim. (SCIENCE — names Alex Hutchinson/*Endure* explicitly) (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific two-category rhythm/fight mantra framework is a concrete, actionable mental tool that a general "sports psychology" section might not spell out this specifically)
- Tip 7 — Mental tools: "going fishing" (using runners ahead as targets to chase down/pass late in a race, looking ~50m ahead for a target) and "counting" (counting to 30 or 60 repeatedly in the final mile(s), telling yourself "I can do anything for 30/60 seconds"). (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY — "going fishing") (POSSIBLE GAP — specific named late-race coping techniques)
- Tip 8 — Visualization: claims visualizing performing an action produces a "similar neurological response" to physically doing it (cites Aaron Rodgers' reported use of visualization for tight throws as an analogous example, not a runner-specific study). Recommends multi-sensory visualization (sound, feel, smell) of the entire race arc, ideally rehearsed across several easy runs or in one dedicated session pre-race. (SCIENCE — claims a neurological equivalence "science tells us," but no specific study/researcher named; the Aaron Rodgers example is anecdote, not evidence) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — visualization is a known sports psych tool, though worth flagging the science claim is unsourced)
- Tip 9 — Execute the plan (pacing and nutrition) regardless of how you feel; explicitly warns against skipping planned nutrition (e.g., gels) just because you feel good early — "you should feel good at that point... do not skip your gels." Recommends writing the nutrition plan down in advance. Race-start pacing advice: "if you miss, miss slow" — better to go out slightly too conservative than too fast, since an early fast mile is costly later. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general race execution/pacing discipline, though the explicit "don't skip nutrition just because you feel good" warning is a specific, actionable/fact-checkable claim)
- Tip 10 — "All you can give is all you have": frames worthy race-day effort (not just outcome) as the source of lasting pride, regardless of result. Illustrated with a personal story: 2016 Boston Marathon, developed a stress fracture (broken calcaneus) mid-race, walked the final 5 miles to a personal-worst marathon time, and describes feeling pride rather than needing "redemption" because he gave everything his body had that day. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general "process over outcome" sports psychology, but the personal anecdote is a strong, specific illustrative story)

## Episode #352: 5 Tips to Run Your Best Fall Race!
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode with Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Tip 1 — Purpose: distinguishes "why I run" from "why I want this specific goal"; exercise is to complete "I want my goal because ___" in one sharp, prioritized reason (not a list), tested with people who know you well (partner, friend, coach) as a "sounding board." Pitfalls named: not spending real time/iteration on this (should take weeks of drafting/refining), copying others' goals (e.g., wanting Boston "because everyone wants Boston") without personal meaning, and relying on someone else's motivation (a friend's or partner's) to sustain your own effort. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overlaps with general goal-setting/motivation psychology, though the specific "one sentence, tested by others" exercise and named pitfalls are a concrete tool)
- Tip 2 — Focus: pick one single A-race per season ("circle it, highlight it") and treat all training/prep races as *additive* toward it (e.g., an all-out tune-up 10K 4-6 weeks before a half, or a half marathon 4-8 weeks before a marathon — otherwise treat prep races as training runs, not all-out efforts). Claims half/marathon distances require single-peak focus (contrasted with 5K/10K, which allow up to ~3 peak races per season due to shorter recovery). Also: give proper detraining time between cycles (at least 3 weeks post-marathon, 1-2 weeks post-half) before rebuilding — training gains are "not linear," and skipping the interim valley prevents reaching a higher next peak. Named pitfalls: over-racing (often driven by FOMO or need for reassurance), and rushing straight into race-specific workouts without a proper base/priming phase. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization and race-specific peaking are core existing topics; the "which prep races are additive vs. detracting" framework and specific timing windows add practical specificity)
- Tip 3 — Consistency: emphasizes body-of-work over any single run; recommends running 5-6 days/week with clearly labeled day-types (1 quality/speed day, 1 medium-long run, 1 long run, 2 recovery days), aiming for 90-95% adherence rather than perfection. Names the medium-long run as "the unsung hero of half/marathon fitness" due to outsized aerobic benefit relative to injury risk, and explicitly warns against adding a *second* quality day per week ("revved-up four-cylinder" vs. "V8" analogy) — claims this produces short-term gains but limits long-term potential and raises injury risk. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY — four-cylinder vs. V8 engine) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overlaps with aerobic-base/periodization content, but the explicit "one quality day, not two" prescription and medium-long-run emphasis are specific, checkable claims)
- Tip 4 — Polarize pace: reiterates easy-pace rules (marathon pace +1-2 min/mi, half +1.5-2.5 min/mi) but specifically recommends *pushing recovery runs even slower* than the standard prescription (marathon pace +3-4 min/mi, half +3.5-4.5 min/mi, "even a brisk walk is fine") during this cycle as an experiment. Named pitfalls: "cheating" easy runs by creeping faster over the course of a run; comparing pace between easy runs as a proxy for a "better" run (explicitly says a slower easy run can be the *better* one); and failing to slow down further for heat/humidity — explicitly analogizes heat-induced pace slowdown to altitude-induced slowdown ("we'd never question slowing down at 10,000 feet, but we resist doing it at 90% humidity"). (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY — heat vs. altitude comparison) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — polarized training and effort-vs-pace concepts are standard, though the specific heat/altitude analogy and "slower run can be better" reframe are distinct teaching devices)
- Tip 5 — Incremental volume: caps peak-volume increases at ~20% cycle over cycle; recommends prioritizing added volume on long run and medium-long run first (claims later miles of long runs give outsized aerobic benefit vs. early miles), then quality day, then recovery days last (recovery runs already capped by movement/blood-flow purpose, not aerobic-benefit purpose). Explicit rule: never sacrifice effort/pace to hit a mileage target in a fixed time window (e.g., 45 minutes) — "time on your feet is time on your feet," run the correct effort and accept whatever mileage results. Also flags that total volume without "how days fit together" (proper day-typing/polarization) is a lesser priority than volume, and that recoverable volume is capped by life factors (sleep, stress) — pushing volume beyond recovery capacity is counterproductive. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — volume progression/aerobic base is a core existing topic; the specific "prioritize long run then medium-long run then quality then recovery" order and the "don't sacrifice effort for mileage in fixed-time runs" rule are concrete, actionable specifics)

## Episode #353: Listener Questions on Heat, Race Timing, Managing Injury and More

Diagnostic heuristic: urinating frequently and clear = over-hydrated relative to electrolytes (water passing through unabsorbed); dark urine = under-hydrated. Recommends returning to normal urination color/frequency as quickly as possible post-long-run/workout as a rehydration benchmark, and references a sweat test (pointing to episode 283) for heavy sweaters. (SCIENCE — electrolyte/osmosis mechanism asserted as physiological fact, not tied to a specific cited study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "clear/frequent urination = electrolyte deficiency, not over-hydration" diagnostic heuristic is a fairly specific, actionable, and somewhat counterintuitive practical claim that a general fueling/hydration section might not include)
