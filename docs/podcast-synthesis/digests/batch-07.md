## Episode #178: Maffetone Method Redux with Mallory Brooks
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Mallory Brooks — Rogue Running coach (coaches the trail program and Team Rogue morning group with Brad Hudson), co-owner of Spectrum Trail Racing (race director), personal experience undertaking an 8-week+ Maffetone-method training block.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Maffetone Method mechanics explained in detail: MAF (Maximum Aerobic Function) heart rate = 180 minus age, adjusted (-10 if recovering from illness/major detraining, -5 if inconsistent training, +0 if training >2 years without issue, +5 if training >2 years injury-free). All running done at or below this heart rate; no speed work until periodic 3-5 mile time trials plateau. (SCIENCE — attributed to Dr. Phil Maffetone; LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC, low-heart-rate/aerobic-base training is probably covered, but the specific formula and self-testing protocol could be a useful concrete addition) (POSSIBLE GAP for the specific formula/protocol details)
- Maffetone's own caution against "heart rate creep": don't let yourself drift a beat or two over MAF ("if you ask for 152, you're going to go for 153 or 154") — described via a "give a mouse a cookie" analogy. (PRACTICE, ANALOGY)
- Practical proxy for heart-rate-capped effort: "can I close my mouth and breathe through my nose?" — nasal breathing as a real-time check that you're at/under aerobic threshold, used instead of constantly staring at a heart rate monitor. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — nasal breathing as a practical effort-gauge is a specific, actionable technique)
- Reported results: two athletes (Mallory and a training partner, Devon) each improved ~30 seconds/mile at constant MAF heart rate over an 8-week block (with an estimated ~6% of that attributable to improving weather/temperature rather than fitness). (SCIENCE — n=2 anecdote, not a controlled study, self-reported)
- Diet, alcohol, sleep, and stress each strongly and immediately affected her ability to hold MAF heart rate — even one drink or <7 hours of sleep the night before made the low-HR run feel much harder ("if one or two of those things were off, I had a really hard time keeping my heart rate low"). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE-adjacent, self-reported) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery/lifestyle-affects-training, though this specific alcohol/sleep-vs-heart-rate linkage is a vivid illustration)
- Physiological substrate-utilization claim: after the MAF block, lab testing at a university kinesiology center showed she had become fat-adapted — burning fat from the start of a run and even during faster efforts, no longer needing gels/food on 2-3 hour runs. (SCIENCE — named institution "UT Center," un-named researcher; LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — fat adaptation via low-intensity training is a known concept)
- Guest's own retrospective critique/regret: she would add strides in earlier (she waited ~8 weeks before adding any turnover work and felt she'd "lost it," describing "cobwebs" in her hip/stride), and would extend the block to 4-5 months rather than the 3 months she did, citing Maffetone's view that up to 6 months is the ceiling for benefit. (PRACTICE, DISAGREEMENT — she disagrees with her own past execution) (ANALOGY: "growing cobwebs" in the hip from losing full stride extension)
- Host's own coaching add-on/disagreement: even during a full aerobic-base MAF block, he'd recommend keeping 6-8 short controlled strides per week throughout (not waiting for a plateau) to maintain fast-twitch activation — a mild divergence from strict orthodox Maffetone practice. (DISAGREEMENT, PRACTICE)
- Alberto Salazar quote/analogy relayed approvingly: "training is like soup — if you only have one ingredient, it doesn't taste good," used to caveat that a single-method aerobic block shouldn't be treated as a magic cure-all. (ANALOGY, quote attributed to Salazar)
- Recovery/heart-rate tracking tools discussed: chest straps preferred over wrist-based HR for immediate on-run feedback; Oura ring used to track overnight resting heart rate, HRV, respiratory rate, sleep score, and a "readiness score" as a daily training-readiness signal (e.g., detecting reduced readiness after alcohol the night before and adjusting effort down accordingly). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP if the site doesn't already discuss HRV/wearable-based readiness tracking as a training-regulation tool)
- Maffetone method described as only one "spoke" of Maffetone's broader multi-factor health model (also covering carbohydrates, inflammation, vitamin D, folate, stress, etc.) — guest recommends approaching the whole system, not just the aerobic/HR piece. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #179: Training Differences for Women
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Ruth England — Rogue Running co-founder and longtime coach, former collegiate swimmer, Boston qualifier. Jennifer Howard-Brown — Rogue coach and athlete for 12+ years, sub-4:30-to-Boston-qualifier progression, co-coach of the new women's half-marathon program. Both draw heavily on Dr. Stacy Sims' book "Roar" (women's exercise physiology).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central thesis, sourced from Stacy Sims' "Roar": "women are not small men" — women's training, fueling, and recovery needs differ qualitatively from men's, not just by scaling down calories/volume; industries (footwear, apparel — "pink it and shrink it") have historically ignored this. (SCIENCE — book/author named; POSSIBLE GAP if the existing site's fueling/training content is written generically without a sex-specific lens)
- Menstrual cycle phase framework: first ~14 days (from period start) = low-hormone phase, more "male-like" physiology — better heat regulation, better carb utilization, less muscle breakdown, easier hard efforts. Last ~14 days (luteal/high-hormone phase) = elevated estrogen/progesterone — impaired heat dissipation, higher core temp, sodium loss, faster muscle breakdown, bloating, reduced blood plasma volume (lower oxygen-carrying capacity to muscles, described via analogy to "EPO in reverse — but without the benefits"). Paula Radcliffe's 2002 Chicago world record while menstruating cited as an example of low-hormone-phase performance advantage. (SCIENCE — attributed to Sims/Roar and general physiology, with a named elite example; POSSIBLE GAP — likely not covered on a generic running-education site)
- Practical implication: track your cycle and consider modifying hard workouts/long runs around the luteal phase — not backing off entirely, but being aware that reduced performance in that window is physiological, not a personal failing or fitness deficit. (PRACTICE, DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the idea that feeling bad pre-period means "not being in shape") (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Recovery-window claim: women have a narrower post-exercise refueling window (~30 minutes) vs. men (60-90 minutes), because women shift out of fat-burning mode within ~3 hours post-exercise vs. up to 21 hours for men — meaning prompt carb+protein refueling matters more for women. Recommended recovery ratio: chocolate milk plus almonds (added protein/leucine) rather than the standard 4:1 carb:protein chocolate milk ratio alone. (SCIENCE — attributed to Sims/Roar; POSSIBLE GAP if the site's recovery-nutrition content is generic/male-normed)
- Amenorrhea (loss of period) risk factors and consequences: linked with greater stress-fracture risk, low iron, poor nutrient status; explicit disagreement with the historical practice of some elite programs/doctors manipulating athletes' cycles (e.g., birth control) specifically to suppress periods on race day — guests say this was "backwards" and that having a period is a sign of overall health, not an obstacle. (SCIENCE/DISAGREEMENT — explicitly reversing a past practice; POSSIBLE GAP, and relevant to any existing RED-S/amenorrhea content — flag for cross-check)
- Heat regulation nuance: women's resting core temp runs slightly higher (~97.8-98°F) than men's but extremities run cooler; women generally sweat less than men and struggle more with heat before acclimating, but once heat-acclimated, women's heat tolerance can exceed men's. Practical tips: pre-cooling (cold drinks before running), ice towels/cold fluids during exercise. (SCIENCE, attributed to Sims/Roar) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Menopause-specific effects: reduced estrogen/progesterone impairs fructose metabolism and increases insulin resistance, meaning common race fuels (Gatorade, gels, sports drinks — high in fructose) are more likely to cause GI distress and impaired hydration in perimenopausal/postmenopausal women; recommendation to shift toward whole-food complex carbs (fruits/vegetables) with more fiber. Also covers psychological effects — higher depression risk (linked to progesterone drop and rising cortisol/anxiety), brain fog/forgetfulness, and reduced desire for hard training — framed as physiological, not personal weakness. (SCIENCE, attributed to Sims/Roar research on fructose metabolism in pre- vs. post-menopausal women; POSSIBLE GAP — menopause and running is very likely an underserved topic on a general running-education site)
- Coaching-culture observation: women disproportionately come to running/coaching communities after major life disruption (illness, childbirth, divorce, loss) seeking community as much as instruction — informs the design of a dedicated women's coaching program. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if sports psychology/community is covered generally, but the specific gendered pattern may be a gap)

## Episode #18: Self Talk and Visualization
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Co-hosts Chris McClung and Steve (Rogue Running coaches) — no outside guest; framed as part of an ongoing "mental training" series using a "warrior"/battle-plan framework.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Self-talk defined as "the verbal and non-verbal conversations you have with or about yourself, consciously and subconsciously" — claim that people are unaware of roughly 70-80% of their own self-talk, and that most of it defaults negative in Western culture. (SCIENCE — asserted, no study cited, treat as a coaching claim not a research finding) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — self-talk is standard sports psychology, but worth checking whether this specific "70-80% subconscious" framing and stepwise protocol is already present)
- Concrete self-talk protocol: (1) build awareness by journaling/noticing negative words ("can't," "won't"); (2) interrupt negative chatter in the moment with a literal verbal/mental cue — "cancel, cancel" — treating the brain like software being reprogrammed; (3) write 1-3 positive affirmations and repeat them morning and night for one week at a time, cycling to new affirmations weekly, building to a full month; (4) identify people/situations that trigger negative self-talk and consciously avoid or "win" those moments; (5) keep messaging present-tense to short-circuit worry ("what can I do right now?") rather than future-oriented catastrophizing. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP if the existing content covers self-talk only conceptually without this specific step-by-step, dated cadence)
- Host's personal disclosure as a teaching example: describes his own negative self-talk spiral during a 5-month running layoff from a broken elbow ("why am I doing this... I'm getting old, give up"), used to illustrate that self-talk problems affect even confident, experienced athletes. (PRACTICE, anecdote)
- Fear-confronting protocol tied to self-talk: write down the specific fear (e.g., "letting my training group down"), name exactly who you fear disappointing, then investigate whether that's actually true — often the feared outcome doesn't hold up to scrutiny. Reframing suggestion: instead of fearing "letting people down," reframe as "what if I inspire them instead" — including in the case of a bad/failed race. (PRACTICE, ANALOGY — "boogeyman"/"bump in the night" analogy: fear examined directly usually turns out to be smaller than imagined)
- Visualization protocol (detailed, staged over ~2 months pre-race): (1) research the actual course/venue in depth — elevation, logistics, sensory details (sounds, smells, temperature), not just the running; (2) visualize the race in small chunks starting ~2 months out (e.g., just the start line experience first) rather than the whole race at once; (3) progress to visualizing chunks with adversity/problems deliberately inserted (e.g., a teammate falling back); (4) in the final weeks, do one long, complete, quiet visualization (can be done lying down) of the entire race start-to-finish ending in achieving the goal, mirroring how the host's wife's high school cross-country coach had the team visualize for an hour lying down with verbal cues. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — visualization is a well-known sports psychology tool, though this specific staged multi-week protocol with adversity-insertion is fairly detailed/actionable and could be additive)
- Visualization credibility anchor: references NFL QB Aaron Rodgers's quote that he'd "thrown that throw a million times" in his head before making a clutch playoff pass, as a case for why elite athletes use visualization. (ANALOGY/quote, named source)
- Real-world caveat about visualization: host describes his marathon PR race (BCS Marathon) where a last-minute race cancellation/switch (from Dallas, a course he knew intimately, to an unfamiliar course with two days' notice) invalidated all his prior course-specific visualization — recommends also visualizing a generic "fallback" response (e.g., "focus on breathing/relaxation") for unplanned disruptions, since detailed visualization can be undermined by unexpected circumstances. (PRACTICE, anecdote) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific advice to prepare a generic/adaptable visualization fallback for when reality diverges from the rehearsed scenario)

## Episode #180: Lessons from Base Training with Jason Brooks
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Jason Brooks — Rogue Running coach (co-owns Spectrum Trail Racing with wife Mallory Brooks), co-coach of Rogue's base-training program; conversation is a wrap-up/retrospective of a 16-week base-training program co-coached with host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- "Primacy of movement" as foundational strength philosophy: good movement patterns increase the body's capacity to tolerate training stress; strength training built on poor movement patterns is less effective — "if we are moving well, our body has a higher capacity for its stress load." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strength training for runners, though the movement-before-load sequencing emphasis may be a useful specific framing)
- Recommendation to periodically re-test baseline mobility (e.g., at the start and end of a 16-week training block) to track progress, with the caveat that mobility often regresses again once athletes shift into race-specific training and stop prioritizing it — described as normal, not a personal failure, and something even elite/Olympic athletes cycle through every season. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — periodic mobility re-testing as an explicit tracked metric)
- "Chop wood, carry water" repeated as the show's core mantra/analogy for training fundamentals: for strength, the "wood and water" is fundamental movement; for running, it's consistent easy running — i.e., the unglamorous basics matter more than advanced tactics. (ANALOGY) 
- Explicit claim: "consistent, easy running is the foundation of almost all running improvement" — easy runs, medium-long runs, and recovery days build the aerobic capacity that allows hard workouts to be effective; hard workouts are "sexier" but easy running is "the real glue." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base training, though probably already well covered on the site)
- Sleep: "sleep is not expendable time" and "the best recovery modality... full stop" — explicit reframing of napping/sleep-prioritization as a sign of discipline/performance orientation rather than laziness, including a personal anecdote about overriding work-culture stigma against napping. Also: circadian rhythm/chronotype alignment matters for stress management, and sleep quality can partially offset insufficient sleep quantity (though not as a long-term strategy). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sleep and recovery, though probably covered; the "napping stigma" framing and quality-can-offset-quantity nuance may be somewhat additive)
- Stress reframed as not inherently bad — "stress is just like pain, a signal... it's about the balance of stress and rest." Introduces a practical "stress inventory" tool (credited to sports psychologist Simon Marshall): objectively list your stressors, prioritize them, and triage each one by asking "what can I do about this?" — turns diffuse anxiety into concrete, actionable items. Combined with mindfulness practice (noticing physical/thought-based stress signals without judgment) to identify triggers before applying the inventory. (PRACTICE — tool explicitly attributed to sports psychologist Simon Marshall) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "stress inventory" tool as a named, reusable technique)
- Blood-value/underlying physiological deficiency claim: when an athlete is struggling for weeks despite normal training/lifestyle factors checking out, the cause is often an underlying blood-chemistry issue (e.g., vitamin D deficiency contributing to the host's own stress fracture at Boston 2016) that a standard panel might miss — recommends specialized athlete-focused blood testing (references a "Natalie" and prior podcast episode #169) rather than a generic doctor's panel. Quote/reference: "Dr. Moose" (a repeated podcast source) says in these cases "it's not in your head." (SCIENCE — practitioner-asserted, specific named resource episode #169; POSSIBLE GAP — specialized athlete blood panels beyond standard physicals)
- Nutrition takeaways: eat real, minimally processed, non-inflammatory food; don't overeat; experiment with in-run fueling strategy (solid vs. liquid, sugar/protein/fat balance) specifically during long runs since that's the best dress-rehearsal for race-day fueling; have a deliberate daily hydration strategy ("euhydration"). Notably paired with an explicit anti-perfectionism message — quoting Winston Churchill ("perfection is the enemy of progress") to argue against self-punishing over "bad" food choices, framed partly through body-positivity-movement influence. (PRACTICE, ANALOGY/quote) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, though the anti-perfectionism/body-positivity framing specifically applied to athlete nutrition might be a gap)
- Recovery reframed as an active, not passive, process: recovery happens through movement (easy runs, mobility work, walking, foam rolling) and refueling, not just "resting on the couch" — explicit distinction drawn between passively watching TV vs. foam rolling while watching TV. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery)

## Episode #181: Navigating a Running Funk
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode, Chris McClung (Rogue Running head coach) presenting a structured "decision tree" for diagnosing prolonged (2+ weeks) unexplained underperformance/fatigue/low motivation ("running funk"), synthesized from his coaching experience.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Distinguishes a "running funk" (lasting 2+ weeks, resistant to normal week-to-week adjustment) from ordinary bad training days, which don't require this level of intervention. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific 2-week threshold and structured diagnostic framework, as opposed to generic "listen to your body" advice)
- Structured diagnostic checklist/"decision tree" for a running funk, in this order: (1) Have training conditions changed — self-imposed (mileage/intensity/pace ramp-up, added strength) or external (weather/heat acclimation takes 3-6 weeks; general life stress) — distinguish "ride it out" (normal adaptation lag) from "back off" (ramped too fast, e.g., >10%/week mileage rule of thumb); (2) Is it a mental funk — motivation loss (reconnect to original "purpose" for running — competition, community, nature, stress relief — and/or inject novelty/fun into training), stress/mental fatigue (apply the stress-inventory tool), or loss of purpose amid race uncertainty (focus on process/what's controllable rather than unknowable race outcomes); (3) Is it physical — worn-out shoes (typically 300-400 miles, sometimes as low as 200; foam degrades even sitting unused on a shelf), running easy runs too fast (guideline: long runs ~1 min/mile slower than marathon pace or ~90 sec slower than half-marathon pace; recovery runs 2-3 min slower than marathon pace — should be adjusted further in heat), inadequate hydration/electrolytes (especially in heat — electrolytes needed for osmosis to move water into cells, not just plain water intake), inadequate/mistimed nutrition, and insufficient sleep; (4) If all of the above check out, suspect an underlying blood-value issue requiring specialized athlete blood testing (again references episode #169 and "Dr. Moose's" "it's not in your head" framing). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this ordered, elimination-style troubleshooting checklist is a distinct and directly actionable framework, likely more specific/systematic than generic troubleshooting advice)
- Warns that ignoring a persistent funk and continuing to push through can progress into full overtraining syndrome, citing elite runner Ryan Hall's retirement as an example of an athlete who couldn't dig out of that hole — recovery from true overtraining syndrome can take months to years, versus a funk which typically resolves in weeks with adjustment. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — named real-world example; LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overtraining syndrome is probably covered, but the "funk vs. true overtraining" distinction and escalation warning may be a useful specific angle) (POSSIBLE GAP for the graduated severity framing)
- Mentions "mental fatigue" affecting physical performance, citing a (unnamed-in-detail) Alex Hutchinson article on mental fatigue's effect on training/racing capacity — presented as a real phenomenon, not just anecdote. (SCIENCE — named journalist/source, though specific study not cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site already covers mental-fatigue-performance research, e.g., via Hutchinson's "Endure")
- Uses the 1980 US Olympic boycott as a historical analogy (via an offline conversation with Edwin Moses) for dealing with training uncertainty when a goal race may be canceled: those who "stayed consistent through a lost year" had later success, versus those who lost focus. (ANALOGY, named historical figure/context) (POSSIBLE GAP — historical sports-psychology analogy for training through uncertainty, distinctly framed)
