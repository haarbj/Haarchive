## Episode #155: Half Marathon Race Questions
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode with host Chris McClung (Rogue Running coach/founder, Austin, TX; ~20 years running, 26+ half marathons run).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Half marathon deserves equal status to the marathon as a training/racing goal; marathoners should cycle back to half-marathon-focused blocks periodically to get faster overall, and half marathon PR can be used as a diagnostic (via BQ-equivalent calculators) to check whether a runner is actually ready to attempt a marathon time goal. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — using half-marathon-to-marathon time-equivalence as a readiness gate is a specific, actionable technique)
- Half marathon taper: 1-week taper for most (cut volume ~20%, to ~80% of normal) or 2-week taper (cut 15% in week 2-out, another 15% in race week, to ~70%). Includes one "sanity workout" in race week — not for fitness, but to preserve sharpness/routine/mental calm. Example sanity workouts: 10x400m with 60-90s rest (4 at HM pace, 4 at 10K pace, 2 at 5K pace); or fartlek variants (90s on/2min easy at HM effort, or 5min on at marathon-to-HM pace with 3-4min easy). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — tapering generally, but the specific HM taper percentages/sanity-workout concept may be a useful specific addition)
- Race-day routine: shakeout run day before (30-45 min + strides), full rest day two days out. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Half marathon warm-up (contrasted with marathon, where he does no real warm-up): 1-2 miles easy jog (longer if cold), then 2x90sec-2min at HM effort, then activation drills (high knees, butt kicks, carioca, heel-toe walks), then strides. Rationale: HM requires being "on pace" almost immediately, unlike the marathon where early miles double as warm-up. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit contrast of warm-up strategy by race distance)
- HM pacing blueprint (flat course): start 10-20s/mile slower than goal HM pace for mile 1, ease down to target pace by mile 2 (advanced) or mile 3 (less experienced); hold steady mid-race (roughly miles 2/3–10); negative-split close over final 3 miles, progressively picking up pace each mile. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — pacing/negative splits, though the specific mile-by-mile framework is more granular than generic advice)
- Nutrition/hydration for HM is time-threshold-dependent: under ~90 min, often no in-race fueling/hydration needed if pre-race fueling was done; 90 min-2 hrs, hydration-only strategy is reasonable, nutrition optional; over 2 hrs, treat nutrition like a marathon (first gel ~45-60 min in, then every 35-45 min) but hydrate more assertively throughout. Adjust plan upward for heat once forecast highs reach ~60°F+. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, though the two-hour/90-minute threshold framework for the half specifically is a useful granular addition) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Mental tools for the half: still bring "purpose," mantras (rhythm mantra for early/middle miles, fight mantra for the final stretch), but can also simplify to a single mantra for the whole race (example: "every second counts"). Counting exercise (count to 30 repeatedly, reset) recommended especially for the final mile of shorter races like the half. "Going fishing" (reeling in runners ahead) still applies. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sports psychology/mantras, though likely already covered per the prompt's exclusion list)
- Novel to this episode: in local/recurring races, deliberately target beating specific known rivals from the local racing scene (not just anonymous competitors ahead) as an extra motivational/competitive strategy — works well in half marathons and race series because you'll often recognize the same competitors. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this "known-rival" racing tactic, distinct from generic "pick people off" advice)
- Recovery from a half: mirror the taper — ~1 week of reduced volume (50-60%) post-race, can return to 100% volume by week 2 (or week 3 if using a 2-week taper); be cautious reintroducing speed work in weeks 2-3. Recommends minimum ~4 weeks between two half marathons if racing them back-to-back in a season (4-6 week range workable); can use the first as a "training race" and the second as the aggressive effort. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery, though specific week-by-week half-marathon numbers add granularity)
- Anecdote/analogy: host frames "being both afraid and brave on the start line" (citing a Brené Brown talk) as a sign that a goal is appropriately big, and that fear is a "prerequisite for bravery." Also demonstrates that a race can be executed successfully by effort/feel rather than strict per-mile pacing, particularly on hilly courses. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology)

## Episode #157: Mileage Matters?
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode with host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core historical framing: high-mileage training as a foundational principle traces to Arthur Lydiard's work in the 1950s ("more running = faster," aerobic-system development: more mitochondria, more capillaries, improved blood oxygen-carrying capacity, improved lung function). (SCIENCE — physiological mechanism asserted, no specific study cited beyond attributing the training philosophy to Lydiard) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base training)
- Central thesis/pushback (attributed to Rogue coach Jen Harney): "miles matter" as a slogan is dangerous if treated as brute-force/context-free advice — a runner should not chase mileage targets just because another runner (or a training partner) is hitting them; mileage must be individualized to the runner's history, constraints, and recovery capacity. (DISAGREEMENT — explicit pushback against naive interpretation of a commonly-cited training principle) (POSSIBLE GAP — the framing of mileage-comparison/Strava-envy as a distinct psychological risk is a specific angle)
- Host's personal anecdote: fell into unhealthy self-comparison via Strava, comparing his ~2,200-mile year to others' 3,000+ mile years, despite already running a solid, individually-appropriate volume (50+ mpw average, 65-70 mpw in current training). Illustrates that even experienced coaches are susceptible to comparison traps. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Mileage progression rule of thumb: don't increase mileage by more than 10-20% (or roughly 5-10 miles) from one training cycle/year to the next; bigger jumps risk injury and overtraining. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general progressive overload/10% rule, commonly covered)
- Easy/recovery pace rule of thumb: run easy miles at least 1 minute slower than marathon pace, up to 2-3 minutes slower for recovery miles; injuries attributed to "more mileage" are often actually caused by running easy days too fast, not by the volume itself. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general easy-pace guidance)
- Weekly structure emphasized over total mileage: prioritize number of running days before total volume. Recommends 5 days/week (2 off) for half-marathon-or-shorter distance runners, with an aerobic cross-training day as an alternative 4th/5th day; 5-6 days/week (1 off) for marathoners. A 5-day week should include: 1 quality day, 1 long run day, 1 true medium-long run day, 2 recovery days. Argues medium-long run is the "unsung hero of fitness building" — e.g., prefers a 10-mile day + two 4-mile recovery days over three 6-mile days for the same weekly total, because the longer single effort delivers more aerobic benefit. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific claim that composition/structure of a week matters more than the weekly total, and the "unsung hero" medium-long-run framing, is a distinct emphasis worth capturing)
- Weekly structure should stay largely consistent year-round (same days/routine), with volume flexing based on training phase; this consistency is described as more important to long-term development than any single week's total mileage. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Cautions against the "you should always feel wrecked/exhausted when marathon training" misconception — a genuine near-exhaustion window should be limited to roughly the final 2-4 weeks before taper (peak phase); feeling consistently depleted outside that window signals overtraining, and a slightly lower but sustainable mileage level beats an unsustainable high one. (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly named misconception) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Host's personal example: pushing to 80-85 mpw led to a training plateau; backing off to a self-discovered "sweet spot" of 65-70 mpw allowed him to progress again — illustrating that more mileage isn't always better for a given individual even if physically tolerable (he notes he could once handle 90-100 mpw pre-kids). (PRACTICE)
- Emphasizes "individual response, history, current life constraints (sleep, stress, time)" as the real determinants of appropriate mileage — explicitly states there's no algorithm/black box that can calculate the "right" number; requires experimentation and self-observation over multiple cycles. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general individualization principle, though likely already implicit in existing site content)

## Episode #158: Coaching, Bodyweight, and Performance with Dena Evans
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Dena Evans — 20 years coaching at the highest levels of track and field; assistant coach under Vin Lananna at Stanford (6 years), then head coach of Stanford women's cross country (led 2003 NCAA XC title team); coached Sarah Hall and Lauren Fleshman at Stanford; 14 years with Team USA in various coaching/leadership roles at World Cross Country, World Relays, World Track & Field Championships; 12 years as club director/coach for Peninsula Distance Club (formerly Strava Track Club) coaching post-collegiate/elite development athletes.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Framing device for coaching conversations about weight/body composition: "emphasize what you should be doing" (performance, capabilities, outcomes) rather than "avoid, avoid, avoid" — i.e., don't build a list of banned topics/phrases, build a clear emphasis on process and results so appearance-focused talk becomes unnecessary. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific reframing technique for coach language around weight)
- On "race weight": pushes back on treating a target weight as a goal in itself — argues (echoing the host's own framing) that race weight is a byproduct of the right training/nutrition inputs, not a lever to pull directly ("cart before the horse"). Uses analogy: telling an athlete "your kick stinks, run faster" is not itself actionable — the actionable thing is the specific technical adjustments (cadence, arm position, posture) that produce the outcome; same logic applies to weight — the actionable levers are the day-to-day habits, not the number itself. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (DISAGREEMENT — explicit pushback on "lighter equals faster, so target a race weight" thinking, positioned against the general belief on the tension between weight and performance)
- Coaching approach: "context in the larger picture" — avoid isolating/spotlighting conversations about eating or weight, because doing so both (a) creates outsized stress/anxiety around the topic and (b) misses that concerning eating behavior usually reflects deeper issues (control, stress, other life factors), not just food choices. A narrow "just eat more/less" conversation is described as "a band-aid that doesn't fix the wound." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — nuanced coaching-conversation technique)
- Both host and guest describe deliberately declining to coach nutrition/weight directly as adult-athlete coaches — "I'm a running coach... whether or not you lose weight is a separate topic" — and instead referring athletes to nutrition/eating-disorder specialists in their network. Cited as their consistent practice across both interviewees. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit scope-of-practice boundary-setting as a coaching principle)
- On suspected disordered eating: recommends encouraging the athlete toward a trusted third party ("an ally") for these conversations rather than always trying to be that person yourself as the coach, partly because coach-athlete dynamics can complicate the athlete's ability to be fully honest, and partly because coaches aren't qualified therapists. Analogy: "a meeting with yourself" (self-coaching an injury decision) often produces a bad outcome absent outside input — same logic applies to eating-related self-monitoring. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- On menstrual cycle education: guest's single top piece of advice for a high-school athlete is to have open conversations with parents/coaches about the menstrual cycle early, rather than letting irregularities be dismissed ("she's just developing late," "it's just the running") until it becomes a bigger problem in college. Frames menstruation as a normal bodily function/health signal that should be discussed as routinely as an injury or a flu shot, not treated as taboo — echoes the idea that periods can be a "canary in the coal mine" for other health issues (RED-S/female athlete triad, referenced implicitly). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — RED-S/female athlete triad is probably already covered given the site's depth, but the specific "normalize discussing periods like any other health topic" framing/analogy is a fresh angle) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Historical/systemic observation: guest notes that in her own era (80s-90s), there was little formal education for coaches on the female athlete triad/RED-S; oral contraceptives were commonly prescribed somewhat casually to manage symptoms; overall a "Wild West" environment for coach-athlete conversations about body/weight. Athletics has historically been better set up institutionally to deal with male athletes over generations than female athletes, given the historically smaller female participation numbers — implying institutional knowledge/support lagged accordingly. (PRACTICE/historical context) (POSSIBLE GAP — history-of-the-sport angle on institutional gaps in supporting female athletes)
- Guest recounts an anecdote of a college recruiting coach telling her (a bigger-framed miler) "the big girl always beats the little girl in the last 100 meters" — meant as encouragement, but flagged by both guest and host as illustrating how body-focused comments, even well-intentioned or positive ones, can land very differently depending on the athlete and carry real risk. (ANALOGY/cautionary example) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Advocates for a longer-term view of an athlete's career (echoing Lauren Fleshman's op-ed referenced in the episode): U.S. high school/college systems put concentrated pressure on athletes precisely during a period of major physical development, whereas some other countries take a longer-term development approach; argues success should be expected to arrive in the post-collegiate years, not be treated as a make-or-break college outcome. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — long-term athlete development, though may overlap with existing periodization/LTAD content)
- Notes structural/institutional gaps: coaching certification (USATF, RRCA) is focused on training methodology ("X's and O's"), not on this category of topics; no consensus "shared set of values" yet exists across the coaching community for handling body/weight/health conversations; calls for athlete voices (citing AAC, athletes like Lauren Fleshman and Alysia Montaño) to drive standard-setting rather than top-down administrative programs alone. (PRACTICE/systemic observation) (POSSIBLE GAP)

## Episode #159: Long Term Planning
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode with host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central framework: plan running development in 18-24 month blocks, not just single 3-5 month training cycles, because (1) it ensures development of all needed systems toward a long-term goal, (2) gives each shorter block meaningful context within a bigger picture, and (3) sacrifices some short-term gains for larger long-term payoff. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific 18-24 month planning horizon, as distinct from a single macrocycle, appears to be a genuinely different planning angle than standard periodization)
- Analogy: uses the documentary "The Dawn Wall" (Tommy Caldwell's 6-year, ultimately 19-day free-climb of a previously "unclimbable" section of El Capitan) as an extended metaphor for long-term goal pursuit — the key parallel drawn is Caldwell's method of "deconstructing" the wall into small piece-by-piece sections via years of study, then reconstructing a viable route; host applies this to his own marathon goal (needing to "deconstruct and reconstruct" his body/training approach, not just incrementally extend the same 20-year approach, to break through a plateau). (ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — distinctive, memorable framing device for breakthrough/plateau-busting goal pursuit)
- Eight elements to include when doing 18-24 month planning: (1) think in 18-24 month blocks; (2) identify and work on weaknesses (host cites the general business-world "focus on strengths" advice and explicitly rejects it for distance running — argues neglected weaknesses cap your ceiling); (3) build in things that are fun/motivating, not just physiologically optimal; (4) train the full range of race distances (5K through marathon) rather than only race-distance-specific paces, because — per the host — "if you can run the 5K faster, you'll be a faster marathoner," recommends no more than 1-2 marathons in an 18-24 month window so other distances get attention; (5) work on only one primary objective per sub-block (can't train marathon-specific fitness and build a new strength habit at full intensity simultaneously) — quotes Eliud Kipchoge's "you can't chase two rabbits" as the guiding principle; (6) give each sub-block objective enough time to actually take hold — rule of thumb ~12-20 weeks minimum, with specifics: strength training needs ~6 weeks just to stop feeling disruptive to running and another ~6 weeks to normalize; full marathon-specific training blocks need 20-24 weeks, half marathon 16-20 weeks, 10K roughly 10-16 weeks; (7) build in explicit recovery windows (physical AND mental) — cites ~3 weeks post-marathon, ~1-2 weeks post-half/10K; (8) treat three things as non-negotiable across the whole 18-24 month span regardless of phase: weekly strides, a weekly long run, and routine (consistent days/pattern) — describes strides via analogy as "speed allergy shots," small ongoing inoculations of speed that keep fast-twitch fibers primed. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization generally, though the "8 non-negotiables/planning checklist" structure and specific week-count minimums for each block type add useful granularity) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "can't chase two rabbits"/one-objective-per-block rule as an explicit named principle)
- Host applies the framework to his own goal (sub-2:40 marathon from a 2:45 PR at age 40): plans a dedicated ~4-month Olympic-lifting/power-strength block first, followed by a dedicated 5K-focused speed block (goal: break 17:00, something he's never done), reasoning that his weaknesses are raw speed (unused since his soccer-playing youth) and raw strength/power (has only done body-weight injury-prevention work, never true heavy/Olympic-style lifting) — explicitly states he cannot work on both simultaneously per the "one objective per block" rule. (PRACTICE — self-disclosed personal example, illustrative rather than universally generalizable)

## Episode #16: Women and Running
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Jen Harney — Rogue training director/coach for TeamRogue Cedar Park, longtime coach, former competitive runner. Mallory Brooks — Rogue trail coach, race director for Spectrum Trail Races. Also features co-host Steve (UT Women's Distance Team coach for 7 years, cross country/track 5K-10K).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Menstrual cycle and training: both guests report they generally do not adjust training/racing plans around their period and actively avoid using it as an excuse, framing it as a mental-toughness/mindset issue ("as soon as I use it as an excuse, then I'm doing myself a disservice") — but do make practical accommodations (clothing choices, timing/planning around major races, increasing iron intake proactively before races when a period is expected to coincide). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — first-person athlete accounts of managing training through menstruation are a distinct, underrepresented topic for a coaching site)
- Coach Steve's coaching approach with a college women's team: made clear every athlete could discuss period-related issues with him if needed, but did not proactively probe; noticed teammates living together tended to synchronize cycles, so shifted from 21-day to 28-day training-cycle planning to reduce the chance of a "serious episode" coinciding with championship races. States this coordination broke down once athletes moved off-campus into different housing groups. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — cycle-synchronization-aware training-cycle planning is a genuinely novel/practical scheduling tactic)
- Iron/anemia: recommends women get iron levels checked regularly via a doctor; if borderline/low, supplement with liquid iron (absorbs faster), split into two daily doses (morning/evening), paired with vitamin C for absorption, and NOT taken with coffee or soda (interferes with absorption). Notes iron deficiency isn't female-specific — endurance athletes of any gender doing high-mileage training are at risk. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling/micronutrients, iron for endurance athletes is a fairly standard topic likely already covered)
- Anemia recovery timeline and reframing: cites secondhand advice from a UT team physician — recovering from anemia takes roughly 2 weeks before feeling better, another 2 weeks before results improve, another 2 weeks to be race-ready (~6 weeks total). Notable coaching reframe/analogy: treat training while anemic like altitude training — don't hammer training, but don't stop either; back off intensity targets, keep doing consistent work by effort rather than pace, understand the fatigue has an identifiable physiological cause rather than attributing it to poor sleep/stress. Also cautioned that doctors are often more conservative about return-to-training timelines than is strictly necessary, per this coach's experience. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "anemia is like altitude training" reframe is a specific, memorable, actionable analogy)
- Birth control and training: individualized/trial-and-error process (different birth control methods can have different side effects — e.g., one guest gained ~10 lbs on a Depo shot and now avoids hormonal birth control). Key actionable rule stated explicitly: do NOT change your birth control method within 6-8 weeks of an important race, because it introduces an uncontrolled variable during race-critical prep. One guest describes using continuous active-pill use (skipping placebo week) to delay a period around a specific competition — with the caveat "consult your doctor." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the explicit 6-8-week pre-race "don't change birth control" rule is a specific, actionable, non-obvious piece of guidance)
- Body image/eating disorders: guest Jen Harney (coached in the early-90s era) describes a personal history of coaches (both female) directly instructing her to "lose weight," and describes teammates with eating disorders (bulimia, one teammate dropping out of college) as a normalized part of that era's team culture. Describes her own long-running body image distortion (believing she was "fat" when she wasn't) resolving partly through motherhood/reframing around gratitude for what her body could do. (PRACTICE — personal narrative) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — likely overlaps with existing eating disorder/RED-S content given the site's stated depth, though firsthand narrative account may add color)
- Coach Steve's "healthy, happy, strong" mantra/framework for disordered eating: describes using this three-part check (are you healthy medically? are you happy/in control of your life circumstances? are you strong/performing?) as a way to open conversations about disordered eating without being able to discuss nutrition directly (he states he was formally barred from discussing nutrition with UT athletes following a prior program-wide eating disorder crisis in the mid-90s). Frames disordered eating explicitly as fundamentally a "control" issue, not a food issue, and coaches by working with athletes on other areas of life where they can exercise legitimate control (sleep schedule, academics, relationships) rather than addressing food directly. Describes a case where this approach coincided with an athlete's return from a multi-year disordered-eating episode to being an NCAA-meet scorer. Notes his most severe disordered-eating case as coach was actually a male athlete. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "healthy, happy, strong" mantra and the "work on other controllable areas of life, not food directly" technique are specific, nameable coaching tools) (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly notes eating disorders aren't female-exclusive, pushing back on an implicit assumption)
- Observation on gendered pressure around body weight/image: host and guests describe the societal pressure as directionally different by gender — men in distance running are more often told they're "too skinny" and pushed to add weight/muscle to look "normal," while women face the opposite, near-constant pressure to be thinner, reinforced by media imagery. Guest cites an informal "attractiveness scale" anecdote (a spectrum of 10 female body types) where women tend to select leaner/curvier-but-not-thinnest images as most attractive/aspirational while men in the same exercise tend to select thinner images — used to argue "healthy is sexy" should be the standard, not thinness alone. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — candid firsthand postpartum-running physical detail, e.g., stress incontinence as a common but rarely-discussed lasting effect, is a genuinely underserved topic for a coaching site)
- Strength training for women — explicit myth-debunking: guests state that lifting weights will not cause women to unintentionally "bulk up"/gain significant muscle mass, framing this as a common fear that has no physiological basis absent very deliberate, sustained hypertrophy-focused training. (SCIENCE — general exercise-physiology claim, asserted without a named study) (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly named misconception: "lifting will make women bulky") (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strength training for runners, though the explicit "bulking up" myth-debunking framing for female runners specifically may be a useful angle if not already covered)
- Running safety for women: concrete practical tips — avoid running alone at night if possible (run with a partner instead); never run with headphones; stay hyper-aware of surroundings/exits; tell someone your route and expected return time before a solo run; prefer well-lit neighborhoods over isolated trails for solo running (reasoning: easier to seek help/be seen); mixed views on pepper spray (one guest was told by police that under panic, people are statistically more likely to spray themselves than an attacker without practice); explicit safety advice if attacked: fight back and scream. One guest recounts a personal connection to a sexual assault survivor from a local trail attacker case, used to underscore that a baseline level of situational fear/caution is healthy rather than naive. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — women's running safety is very plausibly an entirely missing topic on an exercise-physiology/training-focused coaching site, and this is fairly detailed, actionable, firsthand-informed guidance)
- Anecdote/behavioral pattern flagged as a caution for coaches: a college coach recounts a highly competitive female athlete who was unknowingly turning nearly every easy/social run into an uncontrolled race against random men on a shared trail (repeatedly surging to drop strangers), which was silently sabotaging her structured training (couldn't hit prescribed tempo pace) until the coach discovered the pattern by running with her — used to make a broader point that unstructured "training runs" can covertly become high-intensity efforts, especially in competitive, mixed-gender public running spaces, undermining a training plan without objective data (e.g., HR/Strava) to catch it. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, illustrative failure mode of easy-run pace discipline being broken by ego-driven "silent racing," relevant to easy-day compliance broadly, not just gender-specific)

## Episode #163: The Case for Base [Building] with Jason Brooks
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Jason Brooks — Rogue Running coach in Austin, co-launching a new base-building podcast training program with host Chris McClung; described as the show's "strength expert" in this and other episodes; uses HRV, blood biochemistry testing, and dietary experimentation in his own and his athletes' training.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core case for dedicated base-building blocks (as opposed to always training toward a specific race): gives the body time to heal after a big goal cycle; provides a lower-stress, lower-risk window to introduce new training stressors (strength training, dietary changes) without compromising race-specific prep; and restores broader life balance (family/work) that intense race training often displaces. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — dedicated, standalone base-building programs as a distinct concept from generic "base phase of periodization" — the framing here is explicitly non-race-specific and open to any runner regardless of goals)
- "Metabolic flexibility" and fat adaptation: guest describes base-building as an ideal window to work on the body's ability to switch between fat and glycogen fuel sources, potentially experimenting with lower-carbohydrate, ketogenic, or paleo approaches during lower-intensity training, framed as a way to "ferret out food inflammation problems." (SCIENCE — physiological concept named, but no specific study/researcher cited; presented as the coach's applied framework) (POSSIBLE GAP — ketogenic/low-carb periodized experimentation tied specifically to base-building windows is a distinct, non-mainstream nutrition angle that may not be covered, though it's also a claim some sports nutrition sources would push back on)
- Explicit "don't chase two rabbits" rule applied to base building: warns against simultaneously starting a ketogenic/very-low-carb diet (e.g., 20g carbs/day) while beginning marathon training — cites this as a repeated real mistake he's seen athletes make. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — implicitly pushes back against combining major dietary overhauls with race training, reinforcing episode #159's "one objective per block" principle)
- Base-building volume-building philosophy: prioritize adding running days before adding mileage on existing days; claims (from ~10 years, "hundreds, maybe thousands" of athletes coached) he has never encountered a runner who couldn't be brought up to at least 4 running days/week, and that most claims of "I can only handle 3 days/week" reflect running those days too hard rather than a genuine physiological ceiling. Recommends 4-6 days/week as a base-building target depending on lifestyle constraints, with 5 days ideal (allows for a dedicated speed day, long run day, medium-long day, and 2 recovery days). (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly challenges the common runner belief "I can't handle more than 3 days a week," reframing it as a pacing problem, not a durability limit) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general training-days-per-week guidance, though the explicit "it's not durability, it's running too hard" reframing is a sharper, more specific claim than generic advice)
- Speed work should never be fully dropped during base building (contrasted explicitly against old-school Lydiard-style "pure base = only easy miles" thinking) — recommends two categories: (1) short, controlled speed-development work with generous recovery (flat strides, short hill strides/repeats, 200m on/200m off) to "keep the neuromuscular pathways open" ("keep the lights on"), and (2) aerobic-strength/endurance work (steady runs, effort-based short tempo runs) to build sustainable comfortably-hard pacing capacity. Frames base building as "earning the right to really train" — establishing both an aerobic and neuromuscular foundation before layering in more specific, harder work.
