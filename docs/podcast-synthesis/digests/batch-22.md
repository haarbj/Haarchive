## Episode #306: The "Why" Behind Slowing Down to Get Faster
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung, owner/coach at Rogue Running (Austin, TX), longtime marathon coach who developed this philosophy partly after his own stress fracture training for his first marathon in 2000.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Strength-training corollary: just as lifters do high-rep/lower-weight work most of the time and only occasionally test a 1-rep max (e.g., 60 lbs for reps to build toward a 100-lb max), runners should do low-intensity/high-volume work most of the time and only occasionally "test the max" via races/time trials. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base rationale, though the specific strength-training parallel is a distinctive teaching device)
- Car-engine analogy: easy running "adds cylinders" (builds engine size/aerobic capacity), while speed work/tempo/fast running "fine-tunes" the existing engine. You need both, in sequence, within each training block (build size, then fine-tune, then race, repeat). Athletes who run too fast on easy days get stuck as a "revved-up four-cylinder" that plateaus rather than growing into a V8/V10/V12. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/aerobic base, but the "engine size vs. tuning" framing is a reusable teaching metaphor)
- Four specific reasons to run easy on easy days: (1) puts you in the correct aerobic-capacity-building zone (as opposed to zones that fine-tune other aerobic subsystems); (2) reduces musculoskeletal/neuromuscular stress so you can stay healthy and train consistently for years/decades; (3) lets you get more out of hard workouts by properly polarizing training; (4) improves running-form efficiency at slow paces, which transfers to efficiency at fast paces — running fast is "naturally efficient," so form flaws are exposed (and can be fixed) at slow paces. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for #1–3 aerobic-base rationale; #4 — the claim that slow-pace running exposes/fixes form inefficiencies that transfer to fast paces — is a more specific, less commonly stated angle) (POSSIBLE GAP for the form-efficiency-at-slow-pace point specifically)
- Physiological detail on what changes during easy-effort aerobic development: improved lung oxygen uptake, improved O2 transfer from lungs to bloodstream, improved blood O2-carrying capacity, capillary growth into working muscles, increased mitochondrial density, and improved respiration efficiency within mitochondria. States this aerobic development can continue over "15 to 20 years" of consistent training. (SCIENCE — asserted as physiological mechanism, no specific study or researcher cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — standard aerobic-adaptation physiology)
- Anecdotal/folk physiological claim: the "itching sensation" some runners feel in legs (or stomach) after returning from a running layoff is attributed to dormant capillaries reopening and blood flow returning, causing nerve firing. Presented as the host's own explanation, not sourced to a study. (SCIENCE — asserted, unsourced) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific, memorable physiological folk-explanation not typically covered)
- "Effort is king, not pace." Easy runs should be governed by the talk test (full sentences, no breathlessness) rather than a pace target; the same effort can correspond to very different paces day to day (e.g., 30–60 sec/mile slower in summer heat/humidity) and this doesn't make the run "worse." Runners should stop measuring themselves by whether they ran an easy run faster than last time — that may be a sign of failure (going too hard), not success. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — talk test/RPE-based easy running is standard, though the explicit reframe of "faster easy run = possible failure signal" is a sharper articulation)
- Rule-of-thumb paces: easy/long runs at least 1 min/mile slower than marathon pace (or 90 sec/mile slower than half-marathon pace) or slower; recovery runs at least 2 min/mile slower than marathon pace (or 2.5 min/mile slower than half pace) or slower. Recovery runs are pushed further into the slow end of the range specifically to reduce musculoskeletal trauma, even though the aerobic benefit across that whole range is similar. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Heart-rate rule of thumb: 180 minus age as a max HR ceiling for easy runs (host, age 43, cites 137 bpm ceiling); recovery runs ~115–125 bpm; other easy runs ~125–135 bpm. Host states he trusts pace-based cues more than this HR formula. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — though specific numeric zone breakdown adds detail)
- Claim: easy/recovery-run paces don't necessarily get faster over time even as race times improve dramatically — sometimes they get slower as an athlete "learns" to run easier. This is framed as normal and not something to worry about or use as a success metric. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the common runner impulse to benchmark easy-run pace progress) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific reassurance/myth-correction doesn't appear to be a commonly covered talking point)

## Episode #307: Understanding RED-S with Tina Muir
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Tina Muir — former professional distance runner (British; competed collegiately and professionally in the US for ~9 years while amenorrheic), host of the "Running for Real" and "Running Realized" podcasts, creator of a YouTube video series on RED-S, author (at the time) of an upcoming book "Becoming a Sustainable Runner." Not a physician or dietitian herself — she compiled input from named experts (a dietitian referred to as "Nancy") into her resource, and speaks partly from lived experience.

**Concepts & claims:**
- RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport) is defined as insufficient fueling relative to combined athletic + life energy demands, causing the body to downregulate systems it deems non-essential to protect core function. Explicitly stated to affect all genders, all body sizes/weights, all performance levels — not just "skinny elite runners." (SCIENCE — presented as established sports-medicine framework, no specific study cited by name; note RED-S itself is a well-defined IOC consensus term) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — RED-S/energy availability is arguably already a "general fueling" adjacent topic, but likely under-covered in the depth given here) (POSSIBLE GAP if the existing site content treats fueling only performance-wise and doesn't go deep on RED-S specifically)
- Symptom list beyond amenorrhea/low testosterone: cold extremities (Raynaud's-like symptoms), insomnia (notably even after very long/hard runs), digestive issues, low libido, anxiety/being "on edge" (attributed to the brain being under-fueled and in a resource-scarcity stress state), frequent illness, and recurrent bone-adjacent injuries (femoral, sacral, hip, pelvic stress fractures, Achilles/patellar tendinopathy) as "bright red flags." (SCIENCE — asserted from personal experience + general RED-S literature, not tied to a specific named study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the insomnia-as-RED-S-symptom point in particular is a less commonly flagged connection)
- Key myth debunked: "I eat a lot, so it can't be RED-S." Athletes compare their intake to non-runner friends/family and feel they're overeating, without accounting for the caloric cost of hours of training. Muir notes even elite ultra/endurance athletes (cites Mike Wardian's transcontinental run) struggle to eat enough despite trying hard. (DISAGREEMENT/PRACTICE — explicitly named misconception) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Other named myths: RED-S is a "women's issue" only (false — affects men via low testosterone and non-binary people); RED-S only affects elite/high-mileage athletes (false — can affect first-time marathoners, or anyone with combined life+training stress e.g. moving house, divorce, early postpartum/breastfeeding, or people who simply "forget to eat" during busy stretches). A deficit as small as ~300 calories/day (one granola bar or bowl of oatmeal) can be sufficient to cause it. (DISAGREEMENT/PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "life stress + inadvertent under-fueling" pathway, independent of training load, is a distinct angle from typical athlete-fueling content)
- Diagnostic pathway: general practitioners often just say "stop running" (unhelpful and often untenable for competitive athletes); recommends an OB-GYN to rule out structural issues and an endocrinologist to get bloodwork confirming flatlined (non-cyclical) reproductive hormones; a sports dietitian to assess actual fueling. Advises being brutally honest with providers about real (not "good day") eating patterns. Recommends assembling a "recovery team": doctor, dietitian, and often a therapist, since the root issue is frequently psychological (perfectionism, "hedonic treadmill" of chasing the next goal, body image) rather than purely nutritional. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "recovery team" framework and the point that fixing calories alone often doesn't resolve the underlying disordered relationship with food/body is a specific, actionable angle)
- On recovery approach: Muir's own case (complete cessation of running/exercise for ~3 months, no exercise, significant weight gain, acupuncture) led to fast recovery (pregnant within ~10 weeks), but she now believes this was likely an extreme/atypical case. She cites (unnamed) "studies coming out now" suggesting continued moderate exercise during RED-S recovery may be better than total cessation, since total stopping can create its own obsessive rebound. Typical recovery timeline cited by her endocrinologist: 3–6 months. Practical first step suggested: try adding 300–500 extra calories/day (leaning toward breakfast) before escalating to professional intervention. A tracked physical sign of recovery in women: change in cervical fluid consistency across the cycle. (SCIENCE — "studies show" invoked without specific citation; DISAGREEMENT in the sense that it revises her own earlier practice) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Discussion of race weight culture: cites Ryan Hall as an example of an athlete fixated on a specific race-weight number (~137 lbs) who later described that fixation as damaging to his performance and mental health. Argument: the mental cost of obsessing over a precise number likely outweighs the marginal performance benefit of being at a specific weight. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on rigid "race weight" thinking) (POSSIBLE GAP — a named-athlete cautionary example about race-weight obsession)
- Coach's role: recommends athletes proactively engage coaches (and family) in RED-S conversations, including educating coaches on menstrual-cycle-phase-based training and nutrition, since periods represent hormonal balance that supports (not hinders) performance — directly contradicts the idea in "parts of the running world" that not having a period is preferable/better for performance. Mentions Christine Yu's book "Up to Speed" (chapter "Period Power") as a resource. (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly named misconception that amenorrhea is performance-neutral or beneficial) (POSSIBLE GAP — menstrual cycle and training/fueling periodization is plausibly missing from a typical exercise-physiology-focused site)

## Episode #308: Q&A on Marathon Training with Nicole Winter
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Nicole Winter — running/fitness Instagram influencer (~84K running followers, ~100K strength-training followers) trained by host Chris McClung for her first marathon and current Boston-qualifying attempt; not a coach herself in this episode (she interviews Chris, flipping the show's usual format). Substantive content below is attributable to Chris McClung, owner/coach at Rogue Running.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Advice on finding a run coach: certifications like USATF or RRCA coaching certs indicate some foundational knowledge but can be earned via a single weekend seminar and aren't strong evidence of practical coaching skill. Better signal: real coaching experience with athletes, vetted via word-of-mouth from local running stores, run clubs, or other runners — not just someone posting running content on social media. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general coaching-selection advice, though the specific certification critique is a useful specific)
- Post-race recovery rule of thumb: roughly one day of easy movement/recovery per mile raced before resuming structured training (~2 weeks for a half marathon, ~3 weeks for a marathon) — walk first, then easy running, no speed work. If a half marathon is the "A race," give it its own full recovery+rebuild cycle rather than treating that block as marathon-prep; if used as a "build race" toward a marathon, schedule it 4–8 weeks out from the full and treat it as a training opportunity, not a focus race. Body can only "peak" 2–3 times per year. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/tapering-adjacent, though the specific 1-day-per-mile recovery heuristic and "A-race vs. build-race" framing are concrete, reusable rules of thumb)
- On heart-rate training: useful but has two caveats — (1) individual variability means formula-based zones can be "wildly off," so proper testing is preferable; (2) wrist-based optical HR is only directionally accurate, especially in cold/windy conditions — a chest strap is needed for reliable data. Personally prefers pace-based training because it's easier to calibrate and measure without requiring special equipment or testing. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — asserted, not study-cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — HR training basics, though the optical-vs-chest-strap accuracy caveat is a specific practical detail)
- Cadence: pushes back on rigid adherence to "180 steps/minute" as a universal target — that's an average, and taller/shorter runners will reasonably vary (rule of thumb: 170–190 depending on height/leg length). More useful diagnostic: relative cadence — does cadence increase or stay level when you speed up? A cadence that decreases when picking up pace is a red flag for overstriding/relying on power rather than turnover, associated with injury risk and poor foot-strike mechanics. Fixing this requires drills/strides over time rather than conscious cadence-counting. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — explicitly pushes back on the common "180 is optimal" claim) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — running form/cadence, though the "relative cadence as diagnostic" framing is a more specific angle than a fixed-number rule)
- Top 3 tips for first-time marathoners: (1) have a plan or coach; (2) run easy enough on 85–90% of runs — the most common mistake is progressively running long runs at goal marathon pace rather than easy effort, which risks injury/plateau; (3) fueling/hydration becomes critical at the marathon distance specifically (unlike the half) because "hitting the wall" around mile 20 is fundamentally a fuel-depletion event requiring external carbohydrate intake — practice race-day fueling on every long run over 2 hours. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling/base training, standard content)
- 20-mile long run philosophy: Rogue's approach is 4–6 runs of 20+ miles per marathon block (even for newer marathoners), occasionally up to 22 miles for more experienced runners, to build "muscular resilience" and aerobic foundation; caps around 22 miles because beyond that runners are already covering more time-on-feet than they will on race day at faster pace. Also builds in 2–3 marathon-pace segments within some 20-mile runs. Contrasts with some "high-profile" plans that cap long runs at 16 miles. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — implicitly contrasts with lower-mileage philosophies without naming them) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — long run structure, standard marathon-prep content)
- Definition of "speed workout"/quality session: anything run faster than easy pace, including track intervals, tempo runs, and fartlek ("speed play"). Recommends only one quality session per week for most athletes, even elite ones, because the biggest fitness gains come from the easy/aerobic volume. Quotes Frank Shorter (Munich Olympic marathon gold medalist) as saying "hills are speed work in disguise." Uses training-phase structure: priming/base phase → aerobic strength phase → race-specific phase → taper, each with a different quality-work focus. (PRACTICE — with a named quote/analogy from Frank Shorter) (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization phases and speed-work variety, standard content)
- Pain vs. injury triage rule of thumb: acute pain (fracture/tear/pop sensation) → see a doctor before running again. Soft-tissue pain rated ≤3/10 that doesn't worsen during a run is generally OK to run through (and running can promote healing via blood flow); pain above that threshold or worsening during the run → stop, consider cross-training, see a local PT/chiro/orthopedist familiar with runners. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific numeric pain-scale threshold for "safe to run through" is a concrete, actionable rule of thumb that may not be explicitly stated on a typical site)
- Missed-workout protocol: miss 2–3 days → just resume the plan where it left off, no makeup. Miss a full week → restart at ~75% planned volume for one week, then back to 100%. Miss two weeks → 50% week, then 75%, then 100% (3-week rebuild). Miss more than two weeks → treat as needing to rebuild from closer to scratch and consult a coach. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific percentage-based return-to-training progression is a concrete, actionable protocol not commonly spelled out)
- Marathon goal-pace estimation methods: (1) race-performance calculators (e.g., McMillan Running) extrapolating from a recent shorter-distance race; (2) a standalone 2–3 mile time trial fed into the same calculators; (3) practicing marathon-pace segments (2–4 miles) within long runs/workouts and gauging whether the effort feels sustainable (finishing feeling like you "could have gone a lot longer" is a good sign; tapped-out or spiking heart rate is a sign pace is too aggressive). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race-pace prediction, fairly standard content)

## Episode #310: Mastering the Marathon (especially Boston) with Nell Rojas
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Nell Rojas — professional marathoner (top American finisher at consecutive Boston Marathons, top-10 New York City finisher), daughter of Rick Rojas (won the first Bolder Boulder, beating Frank Shorter), former NCAA runner at Northern Arizona University, former competitive triathlete, and a practicing coach (exercise science degree) who coaches both recreational and sub-elite marathoners.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Boston-specific long-run/workout design: describes a 22-mile long run that opens with roughly a 1-mile marathon-effort warmup, 5 miles at marathon-pace+45-60 sec (altitude-adjusted) on downhill terrain, 5 miles at true marathon pace, 5 miles at marathon-pace+30-45 sec through rolling/hill terrain, then a final 5 miles at marathon pace on a downhill finish, cooling down with an easy mile — explicitly designed to simulate the Boston course's downhill start, mid-race hills (Newton Hills), and downhill finish under fatigue. Host describes his own team runs a near-identical simulator-style workout using Austin terrain. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a specific, transferable "race-simulation long run" design for a point-to-point downhill-heavy course, a level of specificity beyond generic long-run guidance)
- Downhill-specific race prep: "if you can't master downhill running, you might as well not practice uphill running" — Boston-specific training should include weekly downhill-specific quality work (strides, downhill segments) starting early in a 12-week build, scaled individually since downhill tolerance varies a lot (some athletes get knee pain and need to scale back). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — downhill-running-specific training progression is a narrower topic than general strength/hill training)
- Strength training debate for downhill/eccentric loading: describes her own evolution from prescribing pure eccentric-loading work (which still left her quads "busted") toward incorporating isometric holds (sustained loaded positions) based on the idea that tendons need to lengthen while muscles need strong isometric contraction; she explicitly says "I don't have the answer" and currently lands on consistent single-leg squats, heavy deadlifts, and plyometrics (if healthy) as her practical answer. (SCIENCE/DISAGREEMENT — presented as an evolving, uncertain practice, not settled science; she explicitly flags the uncertainty) (POSSIBLE GAP — eccentric vs. isometric loading for downhill-running preparation is a fairly specific strength-training angle, likely beyond generic "strength training for runners" content)
- Aerobic foundation and career-long development: makes the point that many elite peers have been building aerobic base since age 12-14, so a coach evaluating a newer runner should recognize they haven't built that multi-year foundation yet and needs to prioritize base-building even if it "seems boring" or "copy paste." Attributes part of her own marathon success to a prior competitive triathlon background that built a large aerobic engine (she describes it also psychologically distorting her sense of what training "should" feel like, since marathon training now feels comparatively "too easy"). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — asserted from personal experience, not study-cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base building generally, though the "years of accumulated aerobic foundation" framing for evaluating a new athlete's readiness is a specific coaching heuristic)
- Easy-pace definition and enforcement: uses conversational pace and a numeric RPE scale (asks athletes to self-rate on a 1-10 scale) combined with heart rate; notes new athletes commonly self-report an "8" RPE on what should be an easy run. Personal example used as a calibration tool: her own easy pace as a ~2:25 marathoner is 8:30/mile — cited by the host as useful perspective for recreational runners who might be running faster than that on easy days despite being far slower marathoners themselves. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy-pace/RPE training, standard content, though the "compare your easy pace to elite marathoners' easy pace" reality-check is a nice concrete illustration)
- On over-training vs. under-recovery: identifies "over-training and under-recovering" (or training appropriately but under-recovering) as the single biggest destroyer of marathon build-ups and cites her own last two marathon cycles as examples of under-recovering. Describes tracking athlete fatigue via comments/logs in TrainingPeaks and proactively prescribing down weeks. Names general life stress (job, family) as equally impactful to recovery capacity as training stress itself — "the body thinks stress is stress." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery/overtraining content, though "life stress counts the same as training stress" is a useful explicit framing)
- Mental approach to Boston/racing: recommends running the first 10K at goal-marathon-pace minus roughly 5 seconds (i.e., slightly conservative) regardless of downhill start, evaluating from 10K to 15 miles whether goal pace feels sustainable, expecting and accepting time loss on uphills rather than fighting them (recover on the following downhill instead), and only pushing in the final 10K (mostly downhill/flat at Boston). Explicit philosophy: "let the hill slow you down... you're not going to get your time there... you're going to only potentially destroy your ability to go chase at the end" (host's framing, echoed by Rojas). Broader mental framework: process/effort goals over outcome goals, compartmentalizing race distance into manageable chunks, and deliberately not publicizing time/placement goals to the media to avoid external pressure. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general race-pacing/mental-performance strategy, though the specific "goal pace minus 5 seconds for first 10K, hold through 15 miles, accept uphill time loss, attack final 10K" pacing protocol for a specific course archetype is a level of tactical specificity worth flagging) (POSSIBLE GAP for the Boston-specific pacing template as a standalone, reusable "downhill-marathon pacing strategy")
