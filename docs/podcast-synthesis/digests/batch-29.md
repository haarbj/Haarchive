## Episode #365: Staying Present in Training and Racing
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung (owner/head coach, Rogue Running, Austin; 21-marathon finisher, coach of BQ and elite-adjacent athletes).

**Concepts & claims:**
- "The hay is in the barn" (attributed to Bill Bowerman): once you're inside ~10 days of a marathon, no more fitness can be built — the taper's only job is arriving healthy and sharp, not adding fitness. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — tapering, but the Bowerman quote/framing is a nice memorable line)
- Concrete injury/racing decision rule of thumb: if pain is 3/10 or less and doesn't worsen during the run, it's generally okay to keep running (at least until diagnosed); if it's 4+ or climbs to 4-5 during the run, stop and see a professional. Cross-training (biking, swimming, elliptical) can replace minutes (not miles) 1-for-1 under the same pain threshold to preserve aerobic stimulus while injured. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, actionable pain-scale decision rule for "should I run through this" is more concrete than typical general injury-management advice)
- Race-day/injury decision-making principle: defer the go/no-go decision as long as possible (e.g., decide morning-of or even mile-to-mile), rather than deciding weeks out; use each day's data (how the body responds) to take "the next logical step" rather than trying to solve the whole problem at once. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY: "stay present," "one day at a time")
- Distinction between "training results" for the day's stated purpose vs. improvising: a medium-long run's purpose is easy aerobic-capacity building (at least ~1 min/mile slower than marathon pace, ~1.5 min slower than half pace); a recovery run should be run even easier (2-3+ min/mile slower than MP) specifically to promote blood-flow-driven healing, not to bank extra fitness. Deviating from the day's intended purpose (running recovery days too hard, skipping easy days) is framed as the main reason athletes underperform relative to their training. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy-day/recovery-pace guidance, though the specific minute-per-mile offsets are a usable, quotable detail)
- In-workout decision rule for rep-range workouts (e.g., "6 to 8 x 800m"): progress rep-by-rep and ask after each one "can I do another and still feel I have one more in the tank?" — stop when pace or form starts to degrade (cited signs: pace falling off, shoulders tensing, sloppy arm swing, knees/ankles clipping each other). Always finish a workout feeling you could've done one more, rather than exhausted. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete, ready-to-use form/fatigue checklist for when to end a set is a specific coaching tool worth capturing)
- Recommends training oneself to run "by feel" (breathing, heart rate sensation, muscle tension, sweat rate) rather than being watch-obsessed, framed as foundational to being able to make good real-time training/racing decisions. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — RPE/running by feel, though usually covered)
- Two coping modes for pre-race nerves: "associative" (channel anxiety into concrete prep — course-map study, race plan, nutrition/hydration plan, logistics, visualization) vs. "dissociative" (deliberately distract — watch a movie/show) — both are legitimate ways of staying present rather than spiraling. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY)
- In-race pacing principle: never over-correct after an off mile. If you run a mile too fast or slow relative to plan, treat it as one data point and simply try to hit the next mile on plan — don't blindly "roll with it" if ahead of pace, and don't panic-correct if behind. Applies to workouts too (react to an off-pace rep by aiming to nail the next one, not chase the average). Only allow deliberate faster-than-target surges in the final ~3 miles of a half / ~5 miles of a marathon. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific "use the last mile as a calibration point, don't overcorrect" rule is a fairly precise race-execution tactic not always spelled out this explicitly)
- Anecdote/claim: how a runner feels at any given mile does not reliably predict how they'll feel miles later — host describes a marathon PR where marathon pace felt terrible/labored for the first ~8 miles (cold morning) before suddenly feeling sustainable, with no bad miles after; used to argue against "if it's this hard now, it'll be this hard at mile 20" catastrophizing. (PRACTICE, presented as personal anecdote rather than SCIENCE) (POSSIBLE GAP)

## Episode #366: Balancing Running and Life with Listener Nicole
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Nicole Ledesma — longtime age-group athlete/former Rogue Running client (not a coach or scientist); returning to structured training after a multi-year hiatus involving marriage, a cross-city move, caregiving for a parent with Huntington's disease, and fertility treatment (egg retrieval/IVF).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Coaching framework for athletes restarting after a long life-driven layoff: set the weekly-volume/schedule bar at a level that is "doable and sustainable," identify explicit "pressure release valves" (specific days/miles that can be cut first when life intrudes) in advance, and aim for hitting the plan ~80-90% of weeks rather than 100%, reframing that partial-adherence rate itself as the definition of success. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the concrete framing of naming pressure-release valves in advance, and defining "success" as a hit-rate rather than perfection, is a specific coaching technique for adherence during disrupted life phases)
- Coaching philosophy: keep long-term "dream" goals alive but hold them loosely, and deliberately avoid comparing current training capacity to a prior peak version of oneself — the past training blueprint "is never going to be recreated" and using it as a comparison point is described as actively demotivating. (PRACTICE)
- Suggested use of a personal affirmation/mantra to interrupt negative self-talk during a comeback ("there's peace in the process") — a technique of naming the intrusive thought, not judging it, then redirecting to the immediate next step. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — mantras/self-talk, general sports psych)
- Explicit acknowledgment of tension unique to some athletes: coming back from time off (e.g., fertility treatment, caregiving) can trigger guilt about "selfishly" prioritizing performance goals; coach's counter-framing is that returning athletes, especially mothers, often come back stronger ("mom power is a real thing") and that individual striving supports rather than detracts from showing up well as a parent. (PRACTICE, framed as coach's belief/experience rather than cited research) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific psychological tension around fertility treatment/caregiving and athletic identity is a distinct angle a running-education site likely doesn't cover)
- Practical note: coach recommends de-prioritizing strength training entirely during a fragile re-onboarding period, to avoid overloading a runner who is just trying to re-establish running consistency. (PRACTICE)
- Advice to not tie a comeback block to a specific goal race when motivation/consistency is the priority — a race deadline was explicitly described as an added pressure that had previously caused all-or-nothing blowups (train hard or quit entirely). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — "sometimes NOT having a target race is the right prescription" cuts against the site's likely default assumption that all training is race-oriented)

## Episode #368: What Social Media Gets Wrong about Run Coaching
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung, sparked by a public disagreement between running coaches (Steve Magness and "Stage Canada") and neuroscientist/podcaster Andrew Huberman over a kettlebell-carry endurance workout Huberman shared (from a conversation with strength coach Pavel Tsatsouline).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Critiques a viral Huberman/Tsatsouline recommendation (jog 1 mile weekly carrying a kettlebell at 30% bodyweight) as an out-of-context "one workout" pulled from a broader strength-focused conversation, and as inappropriate/risky as an endurance-building tool. (DISAGREEMENT) (PRACTICE)
- Core thesis #1: "No one workout matters" — consistency of the overall body of work over years/decades is what builds fitness; any single workout (viral or otherwise) should only be adopted if it fits a periodized plan, never copied wholesale out of context. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/consistency, standard theme)
- Core thesis #2: rejects the idea of "hacks"/"biohacks"/shortcuts (products, tools, supplements) that promise to bypass the years-long physiological adaptation process; frames long development timelines as "encouraging" rather than discouraging. (PRACTICE)
- Core thesis #3: pushes back explicitly on "David Goggins"/extreme-effort fitness culture — claims ~99.5% of training time should not be near physical limits; building a big aerobic engine (not a "revved-up" one) requires staying in control most of the time, and only race day should approach true limits. (DISAGREEMENT) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — polarized/easy-running philosophy, though the explicit anti-"go to failure" framing and rough 99.5% figure is a notable specific claim)
- On recovery culture: dismisses ice baths, protein powders, and paid recovery gadgets as distractions from the free fundamentals (sleep, easy movement/blood flow, whole-food fueling). States plainly "the science related to ice baths and recovery is not very good." (SCIENCE claim, asserted without a specific citation) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery fundamentals)
- Names Dr. Susanna Soberg (Danish scientist who studied Danish cold-water swimmers) as a credible source on cold/heat exposure protocols: recommends small doses — roughly 11 minutes total per week of cold exposure across 3 sessions, water in the 50-55°F range (not extreme cold) — for metabolism/stress-response benefits, and notes she also covers heat exposure benefits. (SCIENCE — named researcher, specific protocol numbers) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete, named-source cold-exposure dosing protocol is more specific than typical generic "ice baths might help recovery" coverage)
- Revisits and extends a "Zone 2" critique (references his own episode #344, "myths about Zone 2"): argues social media oversimplifies training-zone talk into a narrow target heart-rate band, when in his view "Zone 1" (lower than Zone 2) is actually more important for building the easy-volume foundation, and effort/feel should guide zone work more than rigid heart-rate ranges. (DISAGREEMENT with mainstream Zone-2 social media framing) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "Zone 1 matters more than Zone 2" argument is a specific, contrarian-adjacent claim distinct from standard aerobic-base messaging)
- Argues against racing frequently / chasing "cool kid" content-driven race calendars: to reach max potential, athletes should peak only 2-3 times per year around a single focused "A" race per ~5-month cycle; any other races run in that window should be structured to be additive (long run pace, workout pace) rather than competing peaks. Notes running influencers race often mainly because they're paid/sponsored to generate content, not because it's optimal for performance. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/race focus, standard theme)
- Pushes back on the "you must chase all six World Marathon Majors to be a legitimate/serious runner" social-media narrative — argues smaller/local races are equally valid goals, and that Majors' logistics (travel, crowding) are often not conducive to running one's fastest possible time. (DISAGREEMENT) (POSSIBLE GAP — an explicit counter-narrative to Majors-chasing culture, which is a distinctly social-media-era phenomenon)
- Argues specialization (not the "hybrid athlete"/functional-fitness trend) is required to reach max potential in any one discipline — e.g., a marathoner should treat strength training as a supplement to running, not a co-equal pursuit, unless the actual goal is a hybrid event like HYROX. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — direct pushback on the "hybrid athlete" trend as it applies to distance-running goals is a fairly current, social-media-specific angle)
- Final theme: explicitly counters social-media body-image pressure — "fitness does not have a look"; elite and everyday performance come in all body shapes/sizes, and chasing an aesthetic seen on social media is framed as actively harmful/misleading. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — body-image messaging specific to running culture, distinct from general nutrition/fueling content)

## Episode #369: Listener Questions on Burn Out, BMI, Creatine and More
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo listener-Q&A episode by host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- On weight/BMI (listener "Jake"): argues body weight is an *output* of healthy training/fueling habits, not an input to manipulate directly; recommends against scale-focused management and toward consistent volume + adequate fueling, deferring precision weight management only to a sports nutritionist if pursued at all (references his own episode #222, "the mythology of race weight"). (PRACTICE, with a DISAGREEMENT undertone against "manage your weight to run faster" framing) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, though the explicit "weight is an output not an input" framing is a clean, reusable one-liner)
- On running tangents (listener "Jake," running ~0.6 mi long in a marathon): explains marathon course-certification measures the exact shortest legal path around every curve, so overrunning is essentially unavoidable — a well-executed tangent effort should land around 26.4-26.5 miles (400-600m over), not a full kilometer over. Recommends using the USATF certified-course-database maps (which include hand-drawn notes on lane restrictions/tangent quirks) to pre-study a course's specific tangent challenges before race day. Gives a strategic exception: in crowded races (example: Chicago's early turns), intentionally running *wider* than the tangent to avoid getting boxed in by the crowd can be faster than hugging the mathematically shortest line. Key technique: keep eyes up scanning for the straightest path rather than following the curve of the road or the runners ahead of you (observed many runners unnecessarily "following the curve" at Austin and Houston marathons). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the USATF certified-course-map research technique and the "wide tangent in crowded early miles" exception are concrete, non-obvious race-tactics content)
- On mileage/burnout (listener "Molly," burns out above 30 mi/week): primary hypothesis is that easy days aren't being run slow enough — restates guideline of ≥1 min/mile slower than marathon race pace (or ≥90 sec slower than half pace) on easy days, and 2-2.5+ min slower on recovery days; frames "how easy can I make my easy days" as the real lever for unlocking higher sustainable volume (references episodes #291 "is your training polarized enough" and #306 on running slow). Secondary checks: adequate post-workout/post-long-run fueling (a full balanced meal, not just a bar), and not overreaching in speed workouts (train at current fitness, not goal-race paces — validate via a 2-mile time trial plugged into a race calculator). Tertiary: rule out underlying issues like iron-deficiency anemia via bloodwork if the above are all in order. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — polarized training and easy-day pacing, though bundled into a concrete differential-diagnosis checklist for "why do I burn out")
- On supplements (listener "Lee," re: creatine and ketones): personal stance is to avoid only what WADA/IOC actually bans (caffeine and legal ergogenic aids like sodium bicarbonate — mentions a newer buffered bicarb product designed to reduce the classic GI upset — are fine to consider); does not view creatine or ketones as "performance-enhancing" in a moral sense since they're naturally occurring. States creatine has strong research backing for muscle development/mass maintenance (particularly with aging) primarily from strength/bodybuilding contexts, but says the specific evidence for creatine benefiting marathon-distance runners is still inconclusive ("jury is still out"). References a named source for a deeper creatine dive: Megan Lyons (health coach, "The Lyons Share Wellness," podcast "Wellness Your Way") did a dedicated creatine episode with brand/dosage recommendations. On ketones: describes elite athlete Des Linden's use of Ketone-IQ gel packs mid-race, said to be aimed less at fuel and more at sustaining mental sharpness/focus late in a race; no strong claim of proven running-specific benefit. (SCIENCE claims with a named researcher/source pointer for creatine; anecdotal for ketones) (POSSIBLE GAP — supplement-specific guidance, especially creatine-for-endurance-athletes and the WADA-legality framing for decision-making, may not be covered by a general nutrition section)
- On recovery-modality prioritization (listener "Sujata"): reiterates the "big three" — sleep, easy movement/blood flow, and post-hard-effort fueling — as by far the most important recovery levers, ahead of ancillary modalities (foam rolling, Theragun, massage, chiro, ice baths). Cites Christie Aschwanden's book *Good to Go* (author was a guest on episode #110) as concluding that most ancillary recovery modalities lack strong scientific efficacy, and recommends treating them as personal-preference add-ons rather than priorities. Host's own personal routine: foam rolling 2x/week targeting known tight spots (calf, quad) partly as an ankle-mobility/injury-prevention tool, plus a monthly chiropractor visit for proactive alignment/mobility work. (SCIENCE claim with named author/book cited; remainder PRACTICE/personal anecdote) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery fundamentals, though the named book/author is a useful citable source if not already used)

## Episode #37: Running True or False with James Dodds
**Guest(s) and credentials:** James Dodds — longtime Rogue Running coach (called "the Reverend"), described as strong on the mental/psychological side of coaching. Co-host Steve (Rogue Running co-founder/coach) also participates. Casual, unstructured "true or false" banter format recorded informally (hosts note having had a few beers); much of the episode is philosophical riffing rather than actionable content.

**Concepts & claims:**
- On footwear and injury: general agreement that shoes are not a reliable injury-prevention lever on their own ("if you're injury-prone, you'll be injury-prone in any shoe") — but personal anecdotes are offered on both sides: one coach (James) reports his own calves tightened significantly and impaired a workout after switching to zero-drop shoes, and his wife developed lateral knee pain after inadvertently running in stability shoes she wasn't used to, resolved by returning to her usual neutral shoe. The only claim presented as "definitive science" is that rotating between multiple pairs of shoes reduces injury risk more reliably than any single shoe type/model choice. (mix of PRACTICE/anecdote and an asserted-but-unsourced SCIENCE claim about shoe rotation) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — running shoes/injury, though the "shoe rotation itself is the only well-evidenced factor" framing is a specific, fact-checkable claim)
- On training philosophy (Jack Daniels reference, named by Steve): describes Jack Daniels' VDOT system as intentionally preventing coaches from training athletes at a paces/VDOT level they haven't actually proven in a race — i.e., "you are only as good as your last result" from a *training-prescription* standpoint, even though as a person/athlete's ceiling is broader than one data point. Steve recounts a personal coaching failure at University of Texas where he "cheated" athletes' VDOT upward based on optimism, and those athletes' performance fell apart late-season as a result. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — a named methodology (Jack Daniels/VDOT) with a specific, checkable claim about the failure mode of over-crediting fitness) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training paces/VDOT, common in serious running-science content, but the specific "train at proven fitness, not aspirational fitness" cautionary anecdote is a usable teaching example)
- Debate over "the marathon is more mental than physical": mixed views — two coaches argue it's primarily mental once adequately physically prepared (pacing discipline early in a race determines whether the back half is survivable; athletes rarely reach a true physical limit before quitting mentally); one coach argues the opposite — that it is the physical breakdown that *creates* the mental crisis, so physical preparedness is causally primary. Presented as genuinely unresolved among the three. (DISAGREEMENT, framed explicitly as such) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology re: suffering/pacing, though rarely framed as this specific unresolved either/or debate)
- On genetic/physiological limits: general agreement that every runner likely has a hard genetic ceiling, but practical consensus that virtually no recreational (or even most competitive) runners ever get close enough to it for the ceiling to be a meaningful training consideration — cites the historical pattern of "impossible" barriers (4-minute mile, marathon sub-2:03 era jumps) being broken once believed achievable, suggesting some limits are partly self-imposed/psychological ("artificially created limitations") rather than purely physiological. Eliud Kipchoge's sub-2-hour marathon attempt (unofficial, pace car/rotating pacers, non-record-eligible conditions) is discussed as evidence it's physiologically possible under engineered conditions, though one speaker asserts unsubstantiated suspicion of doping. (mix of SCIENCE-adjacent claim and speculation/opinion — no actual named study, "central governor" theory referenced only in passing) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — VO2 max/physiological ceilings, though the Bannister-four-minute-mile analogy as evidence for the psychological-limitation angle is a notable teaching analogy) (ANALOGY: four-minute-mile barrier)
- On variety vs. consistency in training: agreement that "variety is the spice of life/training" is broadly true (adaptation requires progressive variable changes: pace, recovery interval, terrain, shoes, routes) but coined counter-phrase "consistency is underrated" — illustrated with elite masters runner Allison Maxie(sp.) as an example of an athlete whose long-term consistency, not workout novelty, is what sustains continued PRs into older age. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/progressive overload, standard theme, though the "variety is the spice, but consistency is underrated" pairing is a clean quotable synthesis)
- Nothing else of substance — much of the episode is sports-fandom banter (LeBron James, English press treatment of Mo Farah), philosophical tangents about fate/religion, and a discussion of whether "running is for everyone" that resolves informally to "yes, evolutionarily, though not necessarily as anyone's preferred exercise modality." Low signal-to-noise for a coaching-content mining pass; flagged mainly for the handful of bullets above.

## Episode #370: Managing the 99 Percent + Final Boston Prep Tips
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung, opening with a reaction to comments made by professional athlete Des Linden on a separate podcast ("Nobody Asked Us with Des and Kara").

**Concepts & claims:**
- Frames a "99%/1%" model of training priority, inspired by a paraphrased Des Linden comment about sodium bicarbonate: elite athletes who've already nailed the fundamentals for a decade-plus can reasonably chase the last 1% (supplements like bicarb); everyday athletes chasing 1%-tier interventions while neglecting fundamentals have their priorities backwards. Lists the "99%" explicitly: easy/consistent volume as the top priority, periodized quality work layered on top (peaking 2-3x/year), stress/rest balance within the week (hard day/easy day structure with dedicated recovery runs), sleep/fueling/hydration fundamentals, appropriately paced speed work (training at current fitness, not aspirational fitness), community/coaching support, defined mental-side purpose and race-day mantra/plan, negative-split race execution, and a long-term, mistake-tolerant view of the process. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — this is essentially a restated "fundamentals checklist," overlapping conceptually with episode #368's "no one workout matters"/anti-shortcut framing from the same host — a recurring theme across this batch)
- On 50K-vs-marathon training differences (listener "Jennifer," road ultra): recommends extending the longest long runs to 24-25 miles (vs. marathon's ~20-22), with 5-6 total 20+ mile long runs in a build, at least two of which reach 24-26 miles; treats nutrition/hydration as increasingly critical as distance/duration extends. Gives a concrete sweat-test protocol: weigh before/after a 1-hour run, each pound lost = 16 oz fluid loss, replace ~80% of that per hour during the race; also targets roughly 200 calories/hour (his own ultra-fueling example: ~10 peanut M&Ms/hour for ~100 cal plus ~100 cal from sports drink). Recommends matching long runs to course-specific terrain (rolling hills) in advance. (PRACTICE, with a concrete/replicable calculation method) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific sweat-test math and per-hour fueling/hydration formula is a concrete, actionable protocol worth flagging if the site's fueling section doesn't already include a formula like this)
- On trail ultra prep specifically (extending the above): emphasizes time-on-feet and elevation-gain-matching over raw mileage, plus carrying capacity for nutrition/hydration between sparser aid stations; personal example of training for a 50-mile trail race with 10,000 ft of gain/loss by using the steepest available road hill in Austin (400 ft of gain per mile) combined with separate rolling-trail runs to combine vertical-loading and technical-footing stimulus, since Austin lacks both together in one location. (PRACTICE)
- On establishing a "baseline" year-round mileage floor (listener "Kelsey," inconsistent weekly mileage/boom-bust cycles): recommends defining a non-race-cycle baseline weekly schedule (e.g., a specific medium-long, speed, long-run, and recovery-run mileage total) that becomes the year-round floor an athlete returns to after every taper/recovery period, rather than dropping to near-zero between training cycles. States two build-up rules: don't increase a training cycle's peak mileage by more than ~20-25% over the previous cycle's peak (comparing peak-to-peak, excluding anomalous double-long-run weeks), and don't increase week-to-week mileage by more than roughly 3-5 miles (an alternative framing to the "10% rule"). Frames maintaining this floor across multiple 6-18 month blocks (rather than treating each race cycle in isolation) as key to long-term progress, based on his own early-career pattern of losing months to complete stoppages between builds. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — progressive overload/the 10% rule, though the specific "peak-to-peak 20-25%, week-to-week 3-5 miles flat, year-round floor mileage" framing is a more concrete alternative to the generic 10% rule that a site might want to include as a variant)
- Boston Marathon-specific final prep, three concrete protocols: (1) Eccentric-loading/downhill prep — cites "relatively recent science" (unnamed/uncited) claiming easy-effort hill running provides eccentric-loading benefit comparable to running downhill at goal pace; prescribes adding easy up/down running on a 4-6% grade hill during medium-long runs for 6 weeks (weeks 7 through 2 out from race day), keeping heart rate fully in check/slow rather than turning it into a hard workout. (2) Heat acclimation — a 4-week sauna/hot tub protocol (weeks 7-2 out), 2-3 sessions/week of ~20 minutes, ideally post-run; offers an alternative for those without sauna/hot tub access: deliberately over-layering clothing on some easy runs to simulate heat stress. (3) Course simulation — recommends 1-2 long runs in the final month that specifically mimic target-race terrain (Rogue's own "Boston simulator" course in Northwest Austin is cited as an example), to build both physical and mental readiness for specific terrain like Newton's Hills. (SCIENCE claim asserted without a named study for the eccentric-loading point; remainder PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a fully fleshed-out, dated, step-by-step Boston-specific hill/heat prep protocol is quite specific and actionable; likely a genuine gap versus a general "hill training" or "heat acclimation" treatment, since it packages exact weekly cadence, session length/frequency, and grade percentage)

## Episode #373: To Marathon or Not to Marathon with Listener Marisa
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Marisa — 25-year-old recreational runner/listener (asset management professional, not a coach or scientist) with roughly one year of consistent training (~1,000 miles/year, ~20 mi/week), a half-marathon PR of 1:39, and a prior metatarsal stress fracture from an overly aggressive marathon build-up.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core advice: recommends delaying a first marathon attempt for a relatively new, lower-mileage runner (~20 mi/week) in favor of 1-2 more years spent building aerobic volume and racing shorter distances (5K/10K/half) first, arguing this produces a stronger long-term marathon outcome than rushing into 26.2 early; frames marathon training blocks as "disruptive to consistency" because of the ~3-week taper + ~3-week recovery window that shorter races don't require. Host explicitly says he regrets doing this himself (went from a 10K to a marathon within ~2 years and suffered his own stress fracture). (PRACTICE, includes explicit self-critical anecdote) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general "don't rush the marathon" advice, though the explicit "3 weeks taper + 3 weeks recovery = 6 weeks of consistency lost per marathon cycle" framing/math is a specific, quotable justification)
- Proposes a concrete 2-year mileage-doubling roadmap: ~1,000 mi/year → ~1,500 mi/year in year one → ~2,000 mi/year in year two, alongside racing shorter distances during that window, before attempting a marathon in "year three." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific multi-year annual-mileage roadmap tied to "when should I run my first marathon" is more concrete than typical generic guidance)
- On metatarsal stress fractures specifically: states they are comparatively rare and, in his coaching experience, usually indicate either a biomechanical issue (e.g., a forefoot-striking gait loading the forefoot heavily), a footwear mismatch, or significant volume overload — as distinct from higher-up-the-leg stress fractures, which he associates more often with nutritional/bone-density factors. Recommends a running-specific gait/form analysis (with a PT experienced specifically in running biomechanics, not a generic/"cookie cutter" analysis) as a proactive follow-up after such an injury. (PRACTICE/clinical opinion, not attributed to a named study) (POSSIBLE GAP — differentiating stress-fracture likely-causes by anatomical location, and the forefoot-striker-specific risk claim, is more granular than general "stress fractures come from overload" content)
- On race-distance psychology: pushes back on the idea that a first marathon's only goal should be "just finish" — argues a target time (even a conservative one) is still necessary because racing smart/pacing well requires having a plan, and that goal should just not be gripped "too tightly." Also argues (as a DISAGREEMENT with popular perception) that shorter, faster races (5K/10K/half) are underrated in difficulty/suffering relative to the marathon, and are sometimes harder because the anaerobic pain is more intense even though shorter in duration — first exposure to that specific kind of suffering in a training cycle typically "wins" a little before the athlete adapts to it over repeated efforts a few weeks apart. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race-day mental performance, general, though the specific "5K suffering underrated vs. marathon suffering" claim is a distinct, arguable angle)
- On weekly structure specifics: reiterates one dedicated speed session/week is sufficient (even for advanced runners) because aerobic capacity, not speed, is the limiting factor for most distance runners; recommends filling non-workout days with strides or short pickups (30-90 sec) to keep speed "primed" without taxing the system. On strength-training placement: prefers pairing lifting with hard running days (same day, after the run) to preserve easy days as true recovery days — but if that's not schedule-feasible, picks two other fixed days and holds them consistently, since "the body remembers the routine." Explicit rule for moving a missed workout: it's fine to shift a speed day to another day if it preserves the hard/easy sequence (e.g., swap with the following day's medium-long), but never shift a hard session into a slot that would remove the buffer before the next hard effort (e.g., pushing a missed speed day into the day before a long run). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — hard/easy sequencing and strength-training-for-runners placement, standard theme, though the explicit "safe vs. unsafe workout-rescheduling" rule is a specific, actionable decision framework)
