## Episode #236: 5 Tips for Training in the Heat
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung (Rogue Running coach, Austin, TX), drawing on personal coaching experience training athletes through Texas summers.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Heat acclimation takes roughly 3-5 weeks; during this window athletes should let go of pace expectations entirely and avoid checking their watch, especially on easy runs. (PRACTICE)
- Core reframe: judge training by effort, not pace — in heat/humidity, equivalent training effort can require paces 20-40 seconds/mile slower than cooler-weather baseline. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general effort-vs-pace framing, but the heat-specific magnitude is a more novel data point)
- Rule of thumb given (explicitly called "crude," not derived from a named study): for every 5°F above 55°F at run/race start, add ~10 sec/mile to maintain equivalent effort (e.g., 75°F start = +40 sec/mile). Presented as a heuristic he's found "closely approximates" real heat/dew-point calculators. (SCIENCE — asserted heuristic, no named study or researcher; more coach-derived rule of thumb than peer-reviewed) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete pace-adjustment formula for heat, more specific than most general fueling/training content)
- Athletes who chase old paces in the heat risk training in the wrong aerobic zone, overreaching, burnout, or injury — described as a common failure mode. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overreaching/injury risk)
- "You're fighting physics and chemistry" — heat is framed as friction disrupting metabolic/respiratory efficiency, hence unavoidable pace slowdown. (ANALOGY)
- Practical strategy: train key hard efforts in late-summer heat at slightly slower than goal race pace, then take "a leap of faith" that cooler race-day conditions will let the same effort produce faster paces — described as psychologically hard but reliably borne out in his coaching experience. (PRACTICE)
- Clothing: in humid climates, evaporative cooling essentially stops working, so exposing skin (going shirtless / sports-bra only) outperforms any technical fabric; recommends Craft's ultralight base layer as the one technical fabric he's found tolerable in humidity (for those who prefer not to bare skin, e.g. wearing a hydration pack). Mentions Austin's "Sports Bra Squad" group as a body-positivity/normalization resource. (PRACTICE)
- Hydration protocol: recommends 3-4 liters/day of water outside exercise, roughly a third+ with electrolytes; during runs, aim to replace ~80% of sweat loss (not 100%). Gives his own sweat rate (~2 lb/hr = 32 oz lost, replacing ~25 oz/hr) as an example, and notes sweat rate varies hugely between individuals (cites a friend losing 3x as much). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general hydration/electrolyte fueling is probably covered, but the specific sweat-test protocol and 80%-replacement rule may be a useful specific addition)
- Sweat-test protocol given step by step: weigh nude before a 1-hour run with no fluids, weigh again after; weight lost in lbs × 16 oz = hourly sweat rate. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete self-testing protocol, likely more actionable/specific than a generic hydration section)
- Urine-color heuristic: faint yellow = well hydrated/balanced electrolytes; too yellow = underhydrated overall; too clear (especially with frequent urination) = insufficient electrolytes, meaning water passes straight to the bloodstream/kidneys rather than being absorbed into cells via osmosis. (SCIENCE — physiological mechanism asserted but no researcher/study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if general hydration is covered, but urine-color-as-electrolyte-signal is a more specific/actionable detail)
- Body carries additional water weight (his estimate: ~5 lb) during heat acclimation as a physiological adaptation to support increased sweat needs. (SCIENCE — asserted, no citation)
- Mental/psychological tip: develop a personal "heat mantra" to reframe frustration ("the heat makes me stronger") — ties heat training to a broader claim that heat training may mimic some altitude-training adaptations (he flags this as not "one for one" and not rigorously supported). (PRACTICE / SCIENCE, hedged) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sports psychology/mantras, but heat-specific framing is a natural cross-reference)

## Episode #237: Relaxed Running = Fast Running
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung, drawing on his own racing history and coaching philosophy (credits former coach John Shrupp for the "everything in progression" principle).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central thesis: distance running is "a game of efficiency" — burning as little energy as possible at target pace is the goal, more important than finding "the edge" every day (contrasts explicitly with studio-fitness-class culture of always seeking max effort). (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on general fitness-culture norm of always training to the edge)
- Personal anecdote: in a 10K ~15 years prior, closing his eyes mid-race and consciously relaxing shoulders/arms/legs turned a pace that felt unsustainable into one that felt "almost effortless," unlocking a strong finish and a PR-adjacent result. Used as the seed of his "moving meditation" technique. (PRACTICE)
- Ball-throwing analogy: throwing as hard as possible often produces a shorter throw than a relaxed, loose throwing motion — used to argue relaxed muscles produce more speed/distance than forced effort. (ANALOGY)
- Tip 1 — "Moving meditation": a body-scan routine done while running (sometimes eyes closed on a safe straightaway), moving head-to-toe: face/cheeks relaxed and smiling (cites sprinters' loose cheeks on camera as evidence of relaxation even at max effort), shoulders dropped via upright sternum, arms/hands loosely cupped ("potato chip grip" — hold a potato chip without crushing it), torso with gentle rotation, legs in smooth pendulum motion, feet rolling heel/midfoot to big-toe push-off, plus rhythmic odd/even breathing patterns (e.g., in-3/out-2 or in-2/out-1) synced to footstrikes. Also functions as a pain-dissociation tool during hard efforts. (PRACTICE / ANALOGY — the "potato chip grip" cue)
- Tip 2 — "Rhythm mantras" vs. "fight mantras": rhythm mantras (e.g., his own "I am fast, I am strong") are for early/mid-race to establish relaxed cadence and positive framing; fight mantras are reserved for the late-race pain-cave phase. Mantras must be personal — borrowing someone else's rarely works as well. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — mantras/self-talk in sports psych, but the two-category rhythm/fight framework is a specific, reusable structure)
- Tip 3 — Use weekly strides to deliberately exaggerate form (exaggerated knee drive, arm swing, heel kick) as a way to "bank" mental/form cues that can later be recalled in fatigue-degraded moments during workouts or races. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strides as a tool, but this specific "exaggerate then extract cues" use is a more novel angle)
- Tip 4 — "Everything in progression": nearly every run, workout, and race (except 5K and shorter) should be executed as a negative split / progression, starting conservatively and easing into pace rather than forcing effort from the start; claims this is both scientifically optimal pacing for 10K+ and the key mechanism for teaching the body to relax into pace rather than fight it. States all his own PRs from 10K up were run with negative splits. (SCIENCE — negative-split optimality is a fairly well-established claim in race-pacing literature, though no specific study/researcher named here) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — negative splitting is probably already covered under race strategy)
- Tip 5 — In prescribed workouts (e.g., 8x800 at 10K pace), the goal is to hit the target pace and make it feel smooth/relaxed, NOT to drift faster toward 5K pace to satisfy ego; deliberately avoiding "the edge" ~98-99% of the time is the correct default, reserving max effort for rare, coach-directed moments. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly pushes back on the instinct to always run workouts harder than prescribed, framing it as ego-driven and counterproductive)

## Episode #240: Slow Down, Get Your Time by Taking Your Time
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Framing device: "there's no slow, only degrees of fast" — reframes the self-deprecating use of "slow" as unproductive language athletes should drop. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- Five ways "slowing down" produces faster results, structured as the episode's core framework:
  1. **Long-term goal-setting**: don't rush training cycles to hit near-term deadlines (e.g., chasing a Boston-qualifier cutoff); repeatedly returning to the same race distance (e.g., marathon every 6 months chasing a BQ) short-circuits long-term development because athletes need to periodically train other distances. Uses Sarah Hall's Olympic Trials 10K focus (not making the team) as directly feeding her subsequent marathon American-record attempt as a real-world example of distance-cycling paying off long-term. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/long-term planning, but the specific "rotate race distances rather than repeatedly chasing one standard" argument is a sharper, more actionable angle)
  2. **Slow down on most runs (~80% easy)**: car-engine analogy — running easy "adds cylinders" (builds engine size/aerobic capacity), while running fast only "fine-tunes" the engine you already have; a maxed-out 4-cylinder can never beat a V8. Draws a parallel to strength training (low-weight/high-rep most of the time, rarely maxing out) as evidence for a similar approach in running. Gives specific relative-pace guidance: long run 1-2 min/mile slower than marathon pace (90 sec-2.5 min slower than half-marathon pace); recovery runs 2+ min slower than marathon pace (sometimes up to 3.5 min slower); other easy days 30-90 sec slower than marathon pace. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — 80/20 easy/hard training and aerobic base building, though the specific minute-based pace-offset guidance by run type is a level of tactical detail worth cross-checking against existing content)
  3. **Slow down / stay controlled even within workouts**: reiterates the relaxed-running philosophy from ep. 237 — hitting prescribed marathon/half/10K-pace intervals and making them feel smooth rather than pressing faster than prescribed; cites his own coach Cathy Casey explicitly telling him not to exceed 5K pace during a workout even though he felt capable of more, because the point was pace-zone control, not maximal output. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC overlap with ep. 237)
  4. **Slow down in recovery**: recovery must be an active, prioritized process, not squeezed into scraps of time — pick 2-3 recovery modalities and give them real space rather than trying to do everything. Personal priorities named: napping (his "number one tool"), foam rolling/self-massage, and mindful post-run nutrition. Quotes Steve Magness (via a tweet): "Stress plus rest equals growth" as the core recovery equation — training without adequate rest leads to plateaus. Also repeats Marilyn Faulkner's line "motion is lotion" for recovery-day movement. (PRACTICE, one claim attributed to Steve Magness by name) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery is probably covered broadly, but "choose 2-3 modalities deliberately rather than doing everything" is a specific prioritization heuristic)
  5. **Slow down at the start of races**: negative splitting is optimal for any race longer than 5K (backed by "the science" plus personal/coaching experience and elite/world-record pacing patterns, though no specific citation given); advises athletes to consciously start even slower than their perceived-correct pace because adrenaline distorts pace perception at race starts, and because the body's own physiological "warm-up" cost is higher in early miles — meaning a slower first mile may cost comparable energy to a faster mid-race mile. Recommends "practice races" (low-stakes prep races run specifically to rehearse pacing discipline) and workout progressions as rehearsal tools. Explicitly notes 5K and shorter races follow different (unspecified) pacing science. (SCIENCE, asserted with no named study; PRACTICE for the prep-race tool) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — negative splitting, but the adrenaline/perception-distortion mechanism and "start slow then slow down some more" heuristic add specificity)
- Also embeds current-events content on 2021 Olympic Trials races (Emma Coburn's steeplechase, Emily Sisson's 10K, Athing Mu's 800m) used purely as pacing-strategy illustrations (front-running vs. back-and-close tactics) — kept only insofar as they illustrate race-tactic variety; otherwise discardable color commentary.

## Episode #242: Building Aerobic Strength

**Concepts & claims:**
- Defines the "aerobic strength" phase (second phase after base/priming, before race-specific phase and taper) as building "endurance" / "staying power" — the ability to hold moderately hard paces for longer and longer durations; explicitly reframes "aerobic strength" as a synonym for endurance to avoid confusion with muscular strength. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization phases are probably already covered generally; this is a specific naming/definitional nuance)
- Extends the car-engine analogy from ep. 240: aerobic strength work is "fine-tuning the engine" (as opposed to "building engine size" via easy-mileage aerobic capacity work) and sits in a middle zone that does a bit of both. Argues this phase has flexible duration (can be extended up to 6-8 weeks if the race date allows) unlike the tighter, more time-boxed race-specific phase. (ANALOGY)
- Three guiding principles for this phase: (1) effort > pace — paces given are guidance only, not targets to hit precisely, because getting too pace-specific too early signals the body to prematurely enter "peaking" mode; (2) control and relaxation are paramount — the mindset should be "relaxed and efficient at pace," not "find the edge"; (3) "when in doubt, be conservative" — on marginal decisions (extra rep, extra mileage), always choose less, because pushing signals the body it's time to peak, which should be reserved for the race-specific phase. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the deliberate use of "effort vs. pace" and "conservative vs. aggressive" as an explicit signaling mechanism to the body/nervous system about training phase is a fairly specific coaching heuristic, likely not covered under generic periodization content)

(SCIENCE — mechanistic lactate/lactate-threshold explanation given without citing a specific study or physiologist) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — lactate threshold and tempo training are almost certainly covered on a physiology-focused site; useful mainly as a cross-check for terminology/description accuracy)
- Three goals for this phase: (1) execute aerobic-strength-oriented workouts across the three effort zones above; (2) build to peak training volume during this (relatively conservative) phase rather than the more intense race-specific phase, following standard volume-progression caps (no more than 3-5 mi/week increase, no more than 2 mi/week increase to the long run); (3) maintain (but deprioritize) top-end speed via weekly strides and occasional VO2max-pace touches (roughly every 3-4 weeks) with generous recovery, so speed isn't lost heading into the race-specific phase. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general volume-progression rules, though the specific "peak volume during aerobic-strength phase, not race-specific phase" sequencing is a useful specific point)
- Three example workout structures for this phase, named/described: (1) continuous or "broken" (short-rest) blocks at one of the three effort zones — e.g., a session in his group building lactate-threshold reps from 10 min up to a 40-min continuous block, with 90-sec rest between reps; (2) "combo workouts" mixing short fast reps/hill sprints (to generate fatigue) with sustained aerobic-strength-effort intervals — explicitly names the **"Michigan workout"** (attributed to Coach Ron Warhurst, University of Michigan) alternating short fast track intervals (e.g., 800s at 10K pace) with tempo/steady road intervals, and a **"Hills Plus Tempo"** workout (hill sprints immediately followed by short rest into a tempo-effort interval, e.g., mile or 800m); (3) pure longer hill-interval workouts, framed as safer (lower injury risk, since terrain caps top-end speed) while still building aerobic and muscular strength — quotes Frank Shorter: "hills are speed work in disguise." (PRACTICE, with named sources: Ron Warhurst / "Michigan workout"; Frank Shorter quote) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "Michigan workout" and "Hills Plus Tempo" are specific, named, reusable workout templates that may not be documented on a general physiology-focused site)
- "Fidgety zone/pace" — attributed to co-host/coach James Dodds — describing the particular mental discomfort of aerobic-strength-effort work: not quite at "the edge" (nothing to push against) yet still effortful, making it mentally distinct from harder race-pace/VO2max work. Recommends practicing rhythm mantras (from ep. 237) during this phase specifically to manage that discomfort. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY, named source: James Dodds) (POSSIBLE GAP — naming and addressing this specific "moderate but not maximal" psychological discomfort zone is a distinct sports-psych angle tied to a specific training-effort level, likely underrepresented in general mental-training content)
