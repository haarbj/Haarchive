## Episode #322: Summer Training Q&A with Nicole Winter
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Nicole Winter — Instagram running influencer (@nicolemruns), athlete coached by host Chris McClung for ~2 years; qualified for Boston and ran Eugene. Not a coach/scientist herself — she asks questions from her follower base; McClung (owner/coach at Rogue Running, 300+ episode podcast host) supplies essentially all substantive content.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Heat/humidity training: slow down 30–60 sec/mile (sometimes more) in extreme heat; walk-run is acceptable; no "style points" in summer training. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general heat training, though the specific pace-slowdown magnitude is a usable detail)
- Electrolyte supplementation should happen daily, outside of runs, not just during them — twice-daily dosing recommended in hot climates so the body can actually absorb/use plain water. Chronic unexplained fatigue in runners is frequently traced to missing daily electrolytes. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling/hydration, but the "daily supplementation as a lifestyle habit, not just an in-run tactic" framing is a specific angle worth checking against existing content)
- Off-season/no-race training: maintain some baseline running year-round rather than taking months fully off; pick ONE off-season focus (strength, volume, or speed) rather than doing everything at once; use off-season to add variety/fun (trail races, new routes, new training partners) to sustain motivation. (PRACTICE)
- Running form philosophy: rejects the idea of coaching a specific "correct" form/style, since biomechanics differ person to person. Instead recommends four indirect levers to improve efficiency: (1) consistent volume — "form evolves with you", (2) strength training (esp. glutes/core/lower leg), (3) weekly strides ("allergy shots for speed" — ANALOGY), (4) running-specific drills (high knees, butt flicks, skipping) done as a consistent weekly ritual, not a one-off. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — running economy is likely covered, but "efficient form emerges from volume+strength+strides+drills rather than being taught directly" is a specific, quotable coaching stance)
- Strength/running pairing: best practice is to stack hard strength days with hard running days (same day) so full rest days stay truly restful; body adapts to a stacked routine in 4–6 weeks. If schedule doesn't allow, pick 2–3 other consistent days. On days before a hard running workout, shift strength emphasis to upper body to avoid compounding lower-body stress. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strength training for runners, though the "pair hard+hard, protect the recovery days" sequencing logic is a specific tactic)
- Injury management (IT band, runner's knee): recovery from soft-tissue injury is an "active process" — engage a running-literate provider (PT/chiro/MD), don't just rest and hope. Rule of thumb: if pain is ≤3/10 and doesn't worsen during the run, continue running/cross-training ("movement = blood flow = healing" — ANALOGY, recurring show catchphrase). IT band/runner's knee specifically trace to glute weakness causing knee instability/collapse. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery/injury, but specific pain-scale rule of thumb and the "doing nothing makes soft-tissue injury worse" claim are concrete and actionable)
- Rest-day structure: distinguishes "active rest" (easy movement day after hard/long efforts) from "complete off" days. Active rest days are commonly under-utilized; recommends off days be placed immediately before hard/long days, active-rest days immediately after. Sample weekly templates given. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/recovery)
- Splitting a long run into two shorter runs is explicitly NOT recommended: aerobic-capacity benefit of a long run grows exponentially in the later miles (roughly mile 6+), so two 5-milers give "none of the benefit" of one 10-miler while costing the same musculoskeletal toll. Better to rearrange the week/swap down-weeks than to split. (SCIENCE — asserted, no study cited) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "why splitting long runs doesn't work" mechanism/claim seems like a genuinely useful, non-obvious point that a site might not have)
- Alternating race distances: one of the most common self-coaching mistakes is staying locked into one distance year-round (marathoners only ever marathoning, etc.). Recommends one 5K/10K cycle, one half cycle, one marathon cycle per year to stay "well-rounded," because raw speed at short distances raises the ceiling for longer-distance PRs. Late-starting adult runners especially tend to skip speed development and go straight to endurance. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit distance-rotation-as-annual-strategy is a specific structural idea, distinct from generic periodization)
- Single most important element of marathon training (contrarian pick): NOT the long run — the medium-long run (second-longest run of the week, e.g., 8mi for half marathoners, 10–12mi for marathoners). Argues most external training plans mistakenly hit "speed" twice a week (a workout + a tempo run) when aerobic development, not speed, is the actual limiter for most runners. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the common assumption that the long run is the single most important session) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "medium-long run as unsung hero" concept is specific and may not be covered)
- Choosing a running coach: look for partnership/investment in your goals, not just competent programming ("writing plans is the smallest part of the job"); ask how a prospective coach shows investment (check-ins during injury, understanding the "why" behind a goal, honest calibration of what's possible). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — coaching-relationship/vetting-a-coach guidance is a meta-topic likely outside typical exercise-science site content)

## Episode #323: Building (or Rebuilding) Your Running Foundation
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Solo episode — Chris McClung, running coach/owner at Rogue Running. No outside guest.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Framing: this 9-point framework applies to (a) first-time base-builders and (b) runners rebuilding after a break of 3+ weeks (full detraining reportedly takes ~3 weeks); shorter breaks (1–2 weeks) can rebuild more aggressively and aren't the target audience. (PRACTICE)
- Point 1 — "Keep it boring": foundational building should be intentionally unexciting/low-intensity; explicitly criticizes fitness-industry HIIT/spin/dance-cardio culture as too intense for building long-term running capacity; ties boring-easy-running to physiological adaptations (added lung capacity, more efficient red blood cells, new capillaries, added mitochondria) and to musculoskeletal loading benefits (bone density/size increases with graded impact). (SCIENCE — physiological mechanisms asserted, no specific study/researcher named) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base development, though framed with an unusually explicit "embrace the boredom" psychological angle)
- Point 2 — Routine: cites "science tells us it takes six months to establish a habitual exercise routine" (SCIENCE — asserted, no source named) — argues for consistent, near-year-round training rather than the common pattern of "train hard for a race, take 3 months off, restart." Personal anecdote: took 9 months to shift from night-runner to morning-runner after having kids. (PRACTICE)
- Point 3 — Sequencing rule: build days-per-week FIRST, then distance, then speed — never all three simultaneously (this combination is framed as the primary cause of overuse injury in self-coached ramp-ups). Concrete thresholds: start with 2–3 days/week of ~20-min efforts, add one day per week; build distance by only 3–4 mi/week; don't add structured speed work until ~25 mi/week of total volume (strides are the one exception, allowed once running days are established). Gives a sample 25 mi/week blueprint (10mi long run, 5mi medium-long, 5mi quality, recovery runs). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization, but the specific "days→distance→speed" sequencing rule and mileage thresholds are concrete, actionable specifics)
- Point 4 — Keep it easy: until speed is added, ALL runs should be at easy/conversational effort; gives explicit slowdown targets — at least 1 min/mile slower than marathon goal pace, 90 sec/mile slower than half-marathon goal pace, 2 min slower than 10K goal pace, 2.5 min slower than 5K goal pace. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base/easy-running, but with concrete pace-offset numbers)
- Point 5 — Soreness reframe: normal soreness is a signal that muscles need recovery/movement, not a signal to stop; "motion is lotion" (ANALOGY, attributed to "one of our coaches"); extreme/debilitating soreness signals you went too fast and need to slow down further, not stop entirely. (PRACTICE)
- Point 6 — Don't race yet: recommends 3+ months of base-building/rebuilding before entering a race-specific training block (for both new and returning runners); phrase used: "fit enough to train" before jumping into race training. Okay to do a low-stakes 5K as part of routine, but not to chase serious longer-distance goals prematurely. (PRACTICE)
- Point 7 — Add strength now, but simply: 20–30 min, 2x/week, bodyweight only to start (prisoner squats, single-leg deadlifts, lunges, push-ups, planks); slot after medium-long/quality days if possible. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strength training for runners)
- Point 8 — Recovery routine hierarchy: sleep and fueling are the two most important recovery tools (both already-existing habits that just need modest incremental improvement — e.g., +30 min sleep, don't skip post-run breakfast); active-rest running is third; everything else (foam rolling, mobility, self-massage, yoga, meditation) is individualized "beyond the fundamentals." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery)
- Point 9 — Listen to your body / be conservative when in doubt: plans will not play out perfectly; repeating a week, backing off speed, or swapping a run for a walk are all legitimate adaptive responses. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #324: Training Paces and Race Planning with James Dodds
**Guest(s) and credentials:** James Dodds — fellow Rogue Running coach, coaches Rogue's virtual "Renegades" group; 26 marathons run, non-competitive-collegiate background (came to running at 25).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Training paces and race paces are conceptually separate: training paces are a proxy for hitting effort-based physiological zones, not a prediction of race-day performance; race-pace terminology ("run 5K pace") is really shorthand for "hit this aerobic-development zone." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — pacing methodology, though the explicit "training pace ≠ predicted race pace, it's a zone label" framing is a specific clarifying point)
- Training paces should be prescribed as a RANGE (e.g., ±10–20 sec/mile), not a pinpoint number — because the underlying purpose is hitting an effort zone, and rigid single-pace targets create false pass/fail pressure. In hot/humid conditions the range should be widened further, not shifted by a fixed percentage — heat/humidity response is too individual (build, physiology) for a blanket multiplier. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "why ranges, and why heat requires widening not a fixed % offset" reasoning is a specific, non-obvious methodological point)
- Determining pace ranges: use a fitness test (2-mile time trial preferred for higher-mileage half/marathon runners; 1-mile or 5K acceptable alternates) rather than old PRs from high school/college — "I don't care what you ran in high school," only current fitness matters. Feed the time-trial result into a calculator (McMillan's "race times" tab specifically recommended over its "training paces" tab, which the guest finds confusing/idiosyncratic). Three data inputs used together: time-trial/race result, training performance data, and goals (goals are relevant only if consistent with actual current fitness). Suggested target-race pace ranges: marathon ±5 min (10-min total window), half ±2.5 min (5-min window), 10K ~±1 min (2-min window), 5K ~±30 sec (1-min window). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training-pace calculation, though specific range widths per distance are a concrete detail)
- "Train where you are, not where you want to go" (direct quote, repeated host catchphrase) — McMillan's calculator is explicitly built around this same principle. (PRACTICE)
- Treadmill workouts are discouraged when avoidable (even though sometimes necessary for heat/winter) because they let you "nail" the exact prescribed pace without learning to feel effort/pace by intuition — this removes a "dimension of learning" runners need for race-day pace judgment. Co-host adds a "radical honesty" framing: treadmill use is fine if the reason is genuinely weather, but using it specifically to game/hit prescribed paces (rather than learning to feel effort) is a form of self-deception. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on treating treadmill precision as unambiguously good) (POSSIBLE GAP — "treadmill removes the pace-feel learning dimension" is a specific, less commonly made argument)
- Ego/identity and easy-pace comparison (Strava-driven insecurity) explicitly called out as a common athlete failure mode; coach describes multi-year process (3–4 years) of getting some athletes to trust running truly-easy recovery paces (~135–140 bpm) rather than chasing arbitrary pace floors even in extreme heat. (PRACTICE) — overlaps with Ep. 326's identity theme.
- Recovery/easy-run pace rule of thumb: long run/medium-long run should be ≥1 min/mile slower than marathon pace or ≥90 sec slower than half pace; pure recovery runs should be ~2 min slower than marathon pace or ~2.5 min slower than half pace. Getting faster on recovery runs over time is NOT a fitness indicator and shouldn't be treated as one — host describes his own recovery paces getting slower over time as a sign of "embracing the power of slowing down." (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — explicitly pushes back on using recovery-run pace as a self-esteem/fitness metric)
- Heart rate as effort proxy: caveats that wrist-based HR may be inaccurate (chest strap preferred) and that true HR zones require a VO2 max test. Anecdote: a 9-mile mountain run at 9,100–9,600 ft with heart rate averaging ~120s bpm throughout despite one mile taking 18:45 (climbing) and another taking 7:22 (descending) — same effort/HR, wildly different pace — used to argue effort (not pace) is what matters, and that runners give themselves pace "grace" for altitude/terrain but often fail to extend the same grace for heat/humidity. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — personal data example, not a formal study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — heart-rate training, though the altitude vs. heat "grace asymmetry" observation is a distinct framing)
- Race plan should have three core components: (1) pace strategy — including accounting for course elevation profile, not just a generic "textbook" pacing plan (real example given: an athlete built a flat-course pacing plan for a race with major elevation change); (2) nutrition/hydration strategy; (3) mental strategy — subdivided into "why" (purpose for racing) and distinct "rhythm mantras" (early/mid-race, staying relaxed) vs. "fight mantras" (late-race, when suffering) — these should differ because the required headspace differs by race phase. One coach's personal technique: deliberately "matching pain with pain" — using the last 10K of a marathon as an intentional metaphor for a hard life experience to draw motivation. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race-day mental strategy/general sports psychology, though the rhythm-vs-fight mantra distinction and pain-matching technique are specific, quotable tactics)
- Cross-training/recovery: glute/hip strength (bodyweight, 3x/week, 20 min minimum) called the single best injury-prevention lever; restorative cross-training (bike, rowing machine) should be done at low intensity ("gliding," not "hammering") — hammering the bike specifically named as a cause of calf issues in some athletes. Plug for walking as an undervalued recovery/cross-training tool, both physically (low HR movement) and mentally (a South African "lion tracker" teacher's concept of "slowing down to the pace of life," journaling by hand vs. typing). (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- Book reference: Christie Aschwanden's "Good to Go" cited as evidence that most peripheral recovery modalities (foam rolling, compression, cryotherapy, etc.) lack strong supporting science, while sleep/fueling/easy movement do have solid evidence behind them. (SCIENCE — named book/author, general claim) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery, but useful citation to potentially cross-check)
- Warns against high-intensity group fitness classes (Orange Theory, "dance on your bike" spin classes) being miscategorized as running cross-training — describes an athlete getting injured from doing Orange Theory the day after a hard running workout; fine for general fitness/weight loss but not calibrated for a running training block. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT)
- Injury protocol for non-season-ending injuries: catch "niggles" early and stay in active communication with a coach/provider (near-100% of runners training hard will face some injury-adjacent issue); soft-tissue injuries require an active rehab process, not just rest; return-to-running percentages after a layoff — a few days off: jump back in normally; 1 week off: return at 75%, full by week 2; 2 weeks off: 50% → 75% → full over 3 weeks; 3+ weeks off: reassess from scratch depending on experience/history. Pain-tracking method suggested: rate 1–10, note whether it's focal/pinpoint (bad sign) vs. diffuse/global and bilateral (more permissible) vs. unilateral. 3/10-or-less-and-not-worsening rule repeated from Ep. 322. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery/injury, though the specific %-based return-to-running ramp and focal-vs-diffuse pain tracking method are concrete, reusable specifics)

## Episode #325: Becoming a Sustainable Runner with Tina Muir and Zoë Rom
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Tina Muir — former elite GB (Great Britain) marathoner, host of Running for Real/Running Realized podcasts, RED-S educator/advocate. Zoë Rom — managing editor of Women's Running, editor-in-chief of Trail Runner magazine, elite ultra/trail athlete (training for Run Rabbit Run 100-miler at Steamboat during this recording). Co-authors of the book "Becoming a Sustainable Runner" (Human Kinetics, Aug 2023).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central book thesis: "sustainable running" spans three domains — sustaining your body, your community, and the environment — and the throughline across all three is rejecting perfectionism ("not trying to be a perfect environmentalist, not trying to nail every race"). (PRACTICE)
- Long-term vs. short-term framing: Muir describes burning out of elite running by losing the "process" in favor of purely outcome/finish-line focus over 14 years; argues chasing short-term results by "digging deeper" when things aren't working is a common and ultimately self-defeating runner pattern. Rom: true measure of achievement is "the assemblage of your work," not any single day/race — treating one race as pass/fail is "underselling yourself" and often a way of avoiding the more difficult, vulnerable work of redefining success. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology, though "process over outcome" specific book-length treatment could still add citable material)
- DNF reframing: Rom recounts DNFing Leadville 100 at age 24 due to hyponatremia, and explicitly rejects the "quitting becomes a habit" narrative — argues the cultural pass/fail, quitter/finisher binary is harmful and that stopping to prevent a medical emergency (or additional risk to search-and-rescue) was the objectively correct decision, reframed as "success" against her own values rather than failure against others' expectations. Host shares a parallel anecdote of walking the last 5 miles of Boston with an undiagnosed stress fracture, needing no "redemption" for a day he gave his best effort. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — explicitly pushes back on the common belief that stopping mid-race = weakness/quitting) (POSSIBLE GAP — DNF psychology/reframing is a specific mental-performance angle not obviously covered by generic "sports psychology")
- "Life is not a John Wick movie" — using race outcomes as revenge/redemption narratives to meet an unmet emotional need tends to produce worse athletic outcomes and can harm mental/physical health; better to address the underlying unmet need directly. (ANALOGY/PRACTICE)
- "Seasons of life" concept: running goals and volume should flex around major life stressors (new child, divorce, career upheaval, book launch, etc.) — stress is stress regardless of source (training, work, family) and doesn't compartmentalize; trying to "push through" training during major life stress leads to overtraining/burnout/injury and drains the joy out of running. Both authors describe currently self-moderating marathon training volume goals (e.g., accepting 60 mi/week instead of a targeted 70) around their book launch as a live example. (PRACTICE)
- Critique of wearable/metric culture: both authors describe deliberately removing training-status/recovery-score features from watches (and one removed an Oura ring entirely) because ambiguous "black box" metrics (e.g., "training status: unproductive," proprietary recovery scores) created stress without producing better decisions — argue the industry sells "yardsticks we'll never measure up to so we'll keep buying new yardsticks." Recommends curating only metrics that produce actionable behavior change; better internal signals cited: felt recovery, quality of relationships, ability to say no. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — explicit pushback on the common assumption that more biometric data = better training decisions) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific "divest from wearable metrics that don't drive decisions" argument, and the underlying business-model critique of quantification, is a fairly distinct angle from generic "sports psychology" content)
- Health at Every Size (HAES) and RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport): RED-S is not limited to young, thin, white women — it can affect any gender, size, age, or background; common denominator is inadequate caloric intake relative to output. Under-recognized RED-S symptoms beyond amenorrhea: Raynaud's-like poor circulation in hands/feet, chronic GI distress, insomnia — often misattributed to other causes. Claim: majority of people meeting diagnostic criteria for eating disorders are actually in larger bodies, and are frequently underdiagnosed because medical systems associate disordered eating with a narrow (thin, white, affluent) profile. No linear relationship between body weight and running performance. (SCIENCE — presented as evidence-backed, but no specific study/researcher named beyond the Health at Every Size organization itself) (POSSIBLE GAP — RED-S is adjacent to "general fueling" but this specific under-diagnosis/demographic-bias angle and the non-amenorrhea symptom list look like a genuine gap even if RED-S itself is covered elsewhere)
- Community-building advice: overcoming first-timer intimidation at group runs/clubs by recognizing "your pace/output is not your worth," and that vulnerability is inherent to how running friendships form; recommends finding identity-affinity groups (e.g., city-specific run crews, Martinez Evans's "Slow AF Run Club") if a generic group feels unwelcoming, or starting one's own group. Bill McKibben climate-activism quote used as an analogy for community organizing generally: "First step to activism is join with your neighbors and organize. Second step is join with your neighbors and organize." (ANALOGY/PRACTICE)
- Environmental/sustainability content (largely outside typical training-content scope): recycling's origin is attributed to the fossil fuel/plastics industry as a strategy to make consumers comfortable with disposable plastic (a historical/marketing claim, asserted without a specific citation); "reduce, reuse" should take priority over recycling, which is a last-resort "harm mitigation," not a solution. Practical individual-action suggestions: push race directors on food-waste/t-shirt-opt-out policies (with an explicit note to be "gentle," not confrontational, when doing so — "being a jerk is not climate action"); Community Supported Agriculture (CSA) as a low-effort entry point; be judicious about race travel/flights, combine trips, use verified carbon-offset programs; buy fewer/higher-quality gear items and repair them (tailor, rain-jacket reproofing) rather than buying cheap/frequently. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE-flavored-claim) (POSSIBLE GAP — sustainability/environmental impact of the sport is very likely entirely absent from a physiology/coaching-focused site; flagging as a clear gap if in scope for the site's mission, though it's a different topic domain than exercise science)

## Episode #326: Separating Your Identity from Your Results
**Guest(s) and credentials:** James Dodds — Rogue Running coach (same as Ep. 324/328); describes his own psychological/philosophical background (former aspiring pastor, well-read in psychology/philosophy).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Both hosts share personal origin stories illustrating unhealthy identity-outcome fusion: Dodds came to running at 25 during a "first great divorce" identity crisis after abandoning a pastoral-track path, deliberately used running as a non-outcome-based meditative practice; McClung came to running directly from a lifelong chain of competitive sports (soccer → volleyball → running) explicitly seeking external validation through performance, later realizing this wasn't a healthy foundation. (PRACTICE)
- Two "unhealthy" poles are named: (1) obsession/over-identification — training through injury warning signs, burnout, inability to feel normal without the activity (compared to addiction); and (2) apathy/under-caring as a defense mechanism against the risk of caring. Both are framed as manifestations of the same underlying problem (mis-tied identity), not opposites. (PRACTICE)
- "Clenched fist vs. open palm" analogy (with a Gollum/Lord of the Rings comparison for the clenched-fist obsession state) — goal is to hold goals/roles with an open palm: full effort and attention while holding them, without desperately gripping them as if losing them would be catastrophic. (ANALOGY)
- Frameworks referenced for separating identity from outcomes: Eckhart Tolle's "A New Earth" (the "multiple hats" concept — you are simultaneously many roles, not just "runner"); the "floating man"/"flying man" philosophical thought experiment (self-awareness independent of sensory comparison); Sam Harris's "Waking Up" and the "are you your hand / are you your thoughts" exercise; George Sheehan's "Running and Being" (1978) — quoted directly: "My fitness program was never a fitness program. It was a campaign, a revolution, a conversion. I was determined to find myself." (ANALOGY/PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific philosophical-framework toolkit for athletic identity work goes considerably deeper than typical "general sports psychology" content and could be a distinctive addition)
- Dodds's personal "six buckets" self-evaluation framework (partly derived from Covey's 7 Habits) for defining identity beyond one domain: spiritual/existential, emotional/relational, financial/resource, physical/biological (where sport lives), social, and mental — each self-rated 1–10 on your own terms, not compared to others, precisely so a bad day in one bucket ("running") doesn't collapse total self-worth. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a structured, named multi-domain self-worth framework is a specific tool a site might not have)
- Explicit claim: "It's never not about the running, but it's never been just about the running" — argues nobody runs marathons purely for health (health only requires ~45 min/day of exercise); the deeper motivations (adventure, meaning-making) are real and shouldn't be denied, but shouldn't be the sole basis of self-worth either. Same logic applied to "I run for stress relief" as often being a partial truth. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the common self-narrative that endurance racing is "just for health/stress relief")
- Practical tools offered: (1) treat separating identity from outcomes as an ongoing skill/journey, not a one-time fix; (2) separate "training results" from "race results" explicitly — write down training-cycle wins (mileage PRs, workout execution) independent of race-day outcome, since the two don't always align even with excellent training; (3) reframe "success" around the actual purpose of a given day (e.g., an easy day's success = recovering enough, not running faster than last time; a "prep race" done for practice shouldn't be judged by PR standard) — cross-references Ep. 328's prep-race framing; (4) self-compassion/distancing techniques — Kristin/Christopher Germer's "The Mindful Path to Self-Compassion" and Dr. Maya Shankar's Huberman Lab interview on identity reinvention (a former Juilliard violinist turned White House behavioral-science advisor) cited as resources; talk to yourself as you would a training partner, not a critic; (5) label negative thought spirals without judgment (via journaling or meditation) rather than suppressing emotions — explicitly warns that suppressing "bad" emotions (anger, jealousy, frustration) backfires, and that ALL emotions, including negative ones, need to be metabolized, not pushed down. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for the self-compassion/emotional-processing pieces broadly overlapping general sports psychology, but the specific named books/tools and the training-vs-racing-results distinction are concrete, reusable specifics)
- Cheating (doping) and training "shortcuts" (excessive mileage too soon, under-fueling, chronic overpacing) are both framed as symptomatic of ego/identity being too tightly fused to outcomes — i.e., a psychological root cause is offered for both doping and more mundane bad training choices, not just a rules/ethics framing. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT)

## Episode #327: Live Q&A with Nicole Winter
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Same as Ep. 322 — Nicole Winter (athlete/influencer) posing live-audience questions to coach Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- In-run fueling: two general pathways — (1) fast-sugar gels/chews (Gu, Huma, Maurten, Honey Stinger, Clif Blok, Scratch gummies, etc.), typically starting 1 hour in and repeating every 35–40 minutes to avoid an insulin/blood-sugar trough; (2) slow-burn cornstarch-based product (UCAN specifically named) taken as a pre-run shake ~30 min before start, providing 2–3 hours of energy before needing supplemental gels — an "acquired taste" that doesn't work for everyone. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, though the specific 35–40 min re-dosing interval and the two-pathway (fast-sugar vs. slow-burn starch) framing are concrete, checkable specifics)
- Pre-run/pre-race nutrition: no need for multi-day carb-loading unless following a specific 7-day protocol (otherwise "it doesn't work"); eat familiar low-fiber/easy-digesting foods the night before and a small 200–400 calorie carb-forward pre-run snack — explains the physiological rationale that a small pre-run meal signals "safety" to the body so it doesn't downshift into fuel-conserving fight-or-flight mode, preserving full access to fuel stores; being restrictive pre-run can suppress metabolism/fuel access. (SCIENCE — asserted mechanism, no study named) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "eating a small pre-run meal prevents a stress-conservation response" mechanism is a specific enough claim worth flagging even if general fueling is covered)
- Down-week structure: recommends every 3rd week as a down week, reducing overall volume 15–20% (commonly 4–8 miles off the long run, ~2 miles off the medium-long run) while maintaining the same running days/frequency; explicitly warns runners often feel MORE sluggish, not less, during down weeks because the body is in active repair/consolidation mode (compares to taper sluggishness) — not a cause for alarm. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/deload weeks, though the specific 15–20% figure and the "sluggish during the down week is normal, gains show up the following up-week" claim are concrete and checkable)
