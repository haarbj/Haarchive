## Episode #185: Good and Bad Fatigue, How to Assess and Manage Both
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung, founder/coach at Rogue Running (Austin, TX), ~20 years coaching experience.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Three-tier framework for classifying training fatigue: (1) non-cumulative fatigue — soreness/tiredness from a single hard session, resolves in 24–48 hrs; (2) cumulative fatigue — the "good" building fatigue of a peaking block, lingers but still allows strong workouts most of the time; (3) overtraining — fatigue that degrades performance day after day with no rebound. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overtraining/fatigue management is standard, but this specific 3-tier taxonomy with rules of thumb is a clean, teachable model worth preserving.)
- Rule of thumb: if bad/flat workouts persist more than 7–10 consecutive days in spite of trying to adjust (slow down, sleep, hydrate), you've likely crossed from productive cumulative fatigue into overtraining and need an acute intervention. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete day-count threshold is a specific, actionable diagnostic heuristic.)
- Cumulative fatigue should be a narrow window, not a constant state: ~3–7 weeks out from a marathon (final 4 weeks before taper) or 2–4 weeks out from a half. Feeling it year-round signals something is wrong. (PRACTICE)
- Managing non-cumulative fatigue = "hydrate, fuel, sleep, move": rehydrate with electrolytes to light-yellow urine; refuel with balanced carb/fat/protein within ~30–45 min post-exercise for women, up to ~90 min for men; prioritize sleep/naps after hard efforts; gentle movement the day after promotes blood flow and healing ("motion is lotion"). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — asserted, not study-cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery/fueling windows already likely covered, though the sex-based refueling-window distinction may be a specific detail worth checking.)
- Personal example: host had a lingering "dead-leg" feeling diagnosed by "Dr. Moose" (recurring guest) as a potassium/electrolyte imbalance (not sodium or magnesium) via an in-office test; supplementing potassium chloride resolved it. (PRACTICE, self-reported anecdote, not a controlled claim) (POSSIBLE GAP — electrolyte sub-typing beyond generic "hydrate" advice.)
- Managing overtraining: take 3–4 full days off (not weeks) and use the time to get blood values checked (e.g., for anemia); untreated overtraining syndrome can take 12–18 months to resolve, versus a quick short-term intervention if caught early. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — asserted) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC, but the recovery-timeline framing (days off vs. months-long syndrome) is a useful concrete anchor.)
- ANALOGY: "Riding the fine line" of tearing the body down the right amount to build it up — cumulative fatigue is described as intentionally training on tired legs so the body and mind learn to perform at the end of a marathon when legs "say no."
- Quote (attributed to an unnamed Brooks Hansons coach): "When fatigue is coming from the culmination of training and not from one specific aspect, the athlete is fatigued but still able to run strong." (SCIENCE claim but source is a coach's assertion, not a study.)

## Episode #188: Eight Tips for Marathon Training in a Pandemic
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Jen Harney — Rogue Running coach (Cedar Park location), develops curriculum for Rogue broadly.

**Concepts & claims:**
- "Train for a plateau, not a peak" — when race-day certainty is low, build fitness across a 2–4 week window you can hold rather than sharply peaking for one date; keep training all energy systems (speed, 5K/10K pace, half/marathon pace, LT/aerobic threshold) concurrently rather than committing fully to one distance's specificity. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — "plateau training" as an alternative periodization concept to the standard peak/taper model is a genuinely different angle from typical peaking-for-a-single-race guidance.)
- "Bread and butter" maintenance work: weekly strides (4–6 x 80–100m, controlled fast effort) framed as a standing prescription regardless of race uncertainty. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strides/speed maintenance is common advice.)
- ANALOGY: weekly strides compared to "allergy shots" — negligible effect in isolation, but valuable through cumulative, repeated weekly dosing over months/years.
- Long-run variation techniques discussed: (a) no-nutrition long runs to train fat-burning/metabolic flexibility and build "muscular resilience"; (b) long runs with a prescribed heart-rate ceiling/range (used for an athlete who struggled with summer heat) to keep effort controlled and improve recovery from the session; (c) fast-finish/progression long runs as an alternative to traditional embedded workout long runs. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — fat-adaptation claim asserted, no study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for general "long run variation," though the specific HR-capped long run and no-nutrition long run as deliberate substitutes for hard workout long runs may be a useful specific technique.)
- "Know your why" needs to evolve when tied only to a specific race goal — with race calendars uncertain, motivation must shift to process-level or identity-level purposes (e.g., running as stress outlet, running as community connection) rather than outcome goals. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general "purpose"/motivation coaching.)
- Practical prediction/decision-making tips for uncertain race calendars: build "optionality" (have a backup local/smaller race), and set a self-imposed decision deadline to pivot away from a race roughly equal to [taper length] + [race-specific phase length] before the event, rather than waiting on the race organizer's announcement. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is really a general-purpose "when to abandon Plan A and execute Plan B" framework applicable beyond pandemics, e.g., injury-forced race changes.)
- Both coaches state 20-mile long runs may be scaled back to 18 miles for marathon training when full commitment to the goal race is in doubt, citing a psychological (not just physical) difference athletes report between the two distances. (PRACTICE, anecdotal)

## Episode #190: Seven Tips for Re-Building Your Fitness
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Note: first half of episode is the host's personal, non-expert summary of COVID-19 transmission research (masks, aerosol transmission, outdoor vs. indoor risk) — pure pandemic-era public-health content, not running-relevant; excluded from extraction per instructions.
- Seven-step framework for rebuilding running fitness after a layoff (injury, motivation loss, etc.), aimed at experienced runners, not beginners: (1) reconnect to purpose and set a goal — a process goal (e.g., "run 18 times in 3 weeks") if no outcome/race goal exists; (2) "know what you're in for" — expect 4–6 weeks of it feeling bad/discouraging, and pre-plan a coping mantra or "focus on today only" strategy; (3) establish the target routine (days, workout placement, strength days) from week one, ramping running and strength together rather than sequentially; (4) build days per week before building distance per day; (5) once building distance, follow a "five-mile-per-week rule" (add no more than ~4–5 miles/week) as an alternative to the 10% rule, which the host says can be misleading due to percentage scaling; optionally use a 2-weeks-build/1-week-hold cadence for less experienced ramping athletes; (6) be patient with speed — no traditional hard speed work for at least 4 weeks, using only strides or light fartlek pickups in the interim, and when workouts resume, run 10–15 minutes slower (marathon-equivalent) than prior paces and let paces re-earn themselves; (7) don't shift into a race-specific/rigorous training block until fitness "clicks" (a subjective threshold, often 4–8+ weeks) rather than on a fixed calendar date. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — return-to-running progression is standard, but the specific "5-mile rule vs. 10% rule" argument and the "build days before distance" sequencing rule are more novel, actionable specifics — POSSIBLE GAP for those two sub-points.)
- Personal example: host took 8 weeks off after a 2016 stress fracture, then reinjured a different body part later in the comeback by training with faster teammates too soon — illustrates that every body system used in running must re-adapt after a layoff, not just the originally injured area. (PRACTICE, anecdotal)
- Reference to Des Linden (elite marathoner, Boston champion, Olympian) publicly tweeting that returning from even a one-month layoff is harder than she remembers — used as social proof that struggle on return is normal even for elites. (Not a scientific claim; anecdote/quote from a named elite athlete.)

## Episode #193: Training and Vacation
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Six rules for handling training during vacation: (1) enjoying the vacation itself takes priority over structured training; (2) stay active in unstructured, fun ways (hiking, swimming, walking) rather than insisting on formal runs; (3) use vacation to explore new terrain on foot (host recommends apps: Trail Run Project, AllTrails, MapMyRun — Trail Run Project works offline via GPS); (4) prioritize sleep and relaxation as part of a deliberate recovery/restoration window; (5) have fun/be playful/try new things; (6) upon return, ease back in for only 2–4 days at ~80–85% of prior peak long-run distance, then resume full training in week two — no extended, multi-week ramp-up is needed if the athlete stayed generally active during the trip. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — "how to handle a vacation without losing fitness" is a fairly specific, everyday-life training question not obviously covered by standard base-training/periodization content.)
- DISAGREEMENT/misconception correction: host states detraining effects don't meaningfully set in until roughly 3 weeks of inactivity, framing the common fear of "losing all my fitness" during a 1–2 week vacation as unfounded — reframes reduced training as "gaining recovery" rather than "losing fitness." (SCIENCE — asserted as established, no specific study or researcher cited.) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site already covers detraining timelines; POSSIBLE GAP if it doesn't specifically address the vacation/short-break case.)
- Personal anecdotes offered as evidence: host took a 2-week beach vacation with minimal structured running before a marathon in 2003–04 and ran a ~10-minute marathon PR afterward; took a 2-week Europe trip (only 2 runs total, one a 25-mile/40k trail shakeout) before a 50-miler and beat his goal time. (PRACTICE, anecdotal — small sample, self-reported, not controlled evidence.)

## Episode #194: Finding Run-Life Balance
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung, drawing on ~20 years balancing corporate work, grad school, a coaching business, and raising three kids alongside his own training.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Eight-step framework for balancing running with work/family/life: (1) prioritize intentionally and reject the "black-and-white tradeoff" framing — pursuing personal goals and being present for family can be complementary ("yin and yang") rather than zero-sum; (2) "cancel your excuses" — reframe internal narratives from reasons-you-can't to reasons-you-can (host's own turning point: a Lululemon-run goal-setting session where a facilitator challenged him to stop rationalizing why marathon PRs weren't possible given his life constraints; he went on to PR at every distance from 10K to marathon over the following ~18 months); (3) proactively engage your partner/family/support network on both your goals and the constraints they create — "ask for permission, not forgiveness"; (4) actively make time or explicitly ask for it (e.g., negotiating flexible work hours, shifting training to non-traditional days for shift workers); (5) optimize the time available — e.g., train by time-elapsed rather than target mileage when time is capped, do fewer recovery modalities consistently rather than many inconsistently, combine commute with training runs; (6) multitask recovery/strength work into otherwise "dead" time (calf raises in a grocery line, foam rolling during a work call, bodyweight work on the sideline at a kid's practice); (7) find an accountability partner/group (physical or virtual), since most people can't sustain consistency purely through self-motivation; (8) expect imperfection and iterate — "learn, adjust, adapt" rather than treating missed sessions as failure. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is coaching/life-logistics content rather than exercise physiology; a site oriented toward training science and nutrition likely doesn't cover life-integration/time-management strategy for adult recreational athletes in this depth.)
- Quote (attributed to friend/ultrarunner Paul Terranova, cited again in a different episode this batch): "You can choose the pain of discipline or the pain of regret," used as a mantra for early-morning training motivation. (ANALOGY/quote)
- Example of negotiated household division of training time: a coached husband-and-wife pair (one a marathoner, one an Ironman triathlete) alternate 5–6 month blocks where each partner gets primary priority for their own big goal while still supporting the other's baseline training. (PRACTICE, anecdotal — a specific relationship-logistics tactic.)

## Episode #195: Dealing with Doubts and Fears with Jason Brooks
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Jason Brooks — co-coach of Rogue's "Base Training" podcast group, former U.S. Marine, experienced ultrarunner (50-milers, 100k+); interviewed in two parts, before and after his attempt at "Nolan's 14" (an unofficial Colorado challenge: traversing 14 fourteener peaks, ~88–103 miles, 44,000 ft of gain, in under 60 hours).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Adapted "fear-setting" exercise (explicitly credited to Tim Ferriss): before a high-stakes goal, write out each specific fear, a preventive measure for it, and a response plan if it happens anyway — used here for fear of injury, getting lost overnight, and physical/cognitive failure from sleep deprivation. (PRACTICE, explicitly named source/technique) (POSSIBLE GAP — fear-setting as a discrete, named pre-goal planning tool is likely a different angle than generic "manage race-day anxiety" content.)
- "Circle of control" (attributed to stoic philosophy, referenced via a "Final Surge" mental-skills lesson) — deliberately directing attention only to controllable variables (pacing, fueling plan, route knowledge) rather than uncontrollable outcomes, to reduce anxiety. (PRACTICE/philosophy) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — stoicism/control framing is common in sports psychology content already.)
- Host's distinction between associative and dissociative coping strategies for pre-event anxiety: associative = channeling anxious energy into further preparation/planning (studying maps, rehearsing logistics); dissociative = deliberately distracting oneself (movies, socializing) when rumination becomes counterproductive. Host says both can be needed at different times. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — an explicit associative/dissociative dual-strategy framework, if not already present, is a more specific psychological tool than generic "manage pre-race nerves.")
- DISAGREEMENT/self-diagnosis distinction: guest and host discuss the difficulty of telling "rational concern about being under-prepared" apart from "irrational self-doubt/imposter syndrome" — guest concludes after his attempt that what felt like fear was actually his mind correctly flagging inadequate preparation ("you didn't do your homework"), not baseless anxiety. (PRACTICE/psychology — a specific, non-obvious point that self-doubt can be legitimate signal rather than noise to override.)
- ANALOGY: breaking an overwhelming goal into the next controllable unit — "you don't know if you can run all 26.2 miles at goal pace, but you know you can run the first mile" — applied by the host to marathon pacing and extended by the guest to "one peak at a time" during the ultra attempt. Mantra used: "the best way out is always through" (credited to ultrarunner Paul Terranova, same phrase referenced independently in Episode #194).
- Goal-calibration debate: host raises the question of whether goals should be ones you're confident you can achieve with enough preparation (his own approach — e.g., a sub-2:40 marathon he calls "Project 606," named for the required per-mile pace) versus deliberately open-ended, uncertain "maybe I can't do this at all" goals (the guest's approach with Nolan's 14). Both agree stretch goals should require sustained, multi-year effort and tolerate failed attempts, but disagree on whether the goal itself needs to be provably achievable in advance. (DISAGREEMENT — an explicit, unresolved difference in coaching philosophy between the two, presented without a stated winner.)
- Guest reframes his non-completion (4 of ~14 peaks in 13 hours before stopping) as a successful information-gathering attempt rather than a failure: identified specific gear failures (drink-mix solubility issues, trail gaiters wearing through, a broken trekking pole), route-finding lessons (repeat exposure to terrain made line-finding much faster), and a self-diagnosed pattern of taking on one big physically risky challenge every 9-ish months and then getting injured afterward — leading him to prioritize longer unbroken training consistency (18+ months) before reattempting. (PRACTICE, self-reflective/anecdotal)
- Recommended external media referenced as motivational resources for long-term "impossible" goals: the documentaries "The Dawn Wall" (Tommy Caldwell) and "Free Solo" (Alex Honnold) — used as analogies for meticulous pre-planning of every segment of a huge undertaking. (ANALOGY — pop-culture reference, not fitness-specific content.)
