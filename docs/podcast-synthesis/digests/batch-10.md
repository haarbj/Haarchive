## Episode #205: Tracking Heart Rate Variability and How to Apply It
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Jason Brooks, Rogue Running coach (same as ep. 204).

**Concepts & claims:**
- HRV defined precisely: the variability in time interval between heartbeats. Beating on a rigid, consistent cadence (low HRV) signals sympathetic ("fight or flight") dominance; more irregular beat-to-beat timing (high HRV) signals parasympathetic dominance/relaxation. (SCIENCE — general physiology, not attributed to a specific study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site already covers autonomic nervous system/recovery monitoring, otherwise POSSIBLE GAP)
- Two distinct coaching concepts introduced: "preparedness" (long-term readiness/health foundation for intense training) vs. "readiness" (day-to-day decision-making about whether to train hard today). HRV is positioned as a tool for readiness, not preparedness. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY-adjacent framework) (POSSIBLE GAP — this preparedness/readiness distinction is a clean conceptual framework worth preserving)
- Explicit misconception named and corrected: a low HRV reading does NOT mean "don't train" or "can't race well" — it means the body is in fight-or-flight and ready to produce work, but recovery afterward needs to be prioritized. Host gives an anecdote about an athlete panicking over low HRV before a 1-mile time trial and being told she was fine to race. (DISAGREEMENT / explicit myth-busting) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Practical tool recommendations: HRV4Training app ($10 one-time, iOS/Android, uses phone camera for 60-second pulse reading, best taken first thing on waking, needs ~4+ days/week minimum, ideally daily); Oura Ring (~$350, tracks HRV, resting HR, temp, respiratory rate, sleep); Whoop Band (subscription model); Omega Wave (expensive "gold standard" clinical option). (PRACTICE, product-specific — may age poorly but the categories/use-case guidance is durable)
- Recommended primary use case: acute daily HRV as a go/no-go signal for that day's quality workout combined with subjective check-in (sleep, alcohol, stress) rather than a single "optimize to a number" metric; secondary use case is longer-term trend analysis — an upward HRV trend across a base-training block indicates building fitness/resilience, a plateau or downward trend signals overreaching and the need to back off. (SCIENCE, general — no specific study cited) (POSSIBLE GAP, useful practical protocol)
- Explicit caveat: HRV data is a decision-support input, not the final word — "you have to listen to your body" above all else. (PRACTICE)
- Introduced during base-training phase deliberately (not during peak intensity) specifically to build a low-noise baseline/routine before high-intensity training is layered in, and to build trend-line history in advance. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #206: True or False, Running Injury Edition with Brodie Sharpe
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Brodie Sharpe, physiotherapist (Australia) and host of the Run Smarter podcast, specializing in running injury prevention/rehab.

**Concepts & claims:**
- "Pain is your friend" debate: Sharpe argues false (pain is often misinterpreted; distinguishes "good pain" — DOMS — from "bad pain" — joint/ligament overreaction); host argues true from a coaching-signal perspective. Both converge on: acute pain is a fairly reliable, easily interpreted guide. (DISAGREEMENT, then reconciled) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if pain-as-signal already covered; the acute/chronic distinction below is the sharper, less common content)
- Chronic pain (lasting months+) is NOT necessarily a sign tissue hasn't healed — most tissues (muscle, ligament) heal within weeks to a couple months at most; pain persisting far beyond that is reframed via pain-science/neuroscience: the brain generates pain based on perceived threat, not tissue damage, and can keep producing pain long after tissue has healed due to learned fear/beliefs. Cites the "bee sting" analogy: a soccer player vs. a professional violinist stung on the finger will experience different pain intensity for identical tissue damage because of what's at stake for each. (SCIENCE, general pain-neuroscience literature referenced but no specific study named) (ANALOGY: bee-sting/violinist) (POSSIBLE GAP — central-sensitization/pain-science framing for runners is a distinct topic from generic "listen to pain")
- PEACE & LOVE protocol introduced as the modern replacement for RICE: Protect, Elevate, Avoid anti-inflammatories, Compress, Educate (acute) / Load, Optimism, Vascularization, Exercise (later phase). Claim: NSAIDs can delay healing/lengthen recovery time for overuse (non-trauma) injuries and are increasingly not recommended for that reason. (SCIENCE, named acronym/protocol, described as "new... in the literature" but no specific citation) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Chronic-pain management approach: education about healing timelines, correcting inaccurate information from providers (e.g., "tendons don't heal" mythology), building a graded-exposure "flare-up management plan," and gradually rebuilding tolerance/confidence via small stepped progressions. (PRACTICE)
- "Prehab is better than rehab" — both agree true. Named mechanism: runners are motivationally reactive, not proactive — they keep running through a niggle until it blocks a meaningful activity (stairs, playing with kids), at which point motivation spikes but the injury is now much harder to treat. Injury risk can never be reduced to zero; the goal is catching niggles on day 1–2 rather than day 30. (PRACTICE)
- "Best to completely rest an injury" — both agree false, with stress fractures as the explicit exception requiring real rest/immobilization avoidance debate (host notes he prefers avoiding boot immobilization even for tibial stress fractures, staying mobile without weight-bearing). Complete rest of everything (not just the injured area) risks deconditioning other tissues, creating a "snowball" re-injury risk elsewhere. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the emphasis on maintaining fitness elsewhere in the body during localized injury, and the "pain, rest, weakness downward spiral" model below, is a specific, nameable framework)
- Guidance for running through a tendon-related injury: pain up to ~3/10 during the run is acceptable as long as it doesn't worsen after cooldown and, critically, the next morning (first-steps stiffness/soreness the following morning is the key overuse signal that's often misread when people feel fine mid-run). (PRACTICE, specific actionable protocol) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Pain, rest, weakness" downward spiral named explicitly: injury → rest → deconditioning/weakening → lower tolerance → re-flare at even lower loads → more rest → further weakening, in a self-reinforcing cycle. Illustrated with an elderly-osteoarthritis walking example and a plantar fasciitis example. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE framework) (POSSIBLE GAP — named, structured model worth capturing verbatim)
- "Running is bad for your knees" — both agree false; cites a systematic review of ~125,000 people finding knee osteoarthritis prevalence of 3.5% in recreational runners vs. 10% in sedentary people (runners ~3x less likely to develop OA). Mechanistic explanation: OA is not caused by cumulative "wear and tear" from ground-reaction forces; moderate ground-reaction-force loading actually stimulates cartilage and bone growth. (SCIENCE, specific large study referenced though not named/citable by title) (POSSIBLE GAP — good counter to the "running wrecks your knees" myth with an actual number)
- Host adds a memorable analogy: viewing a body-donation museum exhibit comparing a runner's femur (thicker, more robust) to a sedentary person's femur, illustrating Wolff's-law-style bone adaptation ("use it or lose it"). (ANALOGY)
- "Shoes can prevent injury" — both agree false as a blanket claim; no evidence that shoe category (stability/motion-control) or matching shoe to arch/foot shape reduces injury risk; changing shoes frequently/rotating multiple pairs has some evidence for reducing injury by varying loading patterns. Host references his own blog "The Myth of Overpronation." Shoe replacement should be guided by wear, comfort, or biomechanical drift — not a fixed mileage number. (DISAGREEMENT with mainstream running-shoe-retail marketing) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site already covers footwear/shoe myths, but the "change shoes frequently reduces injury risk" specific claim may be a gap)
- "Stretching before/after running does nothing to reduce injury risk" — both agree true (specifically static stretching); no evidence for injury prevention, performance enhancement, or recovery enhancement. Exception carved out for a small subgroup of very stiff runners (possibly elderly or with arthritic conditions) whose biomechanics may be altered by stiffness. Advice: only keep stretching if you already do it and it "feels good," don't start if you don't. Yoga discussion: some runners who do heavy yoga become hypermobile, which can reduce running-motion stability/efficiency — runners need rigidity/stiffness (elastic "spring" quality) for good running economy, not extreme flexibility. (DISAGREEMENT with common wisdom) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if site covers stretching myths already)

## Episode #220: Anatomy of a Down Week
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Solo episode, host Chris McClung only.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Down weeks serve two purposes: (1) injury prevention by giving connective tissue periodic relief from peak stress, and (2) "consolidating fitness gains" — the claim that fitness adaptation happens during recovery, not during the hard training stimulus itself (training tears down, rest builds up), applied at the weekly-cycle level the same way it applies to daily recovery. (SCIENCE, general supercompensation/adaptation theory, not attributed to a specific source) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if periodization/supercompensation already covered)
- Recommended down-week cadence: every 3rd week (2 up weeks, 1 down week) for Rogue's full range of athletes from beginner to advanced; notes some elite programs use a 4th-week-down or a 10-day micro-cycle instead, but frames the 3-week model as an approximation of a 10-day elite cycle adapted for working professionals' weekly schedules (weekday long runs aren't feasible for most). (PRACTICE, specific prescriptive cadence) (POSSIBLE GAP if site's periodization content doesn't specify this particular 3-week-cycle rationale)
- Down-week volume reduction target: overall weekly mileage down 10–20% from peak, with most of that reduction concentrated in the long run (drop ~20–25%, e.g., ~4 miles less) and the medium-long run (drop ~20%, e.g., ~2 miles less), while keeping all other easy days roughly unchanged. (PRACTICE, specific numeric protocol) (POSSIBLE GAP — this granular, run-type-specific breakdown of where mileage should come off is more prescriptive than generic "cut back sometimes" advice)
- Explicit rule: keep the same number of running days during a down week — don't drop training days, only reduce volume on the days you keep, because dropping days risks losing consistency/neuromuscular rhythm and increases injury risk when ramping back up. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Explicit rule: keep quality/speed work during down weeks but shift it toward shorter total volume, more rest between reps, and often faster paces (e.g., working up toward 5K pace with generous recovery) to maintain fast-twitch fiber engagement without adding fatigue — described as a "speed development" opportunity embedded in recovery weeks. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Advice to reinvest the "found time" from reduced mileage into recovery modalities (naps, massage, high-quality post-run meals, foam rolling) rather than adding something new to the routine — explicit warning against the "down week temptation" to add a new activity, new diet change, or extra project just because there's spare time/energy (parallels "taper madness"). (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY: compares the sluggish feeling of a down week to "taper madness") (POSSIBLE GAP — this "don't add anything new" caution is a specific, nameable pitfall)
- Explicit myth correction: athletes commonly expect to feel good/fresh during a down week and often don't — feeling sluggish during the down week itself is normal and doesn't indicate the approach isn't working; the payoff shows up in the following up-week. (DISAGREEMENT with common expectation) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Down-week guidelines explicitly framed as flexible ranges, not rigid rules — athletes should adjust the 10–20% (or more, up to 25–30% in rare cases) based on subjective fatigue.

## Episode #221: Pain, Clinical Somatics, and Running Injuries with Sarah Warren
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Sarah Warren, somatic educator and author of "The Pain Relief Secret: How to Retrain Your Nervous System, Heal Your Body and Overcome Chronic Pain"; former serious ballet dancer with a history of back injuries; trained in clinical somatics (lineage: F.M. Alexander → Feldenkrais → Thomas Hanna). Host discusses his own case history (plantar nerve impingement/chronic heel pain) throughout as a running thread.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Clinical somatics lineage and definitions: "somatic movement" defined precisely as any movement done with 100% internal attention on sensation (eyes closed, focused inward) as opposed to copying an external form (mirror, instructor) — this internal-attention criterion is what makes a movement "somatic" and is claimed to be what allows nervous-system retraining. Lineage: F.M. Alexander (posture/proprioception, early-to-mid 20th c.) → Moshe Feldenkrais (engineering-influenced self-care movement method, 1000+ exercises) → Thomas Hanna (Feldenkrais's student, added neurophysiological science/explanation, wrote "Somatics," coined "clinical somatics"). (SCIENCE/history, named lineage and sources) (POSSIBLE GAP — likely entirely new territory for a site that doesn't already cover somatic movement therapy)
- Core mechanism: "pandiculation" — the innate, hardwired nervous-system reflex seen in animals waking up and stretching (not passive muscle-lengthening but active contraction followed by very slow release). Clinical somatics exercises deliberately contract a muscle group well past its resting tension (e.g., to 80–90%) then release it as slowly as possible against gravity, which is claimed to send the nervous system accurate biofeedback about actual muscle tension and gradually lower the chronic "resting level of tension" the nervous system has learned to hold. (SCIENCE, named researcher/mechanism — Thomas Hanna's neurophysiology work, not an independent citation) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Direct claim/disagreement with static stretching: static stretching (pulling a passive muscle) triggers the myotatic (stretch) reflex — a protective nervous-system reflex that reflexively contracts the muscle to prevent tissue tearing (same reflex tested by the patellar tendon knee-tap) — meaning stretching doesn't durably lengthen muscle or change nervous-system tension-setting; any looseness felt post-stretch is temporary and resets by the next day. Contrasts explicitly with pandiculation's claimed durable effect. (SCIENCE, general reflex physiology) (DISAGREEMENT — conflicts directly with any site content recommending static stretching for flexibility/injury prevention; also conflicts somewhat with ep. 206's more neutral "stretch if it feels good" stance) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Muscle memory framed as the root cause of chronic tension patterns: repeated daily postures (e.g., hunching at a computer) get reinforced by the nervous system as "efficient" defaults, becoming automatic/subconscious holding patterns — not a structural or purely muscular problem but a learned nervous-system pattern, which is why massage/chiropractic adjustments (externally applied) give only temporary relief — the retraining has to be done "from the inside" by the individual. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Three-category clinical pain taxonomy: nociceptive (tissue damage/threat signaling — most common), neuroplastic (nervous system pathways adapt to amplify chronic pain beyond the original tissue signal, can begin within days of chronic pain onset), neuropathic (nerve damage from injury, surgery, or disease — diabetes, autoimmune disease, stroke, alcoholism cited as causes). Chronic pain often involves overlap of more than one category. (SCIENCE, named clinical framework, not attributed to a specific paper) (POSSIBLE GAP — overlaps with but is more granular than ep. 206's brain/pain-belief discussion; the same underlying pain-science territory recurs across two episodes in this batch — worth flagging as a recurring theme, not a duplicate)
- Practical rule of thumb for when to worry about pain: pain that appears once and never recurs can be ignored; pain that is persistent or recurring warrants attention — the sooner it's addressed the better. Advocates for developing fine-grained interoceptive awareness (noticing very subtle "off" sensations before they become painful) as a trainable skill, achieved through regular somatic practice, that allows real-time postural/movement micro-adjustments to head off injury. (PRACTICE)
- Stress-pain bidirectional feedback loop: pain activates the stress response and elevates baseline muscle tension; chronic pain itself causes stress/anxiety/depression (loss of function, disbelief from providers/loved ones); stress in turn heightens the brain's subjective experience of pain and increases muscle tension, compounding the original problem — cited as the rationale for prescribing anti-anxiety medication to some chronic pain patients (reducing anxiety measurably reduces reported pain). (SCIENCE, general mechanism, no specific study named) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if stress-pain interaction already covered under sports psych/recovery, though the explicit bidirectional-loop framing may sharpen it) (also connects thematically to ep. 204's stress-management content in this same batch)
- Specific injury mechanism claims for runners: (1) plantar fasciitis is described not as a localized foot problem but as the end point of a full posterior-chain tension pattern (lower back → glutes → hamstrings → calves → Achilles → plantar fascia) — treating just the foot is claimed to be less effective than releasing the whole chain. (2) Sciatica/piriformis syndrome both stem from nerve compression (spinal exit point vs. piriformis muscle respectively) typically caused by chronic lower-back/glute tension common in runners; claimed to often resolve quickly by releasing lower-back and glute tension, ideally paired with a brief training break. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE, practitioner's clinical experience, not a cited trial) (POSSIBLE GAP — the posterior-chain framing of plantar fasciitis is a distinct model from typical "roll your arch" advice)
- General self-care/lifestyle advice tied back to injury prevention: 8 hours of sleep, time outdoors, regular exercise, regular tension release, talk therapy for recurring life stress, unstructured "play" (references the book "Play"), balanced diet — framed as reducing the baseline stress/tension load that predisposes to injury. (PRACTICE, fairly generic) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery/lifestyle advice almost certainly already covered)
- Host's own case study used as running example throughout: an ankle mobility restriction from high mileage led to plantar nerve impingement, which was medically released but stayed "irritated," which led to compensatory glute/quad deactivation and secondary ipsilateral knee pain — presented as an illustration of the chain-reaction/compensation pattern discussed above, and of comparing symptomatic-side vs. non-symptomatic-side normal stiffness to recalibrate perceived severity (noticing his "bad" right heel felt similar to normal morning stiffness on his uninjured left heel, which reduced his perceived pain threshold on the right). (anecdote illustrating the awareness/interoception concept above)
