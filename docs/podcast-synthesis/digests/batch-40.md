## Episode #406: Nailing Your Long Run Workouts
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris McClung (head coach/owner, Rogue Running, Austin) and James Dodds (Rogue coach) discussing chapter 20 of McClung's coaching book, covering long run "quality" workouts (long runs with embedded pace work).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Long run workouts exist to apply progressive overload and "specificity of training" — since race day requires running farther at faster paces than typical long runs, the long run workout is the safe way to rehearse that demand. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/specificity)
- Framing device: treat long run workouts as a "race simulation" / "dress rehearsal," analogous to a theater dress rehearsal or a soccer friendly match — the goal is to test routine, gear, terrain, pacing, nutrition/hydration, and mental tools, not to achieve a perfect result. Mistakes are welcomed because they surface weaknesses to fix before race day. (ANALOGY) (PRACTICE) — a reusable framing not obviously covered on a typical site (POSSIBLE GAP: "race simulation" as an explicit named practice, distinct from generic tapering/practice runs)
- Practical detail: simulate race-day gear (shoes, singlet), terrain (e.g., Rogue's "Boston Simulator" — a net-downhill course with four climbs mimicking Newton Hills), and nutrition/hydration strategy during these runs. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — terrain-specific simulator courses is a fairly specific tactic)
- Do #1: it's a simulator — mimic race day as closely as possible in routine, gear, terrain, pace, fueling. (PRACTICE)
- Do #2: challenge yourself but don't overdo it — treat it as progressive/incremental, not a chance to "prove" fitness in one session; coach gives example of once over-cooking race preps at Rogue ~10+ years ago, and of personally "running his race" on his final long run workout 15 years prior and fading on race day as a result. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — cautionary real coaching anecdote)
- Do #3: build progressively across long run workouts (typically 1–3 per cycle) — first is small race-pace segments (2–4 x 2mi), second introduces "in and out" pacing (faster surges mixed with marathon pace), third is most aggressive, closest simulation of race day with early and late progressions. Coach writes long run quality #3 first and works backward to shape #1 and #2 ("keystone" design principle). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "design workout #3 first, work backward" planning technique)
- Placement in periodization: long run workouts belong late in a cycle (end of strength phase into race-specific phase), never in the priming/base phase; last big one falls ~3 weeks out for marathon, ~2 weeks for half, before taper. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/tapering)
- Do #4: combine race pace with faster-than-race-pace segments rather than pure race-pace running — this makes race pace "feel easier" via contrast, and trains the athlete to efficiently find/settle into race pace repeatedly (a real marathon skill, e.g. watch/GPS failing in a tunnel). Longest race-pace segment recommended even for advanced marathoners is ~5 miles, not 10. (PRACTICE) (SCIENCE-adjacent but not evidence-cited — asserted from coaching experience) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "recovery at race pace is a trainable skill" framing)
- Faster surges within long runs (e.g., a 5K-pace fartlek segment) are used specifically to generate fatigue that mimics late-race tiredness (e.g., "mile 12 feels like mile 18") without requiring the runner to actually run 20+ miles at race pace — deliberately manufacturing specificity of fatigue rather than specificity of distance. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — fatigue-simulation-via-intensity as distinct from distance-simulation)
- Don't #1 (most important): never add distance and intensity at the same time — must run a given distance easy first before adding a quality/workout element at that same distance; skipping long-run-quality steps in sequence (e.g., missing #1, jumping to #2) is also prohibited. Framed as the single biggest injury-risk violation ("red card"). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general injury-prevention/progressive overload, though this specific "don't stack stressors" rule for long run quality is a sharper, more specific version)
- Coach's practical rule: needs at least six 20-milers in a marathon program to give three long-run-quality workouts at 20 miles, since each first requires a plain 20-miler at that volume before adding intensity. (PRACTICE)
- Don't #2: don't run race pace for the entire long run (not even mostly) — physiologically too stressful, wastes the fitness built from easy aerobic running, and functionally becomes "already running your race" in practice, which ironically tells you nothing useful and can pre-spend fitness. Cited real anecdote: athletes at community talks reporting 20-mile race-pace practice runs, to the coach's private alarm. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the common belief that runners should test full race pace/distance before race day)
- "Elastic thinking"/zoom-in-zoom-out concept (attributed loosely to Steve Magness) — confidence for race day comes not from one single long-run observation but from trusting the cumulative, distributed stress load across an entire training block (weekly rhythms, two-up/one-down cycles, workouts, lactate/muscle fiber adaptation over ~20-24 weeks). (PRACTICE) (SCIENCE — named researcher/coach reference, though underlying physiology claims are asserted, not cited to a specific study) (ANALOGY — "puzzle" analogy: no single training piece gives the complete picture; you need easy runs, mediums, recovery runs, quality progression, long run workouts, and mental prep all together)
- Don't #3: don't bury yourself — must be willing to pull the plug mid-workout if form falls apart, injury twinges appear, or things feel wrong; "the number one reason people don't hit race goals is they don't make it to the starting line." (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY — over-easy-egg / slow-scrambled-eggs metaphor: cook these workouts on "low heat," with patience, rather than rushing/forcing — practice executing them under many different conditions ("as many different stovetops") to master adaptability.)
- Don't #4: don't stack long run workouts too close together — minimum ~2 weeks apart, ideally 3 weeks (fits Rogue's standard "2 up weeks, 1 down week" 21-day cycle); newer athletes should space them even further (4 weeks). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery/periodization cadence)
- Named example workout structure: "McClung Special" — full recipe reserved for the book, but described as spanning a wide range of paces (slower-than-race-pace opener building into race pace, "start simulation," fartlek-style middle, and a faster finish/negative-split simulation). Other named/described workouts: race-pace intervals (2-4 x 2-3mi), fartlek "in and out" (e.g., 1 min faster / 4-5 min marathon pace repeated in 20-min chunks), tempo-in-the-middle builds, and "close simulation" finishes progressing toward half-marathon pace to rehearse negative splits. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for the general concept of race-pace long runs, but the specific named-workout catalog is more granular than typical site coverage — POSSIBLE GAP for the actual workout recipes)

## Episode #407: But WHY do YOU Run?
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — Chris McClung and James Dodds, covering chapter 21 (opening of the book's "mental side" section) on crafting a personal statement of purpose for running.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central quote/framing: Nietzsche's "He who has a why can bear any how" — used to justify spending deliberate time defining a deep, personal "why" for running, distinct from surface reasons (stress relief, health, longevity) which don't require racing or goal-setting to obtain. (ANALOGY/quote) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit "purpose statement" as a structured coaching exercise, not just generic motivation advice)
- Six-step process for writing a statement of purpose: (1) write it down via stream-of-consciousness / free-writing without judgment (explicitly modeled on Julia Cameron's "The Artist's Way" morning pages practice) — pen-to-paper surfaces deeper thoughts than rumination alone; (2) give yourself real time (weeks, in 30–60 min chunks, letting it "marinate"); (3) don't multitask — dedicated, distraction-free focus time (Ron Swanson-attributed "don't half-ass two things, whole-ass one thing" quoted as a memorable line); (4) keep it succinct — ideally one sentence completing "I run for performance because…", since longer statements tend to blend multiple reasons rather than isolate the single most important one; (5) use thought-starter questions — what emotions does running bring (including "borrowing from the future" by imagining crossing the finish line, and "borrowing from the past" by recalling emotional past milestones), what inspires you in the running world (example given: Daniel Komen and David Rudisha admired for competing at the highest level while remaining humble/self-possessed), who inspires you outside running, and what deeper personal philosophy running connects to (host's example: integrity/congruence between inner values and outer action); (6) own it and let it evolve — read the statement aloud to yourself and to a trusted person as a "gut check," and revisit/rewrite it periodically (roughly every 1-3 years, and notably after achieving a major goal, since goal-achievement can otherwise leave an athlete feeling empty — described as guarding against "postpartum blues" after a big goal). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a full structured methodology for purpose-setting that goes beyond typical "find your why" advice)
- Named recurring purpose "themes" offered as examples/prompts (not prescriptive): proving something to yourself (framed as evolving from "I'm not sure I can" toward a "celebration of what I already know" over time); proving something to others (noted as extrinsically motivated — "science would tell us intrinsic motivation is more powerful than extrinsic," though no specific study cited); inspiring others; adventure/curiosity (cites ultrarunner Courtney Dauwalter's concept of exploring the "pain cave" as a curiosity-driven motive); and problem-solving (illustrated via ultra/triathlon athlete Paul Terranova, who reportedly pursued extreme endurance challenges — including a "Grand Slam" of four 100-mile races plus Kona Ironman in one year — primarily to solve logistical/physiological puzzles rather than to prove anything or inspire others). (PRACTICE) (SCIENCE claim about intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation is asserted, not sourced) (POSSIBLE GAP — the taxonomy of purpose "themes" is a distinctive angle)
- Memorable quote attributed to Paul Terranova: "You can choose the pain of regret or the pain of discipline" — used as a race-morning/alarm-clock mantra. (ANALOGY/quote) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — discipline-vs-regret framing is common in sports psychology, but the specific quote/attribution may be worth preserving verbatim)

## Episode #408: Developing Mental Fortitude
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — Chris McClung and James Dodds, continuing the book's mental-training section (chapter on building mental resilience).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central question framed and answered: is mental fortitude innate or trainable? Hosts argue it is overwhelmingly built/trainable (nurture), not fixed talent, though acknowledge some component could be innate. James cites the book "Willpower" by Roy Baumeister (a real named source) — willpower/mental fortitude behaves like a muscle that fatigues over the course of a day, which is offered as an explanation for why "judges make their sloppiest decisions on Thursday afternoons" and politicians get in trouble late in the week. (SCIENCE — named author/book, specific claim about decision fatigue) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology/mental toughness, but the ego-depletion/decision-fatigue angle with Baumeister citation is a more specific, checkable claim) — NOTE: ego depletion research has been substantially challenged by replication failures since Baumeister's original work; a fact-checker synthesizing this should flag that the science here is contested, not settled.
- Reframe: "I'm just not mentally strong" is treated as a limiting self-story analogous to "I'm just not good at math" — both are framed as changeable narratives rather than fixed truths. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY)
- Bicep-in-the-gym analogy: don't just compare your current mental toughness to someone else's and conclude they "have it and you don't" — instead ask "how did they build that," turning a fixed comparison into an actionable question. (ANALOGY)
- Central Governor Theory (attributed by name to Tim Noakes) is mentioned as one model of how the brain limits physical performance as a protective mechanism before true physiological limits are reached; hosts note there are competing/disputing theories but say most models agree the brain shuts things down well before actual limits, which is why mental training can push the "governor" back. (SCIENCE — named researcher/theory, explicitly flagged by the hosts as contested among scientists) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site already covers Central Governor Theory under exercise physiology; otherwise POSSIBLE GAP)
- Five components of mental-training practice presented: (1) Know your plan — have a detailed race plan (pacing, nutrition, mental tools) to "control the controllables" and limit decision-making/emotional load during the race; quoted line: "a bad plan is better than no plan," since even an imperfect plan gives you something concrete to iterate on. (PRACTICE)
- (2) Bring your tools/mental "weapons" — pre-race visualization (visualize the entire race start-to-finish in chunks over the final 4-6 weeks of training, on easy runs); claim that visualization produces real nervous-system responses similar to physically performing the action (SCIENCE — asserted, no specific study named), illustrated via Aaron Rodgers' anecdote about a difficult throw he said he'd "done in his head" before making it in a game. Positive self-talk / mantras, split into "rhythm mantras" (early/middle miles, meditative) and "fight mantras" (late-race, for pushing through pain) — explicitly distinguished from cliché/toxic-positivity self-talk ("I'm good enough, I'm smart enough…"); the claim is that repeated self-narratives function as programming because "our brains are information receiving machines," so habitually negative self-talk ("I'm slow, I always blow up") is described as actively harmful, equated by the coach to self-absorption. Also named: positive affirmations (spoken to self or reinforced by others), "going fishing" (using a competitor ahead as a focal distraction from pain), counting (e.g., counting to 30/60 repeatedly as a neutral mantra to break a race into manageable chunks), and landmark-based counting (host's example: counting down 10 street-crossing landmarks in the final mile of a Houston Marathon PR to trigger a "gradual unleashing" kick). (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY — mantras/landmarks) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psych self-talk/visualization is probably covered already, though the rhythm-vs-fight mantra taxonomy and landmark-countdown technique are more specific, actionable tactics — borderline POSSIBLE GAP for the specific taxonomy)
- (3) Build the mentality of "I can"/never-quit — explicitly reframed as NOT requiring perfection or "superhero" continuous effort; the real skill is bouncing back after doubt/bad patches, not avoiding them. Illustrated with host's account of dropping out mentally never but physically reduced to walking the last 5 miles of the 2016 Boston Marathon due to a stress fracture, finishing in a personal-worst time he calls one of his proudest races because he "gave all he had" in the form available to him that day. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — reframing "mental toughness" as resilience/recovery rather than unbroken intensity is a distinct, nuanced angle worth preserving)
- Neuroscience tangent: James cites Andrew Huberman (named, real neuroscientist) on the anterior mid-cingulate cortex (aMCC) — claimed to grow/activate specifically when a person chooses to do something difficult-but-beneficial that they don't want to do, and described (per Huberman, secondhand) as linked to "will to live" and offered as a neuroscientific explanation for how David Goggins-style extreme discipline is built through repeated small choices (e.g., taking electrolytes, going to bed on time before a long run). (SCIENCE — named neuroscientist/brain region, but this is a secondhand paraphrase in a podcast, not a direct citation — flag for fact-checking the specific aMCC/"will to live" claim) (POSSIBLE GAP — the aMCC-and-micro-choices framing is a distinctive, fact-checkable science angle not likely already on the site)
- (4) Apply and learn — treat every race (good or bad) as a repetition that builds the "same mental muscle" used both when things go well (closing hard) and when they go poorly (grinding through a bad day); mental resilience is asserted to be built cumulatively via daily training choices, not just in races. (PRACTICE)
- (5) Do it with a team — a training partner's success can directly shift an athlete's internal belief ("why not me?"). Named real example: host trained alongside Allison Maxis (4x Olympic Trials qualifier, Rogue co-coach) around 2014; being consistently shoulder-to-shoulder with her in workouts eventually shifted his internal narrative and coincided with PRs in every distance except the 5K over the following ~18 months. Coined mantra: "Why not me?" (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY/anecdote) — related idea: "remember who's watching you" as a source of positive obligation/inspiration rather than pressure, and "running is only a solo sport if you want it to be." (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training-group/team culture and social motivation is probably covered under sports psychology or coaching methodology generally, though the specific "why not me" mechanism and team-culture framing may add nuance)

## Episode #409: Creating Your Vision and Goals
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — Chris McClung and James Dodds, continuing the mental-training section with a chapter on vision-setting and goal-setting.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Vision vs. goals framework: vision is "capital A to capital B" — a timeless, long-term, multi-dimensional dream state (not anchored to a specific timeline); goals are the "little a's to little b's," the specific, time-bound milestones that bridge current reality to that vision. (ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — vision as explicitly distinct from and prior to goal-setting, with its own dedicated exercise, is a structured framework beyond generic "set SMART goals" content)
- Recommended tool: physical vision board (cutting images from magazines, poster board, arranging/grouping by theme) — explicitly modeled on Julia Cameron's "The Artist's Way" exercise (same book referenced in Episode 407). Framed as deliberately "arts and crafts," not just words — because vision should be visual/multi-dimensional (accolades, values, how you want to work, characteristics of the journey), not just a single outcome image. Alternative for word-oriented people: start with brainstormed lists/questions (heroes in the sport, specific goals like "qualify for Boston," etc.) and translate into imagery afterward. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — vision boards as a concrete, specific tool are not something a typical evidence-based running site would likely already include, and may register as "soft"/unconventional coaching content worth flagging even if included)
- "Duality of mind" concept: you must let yourself dream periodically (vision board) but cannot stay in the dreaming state — you must return to being present and execute the next concrete step; getting "stuck in the middle" (fixating on months-away milestones) leads to overwhelm, shortcuts, or under/over-training. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY)
- "Spirit animal" exercise — a playful supplementary tool used with Rogue's virtual team athletes to characterize how an athlete wants to show up in their journey (examples given: "the Buffalo" for an athlete who runs through adversity rather than around it, referencing the folk claim that buffalo run through storms while cattle flee them; "the Fox"; "the Owl" self-described by host as wise/analytical with "killer instinct"). Host connects this to a Jordan Peterson-derived thesis ("the world manifests itself according to your aim") and a personal anecdote about noticing hawks while driving, framed as attention/salience bias rather than anything mystical. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY) — the "buffalo runs through the storm" claim is a folk zoological claim, not verified science; flag as unverified natural-history trivia if ever repeated. (POSSIBLE GAP as a novel coaching device, though clearly a "soft"/optional tool)
- Goal-setting: process goals vs. outcome goals — hosts assert (citing coach/writer Steve Magness by name as having recently spoken on the science of goal-types) that process goals are more effective than outcome goals, and recommend always pairing an outcome goal with specific, metric-based process goals (e.g., weekly mileage, number of quality sessions, recovery-run pace discipline) since outcomes are not fully controllable. (SCIENCE — named source, Magness, though the specific study/mechanism isn't detailed) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — process vs. outcome goals is a fairly standard sports-psychology concept, likely already covered)
- Hank Aaron anecdote (used to illustrate process-over-outcome): reportedly said he never set a goal of hitting a specific number of home runs per year, but instead set process goals around batting practice time, studying pitchers, and strength/mobility work — home run totals were the byproduct. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY/anecdote — historical claim about Aaron's stated approach, not independently verified here, worth a fact-check if used)
- First marathon guidance: recommend NOT setting a time goal for a first marathon — the outcome goal should simply be "finish," with process goals governing preparation; time can be "held loosely" via pacing plan without being the target. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general race-goal-setting advice)
- "No Plan B" principle: hosts strongly advise against setting backup A/B/C goals for outcome-based goals, arguing a backup goal functions as a psychological escape hatch/crutch that undermines full commitment; failure to hit the single A-goal is reframed as acceptable ("failure is only quitting, not missing a goal") as long as full effort was given. Illustrated with Rogue athlete Travis, who reportedly held a "no plan B" mentality qualifying for Boston ("I only asked how, not if"). (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly pushes back against the common goal-setting advice of having A/B/C contingency goals) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a fairly strong, specific, contrarian coaching stance worth flagging for a synthesis pass)
- "If you're absolutely certain you can do it, it's not a goal" — a goal must carry genuine uncertainty/stretch; right amount of uncertainty is described as individual-specific, and a coach's job is partly calibrating that uncertainty band. Coach's stated heuristic (explicitly framed as not evidence-based, just a communication device): athletes should start a season around "20% confident" and the coach's job is to get them to "80% confident" by race day — never 100% ("guarantee"), because 100% confidence just makes it "practice," not a real goal. (PRACTICE) (explicitly NOT SCIENCE — self-flagged as a non-quantitative, illustrative device) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific confidence-percentage coaching heuristic is a distinctive, nameable technique)
- Handling unrealistic goals: coach's approach is to relabel an athlete's too-ambitious near-term target as belonging to the long-term "vision" bucket rather than rejecting it outright, then ground the immediate goal-setting conversation in present, factual training data; coach notes he has directly lost at least one athlete over a blunt conversation about unrealistic outcome goals. (PRACTICE)
- Metric-based process tracking: recommends explicit numeric process targets (e.g., "33 of 36 possible strength sessions this cycle") and physical/paper (not just digital) habit-tracking grids, on the theory that people commonly have a "mismatch between what they're doing and what they think they're doing." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — habit tracking is common in general training-adherence advice)

## Episode #73: What is Quality? (Part 1)
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Steve (Rogue Running co-host/coach, referred to as "Steve" throughout — collegiate coaching background, including coaching at University of Texas and 800m/mid-distance specialists) and Chris McClung. Note: this is a much older episode (2018-era, pre-406/407/408/409 by several years, references Kiprop doping news and 2019/2020 Olympic Trials planning), so the content is more purely X's-and-O's training-methodology focused, not from "the book."

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core definition of a "quality workout": "hard running with a purpose" (preferred over "fast running with a purpose") — done either to drive a physiological adaptation or to prepare psychologically/tactically for a specific race distance. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — standard training-workout taxonomy)
- Car-engine analogy: easy/long/consistent aerobic running builds "the size of your engine" (aerobic capacity/foundation); quality/speed workouts mostly "fine-tune the engine" you've already built — most of the speed benefit is about extracting more from existing aerobic capacity, not increasing engine size. Getting faster is misattributed by many runners solely to hard workouts, when aerobic volume is doing the heavier lifting. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base training, though the specific "engine size vs. tuning" framing is a nice teaching analogy that may be worth borrowing verbatim)
- Running economy reframed: getting "faster" is really about becoming able to run previously-hard paces "more easily" — i.e., improved economy/efficiency, not raw power or explosiveness. (SCIENCE-adjacent, asserted) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — running economy)
- Four categories of quality workouts proposed (own framework, explicitly called "a little arbitrary" by the hosts): (1) running economy workouts, (2) VO2max/speed workouts, (3) threshold/aerobic-strength workouts, (4) race-specific workouts — plus a "mixed" category covered in part 2. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general workout taxonomy, though specific category definitions below add detail)
- Strides defined precisely: a sub-maximal sprint at ~95% of absolute max sprint speed (practically ~70-90%+ effort ramping up over weeks), lasting 10-15 seconds / 50-75 meters, with full walk-back recovery between reps (4-6 reps) — explicitly NOT intended to build fitness/generate lactate, but to "inoculate" the neuromuscular system toward smoother, more efficient mechanics that transfer to other running. Recommended near-year-round, after easy runs only (one coach's strict rule; the other coach is more permissive, allowing them after medium-long runs too). Divide each stride mentally into thirds: build to ~85-90%, hold, ease off without braking. Cue: relaxed face/neck/shoulders (referencing slow-motion sprint footage showing "wobbly" faces as the model of complete relaxation); exaggerate arm/knee action to find efficient form; emulate "the most beautiful running form you've ever seen." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — running economy/strides are probably covered, but the specific execution protocol — walk-back recovery, thirds-based pacing, relaxed-face cue — is detailed enough to be a POSSIBLE GAP if the site's current treatment is more general)
- Hill strides described as a distinct variant — requires a gently graded hill (not steep), explicit differing arm mechanics (throw the elbow back rather than lifting it like an uppercut) and more forceful ground push-off than flat strides; used mainly early in a training cycle/phase (to prepare tendons/ligaments and biomechanics for later hill work) or late in a 5K/10K-specific phase — but avoided late in marathon-specific cycles due to elevated injury risk from altered mechanics under fatigue. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific hill-stride mechanics cueing is a fairly granular, useful technical detail)
- Additional named economy-building workout variants: "straights and curves" (alternating stride-pace straightaways with easy curves on a track, e.g. 2x6 laps), "pickups" in the middle of easy runs (gradual 30-90 second surges up toward 5K pace, used partly to prevent group easy runs from drifting into unintentional tempo/race pace — a training-discipline tool as much as a physiological one), "in-and-out 200s," and "300m builds" (switching effort gears every 100m, 65%→75%→85-90%). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — fartlek/stride variants generally, though the "pickups as anti-drift discipline for group runs" rationale is a distinct practical insight)
- VO2max-zone quality work (5K-10K pace range, roughly 800m pace up to 10K pace) recommended in small "sprinkled" doses year-round, including during marathon/half-marathon training blocks, specifically because most marathoners under-do fast work and this is described as "a big differentiator" of Rogue's programming versus generic marathon plans that neglect true speed work. Claim: "the faster you can run a 5K, the faster you can run a marathon" — improving short-distance speed is asserted to raise the ceiling on long-distance race pace. (PRACTICE) (SCIENCE-adjacent, asserted without citation) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific claim that marathoners should regularly include real 5K/10K-pace work, and the "sprinkle it in monthly" cadence rationale, may be a genuinely underrepresented angle if the site's speed-work content skews toward threshold/tempo only)
- Named example VO2max workouts: 12 x 200m at 5K pace with equal-time easy jog recovery (~3 miles total quality volume); a marathon-adapted "10K ladder"/"Texas 10K" workout (attributed to coach Stan Huntsman, who reportedly coached Patrick Sang — described as Eliud Kipchoge's coach — and whose influence the host claims to see echoed in Kipchoge's training) — structure: 4x400 (100 jog) → 2x800 (200 jog) → 1x1600 (400 jog) → 2x800 (200 jog) → 4x400 (100 jog), all hard reps at 10K pace; and the classic "3-2-1 fartlek" (3 min at 10K pace / 3 min recovery, 2 min at 5K pace / 2 min recovery, 1 min faster-than-5K / 1 min recovery, repeated 2-3 sets). (PRACTICE) (SCIENCE — Stan Huntsman/Patrick Sang/Kipchoge lineage claim is a specific, checkable factual claim, presented as the host's personal belief/observation rather than confirmed fact) (POSSIBLE GAP — named, structured VO2max workout recipes with clear rep/recovery schemes are concrete and actionable, likely more granular than typical site content)
- Coaching cue specific to VO2max work: pace precision matters more here than in any other quality category — "pace, not effort" — because hitting the exact target pace is what "checks the physiological box," unlike other categories where effort-based flexibility is fine. Coaches claim they can identify what pace zone an athlete is running purely by listening to breathing patterns, and will stop/adjust an athlete whose form deteriorates (shoulders creeping up, arm flailing) since continuing in poor form both risks injury and stops delivering training benefit. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general workout-execution coaching, though "breathing as a real-time coaching diagnostic" and "pace is the one non-negotiable metric in VO2 work" are specific enough details to note)

## Episode #74: What is Quality? (Part 2)
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Same as Episode 73 — Steve and Chris McClung, continuing the quality-workout taxonomy (threshold, race-specific, and mixed categories).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Threshold/"aerobic strength" work defined loosely and deliberately without strict scientific precision — hosts explicitly decline to draw a hard scientific line between "lactate threshold," "anaerobic threshold," and "aerobic threshold" terminology, instead defining the practical training zone as "anything slower than 10K pace and anything faster than marathon pace." States plainly that they don't consider work across this whole zone (including paces slower than typically labeled "threshold") to be "junk miles." (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — implicitly pushes back on stricter physiological threshold-zone models some coaches/scientists use) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — lactate threshold, though the deliberately loose, practice-based zone definition contrasts with more precise physiological treatments a site might already have)
