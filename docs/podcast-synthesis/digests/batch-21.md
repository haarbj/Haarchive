## Episode #297: 6 Tips for Winter Training
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung (founder/head coach, Rogue Running, Austin TX; 20+ year competitive runner, 15+ year coach).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Choose a single "emphasis" for winter/off-season months (base volume, strength, cross-training variety, or top-end speed) rather than an all-or-nothing approach when weather/logistics limit normal training. (PRACTICE)
- "There's no bad weather, only bad gear for the weather." Recommends building a personal temperature-to-clothing reference chart (adjusted for wind/precipitation) refined year over year. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY)
- Cold-weather gear specifics: wool base layers (e.g., Smartwool) wick moisture and insulate even damp; if double-layering socks, the layer against skin must be thin/wicking or sweat gets trapped and can freeze the foot; traction devices (Yaktrax, Kahtoola) for ice/snow; you should feel slightly cold for the first 10-15 minutes of a run — if comfortable at the start, you're overdressed. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — practical cold-weather gear/logistics is a fairly narrow, actionable angle a physiology-and-coaching-focused site may not otherwise cover)
- Treadmill training tips: set incline to ~1% to mimic outdoor road resistance; convert short track intervals (200m/400m) into time-based fartlek-style efforts (e.g., 90-second surges) since precise short intervals are impractical on a treadmill; on elliptical/bike, target heart rate (recalled from a recent outdoor tempo) rather than pace. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — treadmill/cross-training workout-translation is a narrow practical topic likely not covered)
- Hydration/electrolyte needs don't disappear in winter just because sweat is less visible (drier air and evaporative cooling can mask fluid loss); recommends at least once-daily electrolyte supplementation in winter vs. twice-daily in summer, and deliberate rehydration after long/hard efforts even when "it didn't feel like I sweated much." (SCIENCE, asserted without a named study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general hydration/fueling, though the "winter false sense of security" framing is a specific nuance)
- Holiday/travel planning: proactively rearrange the schedule around travel (e.g., move the long run a day earlier), accept a shortened/modified run rather than skipping entirely ("something is always better than nothing"), and when normal workout infrastructure isn't available, substitute a fartlek that preserves the *purpose* of the workout (aerobic strength, VO2max, or easy volume) rather than its exact format. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general training-flexibility/consistency messaging)
- Goal-setting philosophy previewed here and expanded fully in Episode #299: dream without a timeline first, connect the goal to a specific "why," then add a timeline; advocates planning 12-18 months ahead "in pencil." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overlaps heavily with Ep #299 in this batch)

## Episode #298: Solo vs Group Training
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode, Chris McClung, responding to a listener-submitted topic (Victoria, FL).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Solo-running benefit as mental practice: recommends running without headphones, setting a loose "intention" (a topic to mull, not necessarily resolve) at the run's start, then letting the mind wander freely; capture any resulting insights in notes immediately afterward before they fade "like a dream." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific mental-practice/journaling technique distinct from standard race-day sports psychology content)
- Solo running lets an athlete stay strictly in their own prescribed effort zone without being pulled off-pace by training partners; requires self-imposed accountability substitutes instead — signing up for a race in advance, hiring a coach (even virtual/remote) to monitor progress, keeping a written plan and checking off completed sessions, or recruiting a non-running friend/partner simply to ask "did you do your run today?" (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general motivation/accountability, though the "non-running accountability partner" tactic is a specific wrinkle)
- Group training benefits: a slightly faster training partner provides a controlled way to find "another gear"; deep, unfiltered social bonding during long runs ("no masks" at 5:30am) extends into off-run friendship and support networks beyond social media. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY: "you simply can't hide from who you are")
- Explicit pushback on a common runner rationalization: "the group went too fast, but it wasn't my fault" is called out as an invalid excuse — individual pacing accountability must coexist with group dynamics; if a group creeps too fast, the athlete should communicate with the group or drop off rather than default to its pace. (DISAGREEMENT/PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — reinforces standard "run your own race" advice)
- Practical group-finding advice: local running-store-affiliated groups, virtual/online training groups, trying multiple groups until one fits both pace range and "ethos" (culture/vibe). (PRACTICE)

## Episode #299: 6 Steps to Set Goals and Plan for 2023
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode, Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Structured 6-step sequential goal-planning framework: (1) identify the single highest-priority long-term goal, timeline-free; (2) crystallize the specific personal "why" behind that goal into one written sentence — explicitly distinguished from general reasons for running (stress relief, health, community), since goal-specific motivation is typically about proving something to oneself; (3) identify training/component gaps needed (speed, strength, volume — not just target times) using an equivalent-race-time calculator (McMillan cited by name) compared against current PRs to objectively locate the biggest opportunity areas (worked example: a 4:00 marathon goal implies roughly 24:38/5K, 51:09/10K, 1:54/half); (4) pick races in the needed gap distances that are personally exciting, not just distance-filling; (5) sequence 2-3 "peak"/A-races per year, spaced 4-5 months apart, each cycle built around exactly one (max two) training-component focus; (6) build the actual periodized day-by-day training plan for the next cycle, referencing Episode #137 (with coach Jen Harney) for plan-building mechanics. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific sequencing and the equivalent-race-time gap-analysis diagnostic technique are more concrete/actionable than generic goal-setting content)
- Explicit physiological claim: "You can only peak two or three times a year... that's how human physiology works" — a detrain/retrain cycle naturally follows each peak, which is the basis for limiting A-races and single-focus training cycles. (SCIENCE, asserted without a named study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization fundamentals)
- Rule of thumb: don't increase peak weekly mileage by more than ~20% from one training cycle to the next (or a flat ~10 miles/week ceiling once above ~50 mpw). (PRACTICE, presented as personal rule of thumb) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — a variant of the standard "10% rule," though the per-cycle framing differs from the usual per-week framing)
- Claims intrinsic motivation (wanting to prove something to yourself) is "more powerful than extrinsic motivation, according to science," without naming a study or researcher. (SCIENCE, asserted/uncited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general motivation psychology)
- Personal throughline: McClung's own 20+ year pursuit of a sub-2:40 marathon (PR 2:45), used to illustrate long-horizon goal-setting in practice — echoed extensively in Episodes #300 and #301.

## Episode #300: 9 Reflections on Training, Coaching, and Living from 2022
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Chris McClung (host/coach), joined by James Dodds — fellow Rogue Running coach, co-coach of the "Rogue Renegades" virtual training group, and a former high-school powerlifter/college rugby player before transitioning to distance running. Episode marks the show's 300th installment.

**Concepts & claims:**
- "It's never a bad time to reset on the basics" — applies both to an athlete plateauing (McClung's own multi-year rebuild toward sub-2:40) and to a coach facing an unfamiliar sport/athlete (illustrated via coaching a HYROX-style hybrid strength-and-running athlete by deconstructing standard rules to first principles). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general coaching philosophy)
- Patience as an explicitly named, under-discussed strength; impatience is framed as manifesting in one of two failure modes: (a) quitting prematurely when results don't come fast enough (psychologically damaging despite short-term relief), or (b) overcompensating by doing too much too soon, risking injury. James Dodds ties failure-mode (b) to the "80-90% easy mileage" discipline. Illustrated with an athlete (Karen) who had a bad-weather Chicago Marathon, stayed patient, and got her first Boston Qualifier the following year. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — patience/long-term development, though naming the two specific failure branches is a useful teaching frame)
- Strength training (heavier external load, not bodyweight) takes far longer to manifest in running performance than expected — McClung estimates roughly 12 months of consistent heavy strength work before it shows up in race results, even though ancillary benefits (resilience, feeling "healthy") appear sooner. Ankle and hamstring mobility limitations had to be addressed *before* meaningfully progressing load. (PRACTICE, personal n=1 account) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "expect ~12 months, sequence mobility before load" guidance is more granular than typical general "strength training helps runners" content)
- James Dodds's distinct framing: for a high-mileage distance runner, the primary measurable benefit of strength training is often invisible — "the absence of injury" rather than a new capability — and bulking mass is physiologically in tension with high running volume, so runners shouldn't judge strength-training success by max-lift or hypertrophy metrics the way a bodybuilder or powerlifter would. (ANALOGY/PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, useful reframing of what "success" should look like in runner-specific strength training)
- Micronutrient deficiency as a hidden performance limiter: a 2016 stress fracture was traced to vitamin D deficiency compounded by a genetic absorption mutation (identified via 23andMe cross-referencing); a 2021 Boston Marathon's "inexplicable" late-race fatigue was later traced via micronutrient blood testing (with a named nutritionist, Carly) to deficiencies in copper, selenium, and zinc, possibly compounded by a prior COVID infection. Recommends blood work/micronutrient testing when performance or recovery is inexplicably off despite sound training, sleep, and stress management. (SCIENCE, presented as personal medical case history rather than a cited study) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete, actionable diagnostic pathway likely more specific than generic "eat a balanced diet" fueling content)
- Heart-rate-based training applied more rigorously in 2022 coaching (writing entire blocks around HR zones rather than pace); cites the Maffetone formula (180 minus age ≈ top of aerobic zone 2) as a usable rule of thumb, with the caveat that wrist-based HR is directionally, not absolutely, accurate — still useful for catching cases of running too hard on easy days. (SCIENCE, named formula/method — Maffetone — not a cited study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — HR training and the Maffetone method are fairly standard for an aerobic-base-focused site)
- "Seasons of life" framing: training capacity should be expected to ebb and flow with life stress (parenting, work, travel); proactively scaling back is correct, not a failure. James Dodds's financial-market analogy: a "bear market" season calls for patience and staying the course rather than panic-selling (abandoning training principles) or overreacting. (ANALOGY/PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the explicit, named "seasons of life" coaching frame for resetting expectations may be a genuinely underrepresented angle relative to purely physiological content)
- "Something is always better than nothing" and "reframing success" — a shortened/modified session during a busy period preserves aerobic connection and routine, and should be counted as a win (good training judgment) rather than a loss against the original plan. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general consistency messaging)
- Dual mental orientation: keep long-term dreams alive (for motivation/passion) while staying grounded in the single next concrete daily task, to avoid both demotivation (goal feels too far away) and complacency. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overlaps with general goal-setting content, though the explicit "dual mentality" framing is a distinct teaching device)
- Passing anecdote: James Dodds encodes personal mantras/goals into rotating computer passwords as a repeated-exposure behavior-change technique (elaborated again in Episode #301). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — small, concrete, novel tactic)

## Episode #301: My Running Insecurities
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode, Chris McClung, framed explicitly as a personal/vulnerable account prompted by a listener email (Kerianne), following up on Episode #300.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Names and dissects four escalating categories of athlete insecurity: (1) insecurity of injury, (2) insecurity of uncertainty (both short-term pre-race and long-term "will this plan work"), (3) insecurity of ability (including age-related self-doubt), (4) insecurity of identity ("do I even want this anymore? Am I wasting my time?"). Presented as a reusable taxonomy for locating which specific insecurity an athlete is facing rather than treating all self-doubt as one undifferentiated feeling. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this structured four-category taxonomy is a distinct mental-performance framework beyond generic "sports psychology"/mental toughness content)
- Injury-insecurity coping: describes a "doom spiral" pattern (minor niggle → catastrophizing to worst-case, e.g., meniscus tear/surgery) as common and normal. Coping approach: (a) maintain a standing roster of trusted practitioners (PT, chiropractor, massage therapist, orthopedist) to get an actual diagnosis quickly rather than ruminating; (b) deliberately reframe the internal narrative from "worst case" to "probably small and fixable." Concrete case: a knee scare was patellar tracking dysfunction from an underactive medial hamstring, resolved in about a week via manual therapy plus targeted activation exercises. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "doom spiral" pattern plus concrete reframe is a specific, actionable mental-performance technique)
- Pre-race uncertainty management: you don't need to know how you'll feel late in a race — only (a) a plan you believe you can execute for the *first* portion, and (b) a pre-assembled "mental toolkit" (mantras, counting to 60 repeatedly, "going fishing"/chasing competitors) to deploy when things get hard, without needing to predict which tool will be needed when. Concrete mantra example: "every second counts," used during a fast finish. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race mental-toolkit/mantra content is fairly standard sports-psych territory, though "you only need to believe in the first part of the plan" is a distinctive framing)
- Long-term ability-doubt coping (including age-related doubt): (1) lean on process/plan rather than outcome — zoom into "what do I need to do today"; (2) audit personal historical evidence (races/workouts already pointing to capability) rather than dwelling on evidence of failure; (3) draw on social proof from peers/training partners who've achieved the same goal ("if they can do it, I can too"). On age specifically: explicitly recommends refusing to engage with age-based doubt as unproductive, citing personally known older runners who beat their younger selves. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the assumption that aging inevitably caps performance) (POSSIBLE GAP — a structured, reusable self-talk/evidence-audit technique for long-term goal doubt)
- Identity/purpose insecurity coping: (1) write down and revisit a crystallized personal "why"; (2) seek external affirmation by telling trusted people (coach, partner, friends) so they can validate the goal's worth; (3) create tactical, repeated-exposure reminders — cites James Dodds's password-mantra habit again. Candidly admits he has not yet done this "affirmation-writing" work himself despite recommending it to athletes. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete, low-effort behavior-change device)
- Explicitly distinguishes "why you run" (stress relief, community, longevity — achievable via much less rigorous activity) from "why you want this specific big goal" (typically proving something to yourself, intrinsically driven) — argues athletes often conflate these two related but different questions. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a clean conceptual distinction a general sports-psychology section might not currently draw explicitly)
- Personal specificity: PR 2:45:36 (Houston 2018), longtime goal sub-2:40; describes deliberately rebuilding his stride, mobility, and strength base in 2022 as a multi-year rebuild rather than chasing incremental gains — continuity with Episodes #300 and #304.

## Episode #304: Build Your Running Intuition
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode, Chris McClung, presenting a six-category framework for developing training "intuition."

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central thesis: training decisions (pace, workout modification, injury calls) ultimately require developed personal judgment, not just rule-following; frames this with a repeated Star Wars/"use the Force" analogy and the encouragement that "no single decision matters in the long term" provided mistakes are used as learning data over time. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — this meta-level framing about developing judgment vs. rigid rule-following is a distinct pedagogical angle, not just a training-content topic)
- Over-reliance on GPS watches is framed as eroding an athlete's ability to self-regulate by feel (compared to losing native navigation sense from dependence on phone maps); recommends periodically running — including some quality workouts and races — with the watch covered, calibrating against the data only afterward. If using a watch, use lap/average pace (not noisy instantaneous pace) and let it accumulate enough distance before checking. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP for the deliberate "watch-free practice" framing; LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for effort-vs-pace generally)
- "Progression principle," attributed to a named coach mentor (John Shrupp): always start a rep, run, or race slightly slower than target and gradually work down to pace, rather than starting fast and correcting backward — overcorrecting from too-fast tends to overshoot and "yo-yo," while working up from slow is a controlled, one-directional adjustment. Applied to easy runs too (start "painfully easy," let pace drift naturally faster as the body warms). (PRACTICE, attributed to a named coach) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, nameable pacing technique with clear practical value, distinct from standard negative-split framing)
- Workout/illness decision rules: hard stop is fever (never run with one); softer signal is resting heart rate elevated ~5+ bpm above personal baseline as a cue to skip or scale back; general bias toward conservative choices when recovering from illness or unresolved fatigue. Mentions HRV tracking (Whoop, Oura, HRV4Training) as an additional readiness signal. (SCIENCE-adjacent rule of thumb, no cited study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — resting HR/HRV readiness monitoring is fairly well-known in endurance circles, though depth may vary on the existing site)
- Workout execution rule: an athlete should finish a quality workout feeling like they "could have done one more rep" (targeted ~99%+ of the time); if form starts breaking down or a rep can't be held on pace, that's the signal to stop, since compromised-form reps risk injury and reinforce bad movement patterns. Workouts are deliberately written as ranges (e.g., "6-8x800m") so an athlete can succeed at the lower end on an off day. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overlaps with general "don't overdo workouts" guidance, though the specific framing and deliberately-ranged prescriptions add a usable coaching device)
- Recovery-run pacing should be dictated entirely by how the body feels that day (potentially "glacially slow," including walking on hills), judged only by "did I feel better at the end than the start" — not by hitting any pace target. Quotes a fellow coach ("Coach Marilyn/Maryland" — name garbled in transcript): "motion is lotion" for recovery-day movement. (ANALOGY/PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — standard easy/recovery-day philosophy, though "motion is lotion" is a nice quotable phrase)
- Body-signal literacy beyond pace: describes reading cravings as physiological signals (e.g., a banana/potassium craving coincided with a measured potassium deficiency during a summer of heavy electrolyte use; sweets craving as a signal of carb need) and learning to embrace napping without guilt when the body signals fatigue. (PRACTICE, personal anecdote, not warranted as general science) (POSSIBLE GAP — craving-as-physiological-signal is an interesting, likely-uncommon angle, though it borders on folk-physiology and should be flagged as anecdotal if used)
- Injury intuition: distinguishes ordinary soreness (fades as a run progresses, doesn't worsen) from a developing injury (persists/worsens) through years of personal pattern-recognition (his own recurring trouble spots: left ankle mobility/calf niggles, right hip). Rule of thumb: pain ≤3/10 that doesn't worsen during a run is generally safe to run through (ideally alongside proactive rehab); soft-tissue injuries generally do not resolve with complete rest alone since blood flow from movement promotes healing — root cause must be actively addressed. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general injury/pain-scale guidance is probably already covered, though the specific numeric threshold and "must be improving, not just present" nuance is fairly concrete)
- Program-structure intuition: reiterates the ~20%-per-cycle mileage-increase guideline, and stresses proactively rescheduling around known disruptions with the rule "always follow a hard/long day with an active recovery day, never stack them back-to-back." (PRACTICE)
- Race-execution intuition-building: recommends racing more often during lower-stakes/speed-focused seasons (not just at 2-3 A-races), using structured "practice races" run as progression efforts (e.g., a half marathon at marathon pace+20-30sec for miles 1-3, marathon pace for miles 4-8, half-marathon pace for miles 9-11, then all-out for the final 2) to train both the physical and mental skill of a strong finish; also recommends occasionally racing without a watch and racing "against" familiar competitors as pacing carrots instead of the clock. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific structured progression-pacing "practice race" template is a concrete, reusable race-strategy tool beyond generic race-strategy advice)

## Episode #305: 8 Things to STOP Doing
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode, Chris McClung. **Concepts & claims:**
- RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport) flagged as an important topic: chronic underfueling relative to training load can cause fatigue, lost menstrual periods, low libido, hair loss, impaired thermoregulation, and hormonal imbalance; can affect men or women and can occur inadvertently even when an athlete believes they're eating enough. (SCIENCE, named condition/named source, though the episode itself doesn't explain the mechanism in depth — just points to Muir's series) (POSSIBLE GAP — if the site's existing fueling content doesn't already cover RED-S/energy availability specifically by name, this is a well-established sports-medicine topic worth adding)

Recommends easy pace ~90 sec/mile slower than half-marathon pace or ~60 sec/mile slower than marathon pace, extending to several minutes slower on the conservative end; or heart-rate zones 1-2 / below 180-minus-age.
