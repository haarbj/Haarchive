## Episode #89: What Does the Race Require? (Part 3)
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris (Rogue Running coach) and Steve (Rogue Running program director, former University of Texas collegiate coach) in dialogue.

**Concepts & claims:**
- 10K training requires an extended-focus/concentration skill distinct from the 5K: workouts use longer reps (3K, 2x2K, 3x1K; 3x2 mile; "Texas 10K" ladder of 8x400/2x800/1mile/2x800/8x400 with short jogs, credited to Stan Huntsman at UT) specifically to build the ability to stay mentally locked in for 30–80+ minutes. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "training focus/concentration duration" framing as a distinct trainable skill per race distance is a specific angle beyond generic interval training.)
- For 10K-specific work, recovery interval length matters less than in 5K/half training — 2–5 min recoveries are fine because the goal is time-at-pace accumulation, not strict pace-matching. (PRACTICE)
- Rule of thumb: 10K quality sessions should total roughly 5 miles to 10K worth of "quality work" per session. (PRACTICE)
- Only 3–4 sessions per training cycle need to be at literal 10K race pace; the rest of the work should be faster (5K/3K) or slower (tempo/threshold), because 10K pace itself is highly variable by course/terrain, especially on the roads. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the idea that a race's own goal pace need not be heavily rehearsed, versus adjacent paces, cuts against a common assumption.)
- Long runs need embedded quality (not just easy mileage) once athletes progress past 5K to 10K/half/marathon distances — "closes" (progressively accelerating the final 20–30 min of a long run down toward 10K pace), 1-min-on/1-min-off fartlek blocks, and continuous "3-2-1" fartlek (3 min at half pace, 2 min at 10K pace, 1 min at 5K pace, repeated with recovery) are named long-run-with-quality formats. (PRACTICE)
- Progression runs for half-marathon prep: start ~30 sec slower than marathon goal pace, negative-split down in ~10 sec/mile increments over 6–10 miles, explicitly to teach negative splitting and combat the common mistake of going out too fast in a half. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — pacing/negative splits is standard, but the specific progression-run structure is a concrete, reusable workout template.)
- Named workout: "McClung Special" — a marathon (and adapted half-marathon) long run with a start simulation, a mid-run hard fartlek to fatigue the legs, and a finish simulation, designed to simulate race start/middle/end psychologically and physically within one long run. (PRACTICE)
- Prep-race philosophy differs by distance: under-distance tune-up races (5K before a 10K) are recommended over-distance races are not very valuable for a 10K; for the half, racing down a distance is "good but not necessary" — key long-run workouts (e.g., 2x20 min at half pace) can substitute for a tune-up race. (PRACTICE)
- Core philosophy reiterated: training for shorter distances (5K/10K/half) in dedicated blocks, even during an "off" period from marathon-specific training, ultimately improves marathon performance more than doing marathon cycle after marathon cycle back-to-back, which can create a training plateau. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back against athletes' instinct that any non-marathon-focused block is "wasted time" for a marathon goal.)
- Explicit claim: you generally cannot optimize training simultaneously for a 5K PR and a marathon PR in the same cycle at a "command performance" level — pick one A-race per cycle — though marathon training often yields incidental 5K/10K PRs along the way (not vice versa, and not to the athlete's full 5K potential). (PRACTICE)

## Episode #9: A Chat with Scotty Mac
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Scott "Scotty Mac" MacPherson — professional marathoner (2:14 marathon PR at CIM), 2x NCAA All-American in cross country at University of Arkansas under legendary coach John McDonough, 4x Texas high school state champion, 2x US Olympic Trials Marathon competitor (2012, 2016), formerly with Rogue Athletic Club.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Visualization as a developmental tool: MacPherson describes visualizing racing and beating specific named rivals during solo cross-training (elliptical work while injured) in high school, and believes this contributed directly to a jump in performance when he returned from injury. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — visualization is standard sports-psych content, but the specific "visualize beating a named rival during unrelated cross-training" mechanism is a vivid, usable anecdote.)
- Coach John McDonough's core teaching: "build a callus to discomfort" — training and racing pain is something you build tolerance to deliberately, not something to avoid. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- Coaches with elite tactical "eye" can predict race outcomes and finishing times with startling precision by reading form/mechanics breakdown in real time (anecdote: McDonough predicted MacPherson's race time within 3 seconds, and called race winners from 600m out by watching who was breaking down). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the idea of training a coach's/athlete's own eye to read opponent form-breakdown as a tactical skill is rarely covered explicitly.)
- Jumps coach anecdote/teaching point: sometimes the coach's job is just to get an athlete out of their own head ("your problem is you're not jumping far enough") rather than give technical correction. (ANALOGY)
- "Shit sandwich" framing for repeated disappointing high-stakes races (two Olympic Trials no-shows/underperformances due to illness and poor prep): the emotional processing point is that big goals often require absorbing multiple serious disappointments before any payoff, and that finishing a marathon distance at all, or getting incremental PRs along the way, are valid "wins" independent of the ultimate goal (Olympic team). (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the idea that a "successful running career" requires marathon major medals/Olympic berths.)
- Training philosophy shift: after years of varied approaches, MacPherson attributes his marathon PR breakthrough specifically to a 10-week block of very high mileage (120–140 mi/week, partly at altitude in Flagstaff) with minimal structured workouts — "mileage, mileage, mileage" — over any specific workout scheme. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic volume emphasis is standard, but the specific claim that near-total volume-only training outperformed his more workout-heavy approaches is a notable individual data point.)
- Solo training vs. team training tradeoff: solo marathon training built the mental resilience needed for race day (since the marathon is "just you and your head" regardless of competitors), but athletes get more out of quality/speed work when training with a group who can push pace; ideal is a blend. (PRACTICE)
- Coach McDonough quote used as closing philosophy: "everybody on the starting line wants to win the race, but the winner has to win the race" — winning your own race means achieving your personal goal (a PR, a Boston qualifier), not necessarily finishing first. (ANALOGY)
- Side note (banter, not core content): extended discussion on reformatting track meets (Nitro Athletics team-format experiment) — hosts and guest generally skeptical of gimmicky formats, prefer shortening meets, adding team/stakes elements, and better broadcast storytelling. Minimal generalizable coaching content; flagged mainly as color.

## Episode #91: What Does the Race Require? (Part 4, Marathon Edition)
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris and Steve.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central claim, attributed to years of coaching "experimentation" on adult marathoners at Rogue: doing enough 20-plus-mile long runs (they now prescribe 5–8 per marathon cycle, up to 7 for veterans) is "the single greatest indicator of race success," specifically because it prevents the "heavy legs, can't close" feeling in the final 10K — the legs need to have been somewhere close to marathon distance/time repeatedly before race day. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — long runs are standard marathon content, but the specific claim of needing 5–8 twenty-plus-mile runs, versus the more common 2–3, is a stronger/more specific position worth flagging for fact-check.)
- Recommend lengthening marathon training blocks to 22–24 weeks (vs. typical 14–18 week plans) specifically to allow enough time to build to 20-mile long runs earlier and more sustainably. (PRACTICE)
- Concrete caps by athlete population: ~24 miles is the practical ceiling for long runs for most athletes (occasionally a full 26.2 mile "hard run" at ~15% slower than race pace for groups that want it); 5+ hour marathoners should use a time cap rather than distance cap on long runs; beginners should not exceed 20 miles. (PRACTICE)
- Easy long run pace guideline: about 1 minute per mile slower than marathon goal pace, to stay in the correct aerobic zone and minimize neuromuscular/injury stress. (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Named long-run-with-quality workouts: 20-mile progressive pace run (4 easy, 8 at MGP+15sec, 6 at MGP, close final 2 at 10sec faster than MGP or better) done early-to-mid cycle; the "Tiger Run" (adapted from Bill Squires) — 8 sets of 90 sec at 10K pace + 5 min at marathon pace, bookended by easy miles, on an 18- or 20-mile run — is described as being "like the Canova workout" and puts "the tiger in the cat" (Squires' phrase) by having athletes recover at marathon pace rather than at an easy jog. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "Tiger Run" structure and the psychological framing of using marathon pace itself as the "recovery" interval is a distinctive workout design worth capturing.)
- "No-nutrition runs": deliberately fasted long runs (semi-fasted state, no sugar even in coffee, last meal before 8pm the prior night) done 2–3 times in a cycle at 18–24 miles, explicitly to habituate the body and mind to the metabolic shift from carbohydrate to fat burning, which the hosts equate with "the wall" at ~20 miles — the point isn't primarily to improve fat-burning capacity but to psychologically prepare athletes for the sensation of bonking so it isn't a shock on race day. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — physiological claim about substrate shift is asserted, not sourced to a specific study) (POSSIBLE GAP — deliberately training through/rehearsing "the bonk" as a mental-preparation tool, rather than only trying to prevent it nutritionally, is a distinct angle.)
- Explicit position: strength training is a "must" for marathoners specifically (more than for 10K/half), even if just basic bodyweight work (lunges, planks) 2–3x/week — consistency of a simple routine beats an elaborate inconsistent one. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Mental/physical training should be "dovetailed" rather than trained separately — the hosts argue most mental-training content (visualization, self-talk) is taught divorced from the physical suffering context, and that the real value of hard long-run/quality sessions is rehearsing the mental and physical demons simultaneously so the race-day response becomes a "subconscious, visceral reaction" rather than something worked out in the moment. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a sharper and more specific claim than generic "practice visualization" advice; worth flagging since a site covering sports psychology may present mental training as a separate module rather than integrated into physical suffering.)
- Programming principle: intentionally sequence hard sessions on both sides of a rest window (e.g., quality Thursday before a long-run-with-quality Saturday) so athletes are tired going into and/or coming out of key sessions, treating a 48–72 hour window as "two pieces of the same workout." (PRACTICE)
- Weakness-first, strength-last periodization: work 5K/10K-zone weaknesses early in a marathon cycle, shift to half/marathon-pace strengths late in the cycle. (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general periodization, but stated as a specific rule of thumb.)

## Episode #94: Training for the Trail
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Jason Brooks — Rogue Running trail coach, race director for Spectrum Trail Races, experienced ultrarunner (Bandera 100K); plus hosts Chris and Steve (Steve self-identifies as having trail-run since childhood).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Trail running reframed as "the best form of cross-training there is" for road runners — low impact, works all three planes of motion (sagittal/transverse/coronal), forces glute/hip-flexor engagement and attention, but will make a road runner measurably slower if done exclusively; specificity (some road/track work) is needed to maintain road speed. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — trail running as cross-training modality, and the road-vs-trail specificity debate, is likely a genuinely underrepresented topic on a primarily road/distance-focused site.)
- Training principle translation #1 — "miles matter" becomes "minutes/time matters" on trail: prescribe volume in duration, not distance, because terrain variability makes mile-based comparisons meaningless; rough rule of thumb given: an advanced trail athlete's ~75 mi/week road-equivalent volume translates to ~15 hours/week on trail, and a road runner doing 40–50 mi/week would target roughly 10–12 hours/week on trail (not a simple linear time conversion — higher-volume runners get "more work done in less time"). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Back-to-back ("doubling") long runs on consecutive days become important as ultra distance increases (becomes essential around 50 miles and up), both for practical/logistical reasons (can't always support an 8-hour outing) and to simulate running on already-fatigued legs — e.g., two 3-hour long runs Saturday/Sunday rather than one massive outing. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Claim: "a well-run marathon is harder than a well-run 50K" — there isn't the same physiological "wall"/bonk experience on the trail unless nutrition is mismanaged, because trail pace is so much slower; going from 50K to 50-mile training is not simply "double the volume" — athletes hit diminishing returns or life/recovery limits well before linear scaling would suggest. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — asserted, not study-cited) (DISAGREEMENT — directly contradicts an intuitive assumption that ultra distance scaling requires proportionally scaled training volume.)
- Training principle translation #2 — pace-based/RBF training becomes effort-based (RPE) training on trail, because terrain (grade, technicality) makes pace meaningless; cites Jason Koop's "Training Essentials for Ultrarunning" for a 10-point RPE framework (roughly: 4=recovery, 5–6=endurance, 7=steady/near-MGP, 8–9=tempo, 10=VO2max) taught via breathing/conversational cues (can talk freely at 5–6, one-two sentences at tempo, only a word or two at RPE 10). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — references a named book/author) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC in the sense of RPE-based training generally, but POSSIBLE GAP for trail-grade-specific effort moderation.)
- Explicit coaching rule: on climbs of ~7–8%+ grade, athletes must walk ("power hiking") to keep effort in the prescribed zone rather than let heart rate/effort spike — walking uphill is often the same speed as a poorly-paced run up the same grade but far less costly; road runners transitioning to trail routinely blow this up (over-run climbs) and get "eaten for lunch" on descents by trail-savvy runners who paced the climb correctly. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Never-DNF ethos specific to trail/ultra culture: once you register for a trail ultra, DNFing (short of medical emergency) is described as disrespecting the field — a fundamentally different race-culture norm than road racing, where stepping off is more socially acceptable. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a genuine culture/psychology difference between road and trail racing communities.)
- Trail-specific periodization differs from road: early cycle emphasizes VO2max/high-end aerobic work (many trail runners, lacking a structured running background, default to just "running a lot" rather than working all systems) and even short, fast technical/hill repeats; middle cycle shifts to threshold/tempo; final phase (unique to trail/ultra) emphasizes pure volume and race-specific terrain/technicality/elevation replication, including altitude training camps if the goal race is mountainous. (PRACTICE)
- Proprioception (defined here as the eyes'/nervous system's ability to read terrain without consciously processing every obstacle) is framed as a trainable skill with two components: (1) a physical/neurological skill gained through repeated exposure (can take 3–6 weeks for high-aptitude people, much longer for others), and (2) a psychological fear-management component that's often the larger bottleneck — the "spinning coin" analogy: moving faster/committing more through technical terrain is often easier and safer than moving cautiously, because momentum provides stability the way a spinning coin stays upright. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — proprioception as an explicitly trainable, teachable skill with concrete drills is likely missing from a road-focused site.)
- Concrete technical-terrain coaching cues: look ~10 feet ahead (not at your feet) and let peripheral vision/reflexes handle immediate foot placement; raise arms/elbows out for upper-body balance on technical or downhill terrain (contrasts with the tight-arm-carriage road running form); choose "lines" that are the path of least resistance/energy expenditure, not necessarily the shortest or fastest route, and prefer more, smaller steps over big steps up ledges to protect hip flexors, which fatigue quickly under repeated large knee-lift demands. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)

## Episode #96: How to Assess Your Last Race, Good or Bad
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris and Steve.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Structured post-race protocol: take 24–72 hours post-race to either celebrate or fully feel negative emotions before analyzing anything — write a private race report within that window (splits, how you felt at each point, nutrition, mental state) without drawing conclusions yet, since "your first thought is your best thought" as raw data but conclusions should wait; conclusions/lessons should be revisited and can evolve over weeks-to-months ("write it in pencil, not pen"). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a fairly complete, structured post-race debrief protocol with explicit timing windows is a concrete practical framework a site might not have.)
- Explicitly separate "training results" from "racing results" when debriefing — a good training cycle can still produce a bad race result (e.g., due to GI/dehydration issues), and the two should be assessed independently so athletes don't discard good training lessons because of a single bad-execution race. (PRACTICE)
- Categories for race-result debrief: pacing/plan execution, nutrition (before/during/after), mental game, and honesty about actual fitness level going in (was the goal itself too aggressive relative to true fitness). (PRACTICE)
- Named cognitive bias claim (analogized from a Gracie brothers/BJJ interview about fighters): almost everyone overestimates their near-term fitness/readiness (compared to objective results) but underestimates their long-term capacity for improvement over multiple cycles — training breakthroughs typically take 2–4 full training cycles to manifest, especially in the marathon (rarely a single-cycle "big pop" outside 5K/10K). (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — analogized from an outside interview, not a running-specific study) (ANALOGY)
- Explicit warning against making multiple simultaneous training changes after a race (good or bad) — change one variable at a time over 3–6+ weeks so cause and effect can actually be identified; specifically warns against raising weekly mileage as a fix for a bad race unless there was clear evidence mileage itself was the limiter (mileage increases are described as "the most surefire way to get injured/overtrained"). (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back against a common athlete instinct to overhaul everything after a disappointing result.)
- After a strong result, deliberately resist over-excitement/immediately escalating training — take a full break (family time, non-running life) to avoid burnout; after a poor result, it's fine to race again soon but not at the marathon distance — recommend building back to a shorter race (10-miler/half) after 2–3 weeks of easy running rather than jumping straight into another marathon. (PRACTICE)
- On managing well-meaning but unhelpful encouragement from others after a missed goal ("your time is still great!") — normalize ignoring/deferring those messages for the 24–72 hour processing window rather than feeling guilty about not being cheered up by them. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific social/emotional-management tactic around outside encouragement after a missed goal.)
- Reiterates a recurring show theme: athletes must find their primary motivation in the process/journey rather than purely in outcomes, or they won't sustain the sport long-term; ties this to revisiting one's "statement of purpose" after every race (good or bad) and to having a "foundation" or sense of identity independent of running results. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — process-orientation and purpose-setting overlaps with general sports psychology content already likely covered.)
- Anecdote: coaches often experience post-race disappointment more acutely than athletes because they feel accountable for outcomes they didn't directly control minute-to-minute. (PRACTICE — coaching-culture observation, minor.)

## Episode #97: The Maffetone Method
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Mallory Brooks — Rogue Running trail coach and race director (Spectrum Trail Races), personally self-experimenting with the Maffetone Method; hosts Chris and Steve provide critique/context. Background given on Phil Maffetone: doctor of chiropractic, has coached elite endurance athletes including 6-time Hawaii Ironman champion Mark Allen; author of "The Big Book of Endurance Training and Racing," "The Maffetone Method," and "Training for Endurance."

**Concepts & claims:**
- MAF (Maximum Aerobic Function) Method core protocol: train exclusively at or below a heart-rate ceiling calculated as 180 minus age (with individual adjustments, e.g., +5 for injury-free training history), for an extended base period (Mallory's protocol: 15 weeks, ramping from 6–7 hrs/week to 9–10 hrs/week), with no other cross-training or strength work that would raise heart rate above the ceiling. (SCIENCE — named methodology/named practitioner, though the underlying physiological rationale is asserted by Maffetone rather than cited to independent studies in this discussion) (POSSIBLE GAP — heart-rate-capped/MAF-style training is a genuinely distinct methodology from the site's likely default effort/pace-based framework, worth flagging as a possible topic to cover or contrast.)
- MAF test: run 3 miles holding heart rate at the target ceiling, record per-mile pace, repeat every 4–5 weeks — expect paces to improve (get faster at the same heart rate) as aerobic base develops; described real example of 9:03/9:15/9:34 improving to 8:18/8:54/8:57 over six weeks (~6% improvement, not fully controlled for weather). (SCIENCE — self-reported single-subject data, not a controlled study — flag as anecdotal.)
- Warm-up/cool-down should be gradual, hitting every heart-rate increment between resting HR and target zone rather than jumping straight to target — Maffetone's stated rationale is better sustainability of the target effort and better recovery; the coaches note this also applies practically (jumping straight to target pace makes it harder to hold the ceiling once effort spikes, e.g., on a hill). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — Maffetone's specific claim, secondhand) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "no skipped zones" warm-up/cooldown rule is a concrete, checkable practice.)
- Notable side effect/experience reported: significant pace slowdown during the protocol (roughly 60–90 sec/mile slower than normal easy pace) plus felt mechanical changes (heavier stride, reduced hip range of motion) and ego/social discomfort (running slower than onlookers expect) — described as an expected and often difficult psychological adjustment. (PRACTICE)
- Host Steve's critique (explicit disagreement while still endorsing the base-building premise): the MAF method's strict no-speed-work, no-hills-run (walk only), no-strides rule is an oversimplification that will require an unnecessarily rough "transition period" back to normal training; he'd add short strides (barefoot, walk-back recovery) and hill running even at the cost of brief heart-rate spikes, arguing these don't meaningfully undermine aerobic development but preserve neuromuscular recruitment patterns that pure MAF training neglects. (DISAGREEMENT — explicit pushback from the hosts against the guest topic's core protocol, framed as "this is genius but incomplete.")
- Nutrition component: Maffetone-associated approach (implemented via ultrarunner Zach Bitter's guidance) uses a periodized carbohydrate percentage — roughly 10% of calories from carbs in base phase, 15–20% in build phase, 25–30% in race-week phase — with ~60% of calories from fat, aiming for fat-adaptation to reduce reliance on carried race nutrition. Combined with intermittent fasting (running fasted, ~8-hour eating window) as an on-ramp technique before considering full ketosis. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — attributed to a named ultrarunner/practitioner, not an independent study; presented as experimental self-testing, not settled science) (POSSIBLE GAP — periodized carbohydrate-percentage-by-training-phase as a concrete framework is a specific, actionable structure a general "fueling" section may not include.)
- Host Steve's counterpoint on fueling: he personally runs long runs on water only (no gels/electrolytes) and believes many recreational runners over-fuel relative to effort/duration, citing an anecdote of runners gaining weight over a 20-mile group run from excess Gatorade/gel intake — but explicitly caveats that marathon/ultra race day still requires practiced in-race fueling, and that this fasted-training approach is about training adaptation, not a race-day nutrition plan. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — mild tension with the general "practice your race nutrition" advice; nuance is it's about training-run fueling, not race-day fueling.)
- Explicit caveat repeated twice: MAF-style strict heart-rate-capped training is incompatible with racing during the protocol window — you cannot do the program and race simultaneously, which is framed as one of the method's key benefits (forces genuine commitment to a dedicated base phase) as well as its key limitation (impractical for athletes racing frequently). (PRACTICE)
- Garden-soil analogy for base building: "working on the soil" (aerobic base) isn't sexy but must be right before you can "sow seeds" (speedwork) and expect a harvest (race performance). (ANALOGY)
