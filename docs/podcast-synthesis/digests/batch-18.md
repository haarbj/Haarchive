## Episode #276: 10 Tips for a New Season
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Host Chris McClung, owner/coach at Rogue Running (Austin) and coach of the "Rogue Renegades" podcast-based training group; running/coaching since 1999.

**Concepts & claims:**
- "Book/chapter" analogy (credited to fellow coach James Dodds): each training cycle is a chapter with a beginning, middle, and end; you must consciously close one chapter (reflect on lessons + wins) before opening the next. (ANALOGY) (PRACTICE)
- Post-race lesson extraction process: first "feel the feels" (grieve/celebrate) for up to ~1-2 weeks, THEN switch to logical lesson-extraction; pick only 1-2 priority lessons, don't over-drill into 3rd/4th-order explanations, and don't invent lessons where the real cause was uncontrollable (e.g., hot-weather race day). Uses his own 2:47 Martian Marathon on a 70°F day as an example of nearly over-correcting training after a weather-driven underperformance. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology, but the specific "don't overthink/don't invent lessons" framing is a useful nuance)
- Distinguishes "training results" from "racing results" in a season — a cycle can be a training win (more mileage, better consistency, faster workout paces, better in-run fueling) even if the race outcome itself disappointed; training gains are additive/foundational across cycles even when race day doesn't cooperate. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — framing not typically covered as its own concept)
- "Purpose → Goals → Process" framework: reconnect to your why (purpose) at the start of every new cycle, connect that to a specific goal, then to the concrete training process; recommends explicitly re-validating your "why" even after a big breakthrough (e.g., a first BQ), since losing the purpose-to-goal connection commonly causes post-PR motivation collapse. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sports psychology/goal-setting, though the explicit 3-part chain is a specific model)
- "You have to detrain in order to retrain" — fitness is not linear; after a peak, the body must come down to a trough before building to a new, higher peak via a periodized sequence (priming → aerobic strength → race-specific → taper); trying to skip this and stay near peak fitness leads to injury. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization, but "detrain to retrain" is a distinct, quotable framing worth flagging)
- Priming/base phase specifics: rebuild volume and consistency plus short, fast neuromuscular-priming work (strides, 30-second to 1-minute pickups, short hill repeats) done by effort, not pace; "effort is king," don't chase paces this early. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — base building)
- Easy-day pace discipline: recommends keeping heart rate under ~140 bpm as a general rule of thumb on easy days so you stay in the aerobic-building zone without excess musculoskeletal stress; states egos/Strava are the main reason distance runners struggle with running truly easy. (SCIENCE — presented as a rule of thumb, no study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base/easy running)
- Heat compensation for training: expect easy-run (and most) paces to slow 20-30+ seconds/mile in summer heat, that heat acclimation takes roughly 4-6 weeks, and that runners give themselves "grace" for altitude-driven slowdowns but not for heat — a notable double standard he calls out explicitly. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — implicit critique of runners' asymmetric acceptance of altitude vs. heat effects) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — heat training, general)
- Daily (not just in-run) hydration/electrolyte routine for summer: recommends 2x/day electrolyte servings (e.g., salt pills or products like "Light Switch," Scratch, Element, Nuun) plus using post-run urination frequency/color as a hydration recovery check. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — fueling/hydration)
- Recommends proactively planning training-cycle adjustments (down weeks, long-run placement) around known summer disruptions like vacations/school schedules, rather than reacting last-minute. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — logistics/schedule-planning as its own coaching topic, distinct from physiology)
- Closing message via a Desiree Linden quote/paraphrase: "Just keep showing up" — consistency (targeting ~90-95% adherence over a training block) matters more than any single workout or long run. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — consistency/adherence)

## Episode #277: Running for "Late Bloomers"
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Host Chris McClung answering a listener's (Roey, Israel, started running at 40) submitted questions, drawing on his coaching experience with many "late bloomer" athletes.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central physiological claim: "aerobic development age" starts at zero at whatever age you begin consistent running, regardless of chronological age, and continues to mature/improve for roughly two decades of consistent training. Cites the underlying adaptations as improved lung capacity, oxygen-carrying capacity, added mitochondria, and new capillaries in working muscle. Implication: someone starting at 40-65 can still see meaningful aerobic gains into their 50s-60s+. (SCIENCE — general exercise-physiology claim, no specific study/researcher named) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "two-decade aerobic development clock resets to zero at start age" framing, specifically applied to late-starting/older runners, doesn't map cleanly onto standard "aerobic base" content)
- Claim that runners who start later in life tend to have more durable, self-sustained (intrinsic) motivation than those who started young for external reasons (talent, parental pressure, team obligation), and that the latter group is more likely to quit once the external driver disappears (e.g., not going pro after college). (PRACTICE, presented with anecdotal backing) (POSSIBLE GAP — motivational-durability angle specific to late starters)
- Brendan Burchard (performance coach) quote used as a behavior-change model: "First it is an intention, then a behavior, then a habit, then a practice, then a second nature, then it is simply who you are." McClung shares his own ~8-9 year personal timeline through these stages. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — habit formation/behavior change, general)
- Goal-setting guidance for beginners: no required distance sequence (5K→10K→half→marathon not mandatory) — pick whatever distance is most personally motivating; avoid picking a specific goal TIME for a first attempt at a new distance, instead aim to (1) finish and (2) finish with a negative split for a positive first experience. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race-strategy/negative-splitting general concept)
- Negative splitting is stated as the "optimal strategy" for any race beyond a 5K "at least data and science tell us" — asserted as fact but no specific study/researcher cited in this episode. (SCIENCE — claim asserted, not sourced) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — pacing strategy)
- PR-potential assessment method for newer/late-starting runners: don't set a target number early; instead run consistently for 8-12 weeks, then do a 2-mile (or 3,200m) time trial and plug the result into McMillan's running calculator to estimate potential at other distances — with the caveat that a coach's judgment matters more than the raw number. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific actionable protocol worth cross-checking against existing content on training-potential assessment)
- Explicitly warns that improvement is NOT linear/guaranteed each cycle even though many new runners experience a multi-year "PR everything" honeymoon window; frames the setbacks/injuries/bad-weather races as still valuable via "training results" carried forward. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training plateaus, general)
- On running form and aging: cites that actively/consciously trying to change running form is generally unproductive (asserted "there's been science," unnamed); recommends four indirect levers instead — more running volume, weekly form drills, weekly strides, and strength/mobility work — as the way form efficiency improves without conscious technique changes. Notes older runners have less elastic muscles and more ingrained movement patterns, making bad-habit change harder (but not impossible) with age. (SCIENCE — unsourced) (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — running economy/form, general; the aging-specific caveat is a narrower angle)
- Late-bloomer-specific injury pattern warning: self-coached late starters often get hurt because they don't know to slow down on easy runs, don't know how to build volume gradually, or misinterpret post-run soreness (going out again to "run through" soreness rather than resting) — framed as a reason a coach is valuable "from day one," not just once established. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific late-starter injury-pattern framing)
- Closing message: encourages late bloomers to "dream big," naming a common belief that late starters feel unworthy of ambitious goals/rigorous programs; explicitly frames this as internalized societal messaging to reject. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — psychological "worthiness" framing specific to adult-onset/late-starting runners, adjacent to but distinct from general sports psychology content)

## Episode #279: Q&A on Running Fundamentals
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Nicole Winter (Instagram handle "Nicole M Runs"/"nicolemwinter_"), an athlete coached by McClung (ran a 3:31 debut marathon against a 3:40 target) who interviewed him for her Instagram audience; McClung is the marathon coach/owner at Rogue Running.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Breathing advice cites the book "Running on Air" by Budd Coates by name. Recommends the "odd-even" breathing method: an odd number of steps on the inhale and an even number on the exhale (e.g., 3 steps in/2 out, or 2 in/1 out) so the same foot doesn't always land on the inhale, which helps create balance/rhythm and can help resolve side stitches caused by breath being out of sync with foot strike. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — book/author named, technique attributed to that source) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific breathing-cadence technique, not clearly "general sports physiology" already covered)
- Recommends slowing to a walk or stopping to regain breathing control when it spirals out of control mid-run (e.g., after a hill or effort spike), rather than trying to fight through it at pace. (PRACTICE)
- Cadence: pushes back on the popular "180 steps/minute" target as a one-size-fits-all number — states there IS science behind ~180 spm being efficient for an average-height person, but that taller runners naturally run lower cadence and shorter runners higher, so absolute cadence targets are less useful than relative cadence. Diagnostic rule: cadence should increase as pace increases; if cadence instead DECREASES when running faster, that signals overstriding, which is both less efficient and injury-risk-raising. Explicitly advises against using a metronome to consciously force cadence changes. (SCIENCE — general claim, no specific study named) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back against the common "180 cadence" rule) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — running economy/cadence, general, but the relative-cadence-as-overstriding-diagnostic is a sharper, specific tool)
- Running form: same "four levers" as ep. 277 — run more, form drills (high knees, butt kicks, bounding, skipping), weekly strides, and strength/mobility — framed as how the body finds efficient form without consciously thinking about form itself. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — running economy)
- Defines a "stride": 80-100m stretch, mentally split into thirds — build effort in the first third, hold ~85-90% of peak speed in the middle third, ease off (not brake) in the final third; full walk-back recovery; start with 4 reps building to 6. Calls strides "inoculations of speed" — small, low-cost doses that compound over time into better efficiency and form. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY: "inoculations of speed") (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete stride-execution protocol, worth checking if existing content has this level of specificity)
- Speed-building sequence: increase easy-running volume FIRST (this builds aerobic engine size), then layer in strides, then add dedicated speed work (about one focused speed day/week is enough for most people); race-day speed over distance emerges from combining the aerobic capacity (easy miles) with the top-end speed (small structured doses) — explicitly counterintuitive but claimed to reliably work based on 15 years of coaching. Uses Nicole's own 3:31 debut (following conservative, mostly-easy training) as a concrete example. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base + speed integration, general)
- Training duration guidance: half/marathon training ~20 weeks (4-5 wk priming, ~6 wk aerobic strength, ~6 wk race-specific, ~3 wk taper); 5K trainable in ~12 weeks, 10K in ~16 weeks depending on experience. (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/training-plan length, general)
- Recovery-between-races guidance: at least 3 weeks before restarting focused training after a marathon (light easy running is fine/encouraged during that window for blood flow), 1-2 weeks after a half, ~1 week after a 5K/10K; also recommends built-in "off-season" windows of unstructured easy running even for frequent racers. (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — post-race recovery, general)
- Off-road/trail marathon-specific advice: the two biggest variables are (1) fueling/hydration planning matched to the course's actual aid-station availability (more critical than on roads because time-on-feet is longer and stomach tolerance matters more), and (2) training on terrain that mimics race-day terrain as closely as possible (his own example: doing repeat hill hikes on Austin's biggest hills to prepare for a Whistler-area 50-miler since Austin has no mountains). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — trail/off-road-specific race prep is a different angle than typical road-race content)
- Boston Qualifier advice: build a genuine personal relationship with the goal (recommends watching the documentary "Boston," about the 2014 race) rather than chasing it purely for external validation; treat BQ pursuit as a realistic 3-5 year goal, not a 6-12 month rush; specific training tip — build to 4-6 twenty-mile runs per marathon cycle, with marathon-pace segments built into 2-3 of them. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "treat BQ as a multi-year goal, don't rush" framing is a specific, actionable mindset point)
- First marathon goal-time formula: double your half-marathon time and add 10 minutes for a theoretical marathon-pace equivalent; for a first marathon specifically, add another 5-10 minutes of buffer on top so the runner finishes strong/"hungry for more" rather than depleted. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific race-time-prediction heuristic distinct from generic race calculators)
- "Feeling sluggish for 1-2+ weeks" recovery checklist (in priority order): (1) are easy runs actually easy — at least 1 min/mile slower than marathon pace on easy/long runs, ~2 min/mile slower on recovery runs; (2) sleep sufficiency; (3) post-run fueling, especially the ~90-minute post-workout window (balanced carbs/protein/fat); (4) if all else checks out, get a blood panel to rule out anemia or other underlying issues, and see a medical provider. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overtraining/fatigue diagnosis, general, though the explicit ordered checklist is a usable structure)
- Explicitly debunks the "long runs shouldn't exceed 3 hours" rule (references a live Twitter debate on the topic): no science supports 3 hours as a hard ceiling; if a runner needs 3:30-4:00+ to safely cover 20 miles at proper easy effort, that's fine — a 4-hour soft cap is reasonable but shouldn't be treated as rigid. Key reframe: race-day readiness comes from matching TIME ON FEET in training to expected marathon finish time, not from replicating the exact 26.2-mile distance in training. (DISAGREEMENT — explicit pushback on a specific popular training rule) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "time on feet, not distance, is what transfers" framing for long runs is a specific, actionable reframe worth flagging even though long-run training is a broadly covered topic)

## Episode #280: The Power of Progression
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Host Chris McClung; credits a former coach, John Shrupp (worked with him on Team Rogue in Austin), as the originator of this principle in his own training.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core principle: nearly everything in training and racing (warmups, easy runs, long runs, workouts, and races 10K or longer) should be run in progression — starting slower than you finish — with only narrow exceptions (short races under 5K, and highly dialed race-specific-phase pace work). (PRACTICE)
- Car-engine analogy for why progression conserves energy: a car burns more fuel accelerating from 0 to 40 mph than cruising at 40; similarly, the energy cost of jumping straight to race pace is higher than easing down into it, so progression is fundamentally an energy-conservation/efficiency strategy, not just a pacing preference. (ANALOGY)
- Cites Phil Maffetone's work (named) on warmups: the body should progress as gradually as possible through heart-rate zones during a warmup (e.g., 60→70→80...→140 bpm) rather than jumping straight to target-zone effort; references the "Kenyan approach" to warming up as deliberately glacially slow at the start. (SCIENCE — named source, Phil Maffetone) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific heart-rate-progression warmup protocol, narrower than general warmup advice)
- States that any race 10K or longer is optimally run as a negative split per "data and science," 8K should be roughly even-split, 5K a slight positive split, and anything shorter than 5K should be run all-out from the start since there's no "later" to conserve energy for. (SCIENCE — asserted, no specific study named) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — pacing strategy/negative splits, general)
- Workout-execution guidance: even when a coach prescribes a flat target pace for repeats, the implicit expectation is to start the first (or first two) rep(s) slightly slower than target and work down to it — "miss slow" — because starting too fast on rep 1 commonly compromises the whole workout (forced pace drop-off or dropped reps later). (PRACTICE) (quotable phrase: "miss slow")
- Names the "progression run" (gradually increasing pace by ~5-10 sec/mile per mile) as one of the hardest workouts to execute well because it requires subtle real-time effort calibration; emphasizes it's about "progression, not perfection" — inconsistent per-mile drops are fine as long as the overall trend is faster. (PRACTICE)
- "How to learn it" section: explicitly warns against using a GPS watch as a crutch for executing progression — recommends training the "feel" for pace/effort instead, doing some workouts by feel while only checking splits afterward for calibration, and stresses that correct execution requires only "subtle shifts in effort," not big surges, to drop pace incrementally. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — implicit pushback against overreliance on GPS/pace data) (POSSIBLE GAP — "learn pace by feel, not by staring at the watch" as an explicit trainable skill, plus the "progressive close" drill — finishing the last 15-20 min of a long run progressively faster in 5-min increments — are specific, actionable techniques)
- Final point: progression execution requires setting ego aside — accepting current fitness, heat, or group-pace pressure rather than stubbornly chasing paces/people that don't match today's conditions; McClung admits to personally getting "burned" recently by chasing younger training partners' paces. (PRACTICE)

- Pre-run fueling: explicitly says NOT to carb-load before long runs/workouts — just eat normal, routine, stomach-safe food (regular dinner the night before, small usual breakfast). (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back against a common carb-loading-before-long-run assumption) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, but the "don't carb-load pre-run, do it post-run instead" reframe is a specific, actionable point)
- Alcohol's effect on training readiness: describes using an Oura Ring and observing that even one glass of wine measurably affected sleep quality, resting heart rate, and HRV; personal practice is to abstain from alcohol the night before quality workouts/long runs (not full abstinence otherwise). - Post-run fueling: emphasizes a critical 60-90 minute post-run window for a "filling, balanced" meal (carbs+fats+protein) to rebuild glycogen and repair tissue, and states glycogen stores can actually be replenished MORE effectively immediately post-run than pre-run — reframing "carb-loading" as something that should start right after the previous hard effort, not right before the next one. (SCIENCE — claim asserted without a named source) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific underfueling→overtraining/injury causal claim, and the "next long run's carb-loading starts today" reframe, are sharper than generic "eat after your run" advice)
- Quantifies personal sweat/hydration targets: ~2 lbs (32 oz) fluid loss/hour is "about average," recommends replacing roughly 75-80% of sweat losses during runs (not 100%, since some deficit doesn't hurt performance), and uses post-run urination frequency/color as a practical hydration-recovery indicator. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — hydration, general; overlaps directly with ep. 283's more detailed sweat-test protocol)
- Sleep: recommends napping after long runs specifically to "kickstart recovery," and practical logistics tips (laying out gear/keys/water the night before) to protect extra sleep time before early long runs. (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sleep/recovery, general)
- Movement: recommends light, easy movement (easy run, walk, foam rolling, mobility work) the day BEFORE a big effort to "prime" the body, and gentle movement AFTER a big effort to promote blood flow/healing; explicitly states skipping the active-recovery run between a hard workout and a long run (or vice versa) is one of the most common injury causes he sees as a coach. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery/active-recovery running, general)
- Mental pre-game: recommends reviewing the day's workout/pace targets in advance to avoid burning mental energy figuring it out on the day, prepping mantras/music/pacing partners for hard workouts (not just races), and using techniques like counting (mentioned again, see ep. 283) to dissociate from discomfort. Mental post-game: allow a bounded window to review splits/lessons, then deliberately let it go and de-stress (his own example: late-evening youth soccer games left his mind racing and hurt his sleep, vs. morning games which didn't) since a stressed mind impairs physical recovery. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology, though "your own pre/post-workout mental routine, not just race day" is a specific extension)

## Episode #283: Running in Heat + Positive Self-Talk
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Host Chris McClung. References a Sweat Science/Outside Magazine article by science journalist Alex Hutchinson (named, previously a guest on the show) as the basis for the self-talk discussion.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Distinguishes absolute temperature from dew point as separate heat-stress variables — explains dew point (the temperature at which air can no longer hold its water vapor) as a more precise proxy for how oppressive conditions feel than relative humidity, and states dew points above 60°F meaningfully affect even easy runs. (SCIENCE — general meteorological/exercise concept, no specific study named) (POSSIBLE GAP — dew point as a specific, actionable heat-stress metric distinct from generic "humidity" advice)
- Repeats the altitude-vs-heat double-standard point from ep. 276: runners readily accept slower paces at altitude but resist doing so in heat/humidity, despite both being legitimate physiological stressors; reframes heat/humidity training as "poor man's altitude" — a stimulus with altitude-like adaptive benefit despite forcing slower paces. (ANALOGY) (DISAGREEMENT)
- Detailed sweat-test protocol: weigh nude before and after a 1-hour run with no fluid intake (or track intake if any); each pound lost = 16 oz of sweat loss; recommends replacing ~80% of that hourly loss during long runs (shorter effort/workouts under ~10 miles can tolerate more of a deficit). Gives his own rate (2 lbs/hr = 32 oz/hr) and notes some athletes lose up to 3x that (6 lbs/hr), requiring hydration packs/bladders instead of handhelds. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete, repeatable self-testing protocol, more specific/actionable than generic "stay hydrated" advice)
- Explains electrolytes' physiological role via osmosis: without adequate electrolyte balance, ingested water can pass through the bloodstream and out through the kidneys without actually being absorbed into working cells — i.e., you can drink enough and still functionally be under-hydrated. Recommends daily (not just in-run) electrolyte supplementation, 2x/day, for 4-5 months during hot-climate training seasons, in addition to during-run electrolyte products (names "Enduralytes," "Light Switch," Scratch, Nuun, Element, Osmo). (SCIENCE — mechanistic explanation, general physiology, no specific study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — hydration/electrolytes, general, though the osmosis mechanism explanation is a specific teaching point)
- External cooling tactics: wear minimal clothing in humid conditions (technical fabric doesn't help because evaporative cooling is blocked by humidity regardless of fabric), dump cold water on head/back of neck/wrists, put ice under a hat or armpits (identified as effective cooling points), and carry a cooler with iced rags for out-and-back long runs. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific external-cooling tactics list, more granular than general "stay cool" advice)
- Heat exhaustion warning signs: chills, dizziness, nausea, unusually high fatigue/effort, and elevated heart rate relative to normal; explicit instruction to stop the run immediately, cool both externally (ice on head/neck/armpits/wrists) and internally (cold fluids) if these appear, and NOT resume the run even after symptoms subside; distinguishes heat exhaustion from heat stroke (marked by cessation of sweating and system shutdown) as the danger point never to reach. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — standard exercise-medicine guidance, not attributed to a specific source) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — heat illness, general, but likely worth verifying the site has this level of practical actionable detail)
- Self-talk study (Alex Hutchinson/Sweat Science, named): 29 volunteers ran 1 hour at moderate pace under three conditions — trained positive self-talk, trained negative self-talk, or a neutral distraction (a Stephen Hawking documentary). Reported finding: the negative self-talk group had significantly higher perceived effort, faster breathing rate, and higher salivary cortisol; there was NO measurable difference between the positive self-talk group and the neutral-distraction group.
