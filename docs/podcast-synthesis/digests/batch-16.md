## Episode #261: The Magic of the Medium Long Run
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode hosted by Chris McClung (Rogue Running coach, Austin, TX).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Medium-long run (MLR) has two purposes: (1) primary — build aerobic capacity/the oxygen-delivery pathway from lungs to mitochondria; (2) secondary — neuromuscular resilience from covering distance, sometimes on tired legs. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base development)
- Weekly structure rule: three "anchor" days (long run, quality/speed day, MLR) must each be separated by at least one day, ideally an easy/recovery day. Gives concrete example weekly templates. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit micro-scheduling framework for the week)
- Distance targets: MLR should be 50–75% of weekly long-run distance. Half-marathoners: build to 6–8 mi (up to 10–12 for advanced); marathoners: build to 8–10 mi (up to 15 for advanced). Build by +1 mile roughly every third week, stepping back 1–2 miles on down weeks. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific numeric prescriptions rarely spelled out this concretely)
- Claim that benefit is non-linear: "mile 1 is not the same as mile 2... mile 10" — later miles of an MLR (e.g., miles 6–10) deliver disproportionately more aerobic benefit than early miles. Presented as fact, no study cited. (SCIENCE — asserted, not sourced) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Pace/effort: must be conversational (full sentences); rule of thumb 1–2 min/mile slower than marathon pace or 1.5–2.5 min/mile slower than half-marathon pace; Maffetone 180-minus-age as an upper heart-rate bound for the effort. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy-pace/Maffetone method, though the specific pace-offset rules of thumb are a finer detail)
- Strength-training analogy: distance runners resist high-volume/low-intensity principles that lifters readily accept — "lower intensity, higher volume most of the time, occasionally max out" applies identically to running easy most days and hard occasionally. (ANALOGY)
- Car-engine analogy: aerobic capacity work (MLR, long run, easy running) = adding cylinders/engine size; speed work = fine-tuning the engine. "A V8 will smash a really revved-up fine-tuned four-cylinder all day long." (ANALOGY)
- Recommends tacking 4–6 strides (80–100m, build/hold/cruise thirds, 90–95% peak speed) onto the end of the MLR for speed/form maintenance; advanced runners can add 4–6 pickups of 30–90 sec inside the MLR. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strides)
- Strides analogy: "little allergy shots for speed" — one dose does nothing, but weekly doses over years build cumulative "immunity"/efficiency. (ANALOGY)
- Missed-workout decision framework: (1) something is always better than nothing — a shortened run or cross-training beats skipping; (2) rearranging the week is fine only with advance notice and only if the day-gap rule between anchor days is preserved; (3) otherwise, just skip and move to the next scheduled day — don't stack anchor days back-to-back, which is called out as a common injury cause. Also: if time-constrained, cap the run by time rather than speeding up to hit target mileage. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete triage protocol for missed sessions)
- Seasonal adjustment: pace on MLR may shift 30–60 sec/mile between winter and hot/humid summer conditions to preserve the correct effort zone. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — heat adaptation, though the quantified offset is a useful specific)

## Episode #262: Prepping for Downhill Races
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Downhill courses can create overconfidence; a course like Boston (net downhill + rollers) is arguably harder than a flat course, not easier, despite the elevation profile. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with the common assumption that "downhill = free speed") (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Know your course": dig into detailed elevation profiles (recommends findmymarathon.com, Strava historical runs, MapMyRun) and watch out for elevation-chart scale distortion — narrow y-axis scales exaggerate hills, wide scales flatten them. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete race-recon tooling/technique)
- Eccentric loading explained as the physiological core issue on downhills: muscle lengthens under load (e.g., quads lowering the body on a downhill stride or the lowering phase of a squat), as opposed to concentric loading (shortening under load, e.g., pushing back up). (SCIENCE — standard physiology, not attributed to a specific study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if strength-training content already covers eccentric/concentric, otherwise POSSIBLE GAP for the downhill-specific application)
- Downhill running form cues: lean forward to stay perpendicular to the terrain (skiing analogy — "stay over your skis"); increase turnover instead of overstriding; consciously engage glutes and hamstrings (not just quads) to spread the eccentric load across more muscle groups and delay quad fatigue. (PRACTICE, ANALOGY: skiing) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific downhill running mechanics)
- Uphill form cues (contrast case): stay upright rather than hinging at the waist (hinging "short-circuits" glute engagement), eyes to the horizon, shorten stride/increase turnover, drive knees and arms. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Recommends incorporating hills into ordinary easy/recovery runs at least once a week (walking steep sections is fine) even if this means driving to find hills in a flat city; personal anecdote of running parking-garage ramps in Houston for hill access. (PRACTICE)
- Eccentric-specific strength work: emphasize the slow lowering phase of squats, lunges, and step-ups (opposite of the typical instinct to drop a weight fast and lift slowly, as in a bicep curl) — recommends incorporating this into at least one strength session per week when prepping for a downhill-heavy race. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — eccentric-specific strength prescription targeted at runners is a level of specificity general strength-training content may not include)
- Recommends simulating race terrain at pace roughly once every three weeks, either by modifying a normal quality workout to include the race's hill profile (e.g., mile repeats on a rolling course), doing explicit downhill-repeat workouts (hard up / marathon-pace-recovery down), or building "simulator" long runs that mimic the target course's terrain. Notes this is harder for extreme elevation courses (e.g., Mount Charleston, St. George) and describes runners using a car shuttle to repeat a single long downhill stretch. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — structured course-simulation methodology)

## Episode #264: The Dos and Don'ts of Running Tech
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung; references an unnamed conversation with a training partner who works at a wearable-tech company.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core framing: wearables are useful but "fundamentally flawed" tools, not sources of ground truth — illustrated by the fact that a group of runners all wearing GPS watches will hit a mile marker at slightly different times/distances. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — practical tech-literacy guidance, distinct from physiology content)
- Claims a rough accuracy hierarchy: GPS pace/distance and chest-strap heart rate are relatively accurate; wrist-based heart rate is noticeably less reliable; sleep-cycle tracking (Oura, Whoop) is "the least accurate," per unspecified "studies." (SCIENCE — cites "the studies" without naming any) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Advises distrusting device-generated algorithmic scores/labels — Garmin's "training status" (productive/unproductive/maintaining), Garmin recovery-time estimates, VO2max estimators, and Oura's daily readiness/sleep scores — because they're calibrated to general populations, not the individual; recommends looking at underlying raw data (e.g., HRV trend) instead of composite scores, and even disabling some features. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with vendor marketing claims) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Practical tip: on a measured course or track, use lap time (not the watch's interpolated instantaneous/GPS pace) to judge pace, since GPS pace is unreliable over short distances and especially inaccurate on tracks (frequent turns, infrequent GPS pings). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete workout-execution technique)
- Argues effort/pace self-calibration (RPE) can become "as good as a watch," citing unspecified "studies in science" without further detail, and that over-reliance on watch-checking during a run interferes with the skill of dialing into effort and finding relaxation/efficiency at a given pace. (SCIENCE — vague attribution) (DISAGREEMENT with heavily metrics-driven training culture)
- Recommends checking the watch only periodically during workouts (roughly every 200–400m) rather than staring at it continuously, partly because the display lags/misreads pace for the first ~100m after a pace change. (PRACTICE)
- Recommends uploading/reviewing data after the fact (Strava/Garmin Connect) and calibrating it against how the effort felt, rather than obsessing over real-time numbers; values having a long-term training history to review before races or after an injury. (PRACTICE)
- Warns against becoming "overly obsessed" with the data — examples given: ego attachment to recovery-run pace turning easy days into "junk miles," and sleep-tracking apps (e.g., Oura) potentially causing anxiety about sleep scores that then worsens actual sleep. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific feedback-loop/anxiety mechanism around wearables is a fairly modern, non-book-sourced angle)
- "Don't do it for Strava" — explicit warning against training behavior (going too fast on easy days, chasing segments/badges) driven by social perception rather than actual goals. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — social-media-era training pitfall, unlikely to be covered by traditional book/paper-sourced site content)
- Recommends occasionally running an easy run or even a workout with no watch at all as a "liberating" recalibration exercise. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #265: The Fear and Courage of Running
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung. Draws on two Brené Brown quotes (not a running-specific source).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Opens with a listener Q&A (Victoria): sequencing shorter training cycles (mile/5K/10K/half) before returning to the marathon does not need to be in ascending order — what matters is covering all distances over a year or more, sequenced around local race-season opportunity rather than a fixed progression. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodized cross-distance training, general concept probably covered; specific "order doesn't matter" claim is a minor wrinkle)
- Brené Brown quotes used as framing devices: "You can choose courage or you can choose comfort, but not both, not at the same time," and "Choose the great adventure of being brave and afraid at the exact same time." (quotes worth preserving near-verbatim) (POSSIBLE GAP — sports-psychology framing sourced outside typical running literature)
- Taxonomy of racing-related fears, presented as a checklist: fear of the unknown (not knowing how new pain will feel), fear of the known (knowing exactly how much a familiar distance will hurt), fear of failure, fear of success (subtle, self-sabotaging — raising the bar if you achieve a goal), fear of not being good enough, fear of disapproval (argued to be "mostly not real" in a supportive running community), and fear of disappointment (avoiding sitting with post-race letdown by rushing to the next goal). (PRACTICE/framework) (POSSIBLE GAP — a fairly granular fear taxonomy specific to endurance racing, beyond generic "sports psychology")
- Self-sabotage mechanism named explicitly: some athletes under-train on purpose so they have a built-in excuse if they fail, avoiding the harder confrontation of "I did everything right and still fell short." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Personal narrative-reframe technique: shifting internal story from "it can't be me because [life circumstances]" to "why not me?" — credited with unlocking PRs at every distance from 5K to marathon in his own case. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — self-talk reframing is standard sports psych, though the concrete before/after anecdote adds specificity)
- Three-step coping process for fear: (1) acknowledge and name it, sit in it rather than dismiss it; (2) take the next smallest concrete action toward it (fear is described as primarily "anticipatory" — it dissipates once you're actually doing the thing); (3) repeat, and do a post-race "was it as bad as I feared?" comparison to build confidence for future fear-facing. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete, repeatable stepwise protocol rather than generic reassurance)
- Seven manifestations of courage in the sport: setting big goals, doing the work (framed as courageous because it removes the excuse of not trying), getting to the start line, facing pain mid-race, sitting in disappointment/feelings after a bad result, standing up and racing again after failure, and sharing your goals/journey publicly. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Uses Mikaela Shiffrin sitting on the slope with her head in her hands after skiing out of the Olympic slalom as an example that publicly sitting with failure is strength, not weakness. (ANALOGY/example, not running-specific but used as a running-psychology teaching case)
- Coaching practice: explicitly naming and reflecting an athlete's own courage back to them ("speak truth to them") as a way of transferring belief and unlocking bigger goals. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #266: How-To Guide to the Tempo Workout
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Defines tempo effort as sitting between aerobic threshold (where lactate first starts accumulating) and lactate/anaerobic threshold (where the body can no longer clear/use lactate); tempo work trains the body's ability to process/discard lactate and sustain hard paces longer — described as "aerobic strength." (SCIENCE — standard framework, not attributed to a specific study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — lactate threshold training)
- Explicitly disputes common usage: argues many programs mislabel aerobic-threshold/steady-state runs as "tempo," when true tempo (his definition) is a distinct, harder effort roughly at half-marathon pace/effort. (DISAGREEMENT with loose industry terminology) (POSSIBLE GAP — terminology clarification)
- Names four distinct tempo workout formats: (1) "true tempo" — continuous 3–4 mi / 20–30 min at ~half-marathon effort; (2) "broken tempo" — same total effort split into reps (e.g., 1K–2mi) with short 1–2 min recoveries to stay in the aerobic-strength zone; (3) "mixed" tempo workouts pairing tempo with faster reps to simulate racing on tired legs, including a "Michigan workout" (credited to the late Ron Warhurst, University of Michigan coach) alternating track intervals with road tempo segments, a "Hills Plus Tempo" combo, a self-named "cluster workout" (400s + tempo mile(s) + fast 400s), and continuous "in-and-out" alternating of faster/tempo pace; (4) "long tempo" — a rare 6–10 mi variant mainly for marathoners. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the mixed/combo workout catalog, with an attributed coaching lineage, is a specific practical contribution)
- Personal opinion: if forced to pick only one workout type forever, tempo (plus strides and easy running) "would probably get you really far." (PRACTICE — opinion)
- Warns tempo is frequently overdone in training plans (e.g., speed + tempo + long run with workout, all in one week), calling that combination "a recipe for getting injured" and for plateauing because it never builds aerobic base ("engine size"), only "fine-tunes" it. Recommends one quality/tempo day, one medium-long (easy) day, and one long run per week instead. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with commonly-seen weekly structures)
- Recommended placement across a training cycle: broken tempo in the "priming" phase (~every third week); true/mixed/long tempo every other week during the aerobic-strength phase; tempo work during the race-specific phase for half-marathoners specifically (because tempo effort converges with half-marathon race pace); a short half-marathon-pace fartlek in race week to "prime" the body regardless of race distance. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization generally, with tempo-specific placement as a finer detail)
- Execution guidance: tempo is an effort, not a fixed pace — you should finish feeling like you could have kept going, not fully spent; pace may legitimately vary run to run (illustrated with his own 6K tempo running 3–5 sec/mile slower on a cold day with tired legs versus three weeks prior, same effort/benefit either way). Encourages "letting go of ego" about pace variance. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — RPE-based/effort-based training)
- Mental-execution technique specific to tempo: minimize watch-checking; practice active relaxation as a form of "moving meditation" — scanning face/shoulders/arms/legs for tension, sometimes closing the eyes on a clear straightaway, controlling breathing, using rhythm mantras — argued to translate directly to race-day composure at faster paces. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific mental/relaxation technique tied concretely to a workout type, distinct from generic "stay positive" sports psychology)

## Episode #267: 5K-10K Training with My Coach Cathy Casey
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Cathy Casey — the host's own coach; former head coach/program director of SMU Women's Track & Field for 18 years (volunteer assistant → head cross-country coach → program director); competed in track/cross country at the University of Texas; now coaches the Team Rogue morning group in Austin. Also a highly experienced age-group triathlete (8 full Ironmans, 15 half-Ironmans; competed at the 70.3 World Championship in St. George, UT).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Coaching philosophy learned from mentor Dave Wolman (a throws coach, not a distance coach): great coaching is an "art and a science" — it requires understanding what makes an individual athlete tick, not just applying textbook training principles. (PRACTICE)
- Drew training philosophy from international mentors across England, Norway, New Zealand (Lydiard system), and South Africa (barefoot-on-grass training) and concluded there is no single correct training system — many different approaches produce elite results. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — an explicit "training pluralism" framing that may contrast with a site organized around one canonical methodology)
- Believes pursuing her own athletic goals (Ironman racing) in parallel with coaching makes her a better coach and models to masters (40+) athletes that ambitious goals remain achievable with age. (PRACTICE)
- 5K/10K block periodization: base/foundation phase of easy volume → touching on pace via fartlek/broken-tempo work → race/course-specific work (e.g., adding hill repeats because the target race, the CAP 10K, has hills) → progressively tightening recovery while holding target paces. Uses a 3-week micro-cycle: high-volume week (~10–12K of quality work), medium week (~8K), and a down/short week emphasizing higher-speed turnover work. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general periodization concept, though the specific volume figures are a finer detail)
- Deliberately varies rest-interval length by workout purpose: longer rest (2–3 min) protects quality on true VO2max-pace reps (800s/1200s at 3K–mile pace); short, tight rest is used for threshold/tempo work to keep heart rate elevated and stay in the aerobic-strength zone. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general interval-design principle)
- Named benchmark/signature workouts: 5x1K @ 90-sec rest as a "gold standard" 5K fitness indicator; 10–11x1K @ 90-sec rest or 6–7x mile at goal pace for 10K work. Notably, she deliberately reframes the same underlying stimulus in different "packaging" (miles vs. 1Ks vs. 800s) depending on which distance-unit a given athlete mentally tolerates better — e.g., the host dislikes 1K repeats but responds well to the equivalent workout framed as mile repeats. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — using interval "packaging"/unit-framing as a psychological lever while holding the physiological stimulus constant is a distinct, transferable coaching insight)
- Embeds speed pickups mid-run rather than only at the start or end (e.g., 8x30-second pickups within a medium-long run), reasoning that pickups placed mid-run train turnover in a way that quietly speeds up the whole run without the athlete "grinding" consciously. (PRACTICE)
- Uses the same nominal interval (400m) to train completely different systems depending on terrain and rest structure: flat-track 400s at 3K–mile pace with full 2-min recovery for pure speed, versus 400m hill repeats where the downhill "recovery" pace is deliberately timed/held honest, converting the same distance into a de facto 4–5 mile threshold/strength workout. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — reframing one interval distance as a flexible tool via terrain/rest manipulation)
- Always appends short, fast reps ("cherries on top" — e.g., 4–5x30-sec hard or 200m reps) to the end of threshold/tempo sessions specifically to train and build confidence in the ability to finish a race fast on tired legs — "you always have one more gear." (PRACTICE)
- Maintains long runs even during a 5K/10K-focused block (~16 mi / ~2 hrs) for aerobic foundation, but keeps them shorter than marathon-training long runs specifically to protect recovery for quality-day paces. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — long run/quality-day tradeoff, a standard periodization concept)
- Recommends two tune-up 5K races ahead of a goal 10K: an early one to "bust the rust" and reacquaint the athlete with race-pain tolerance, and a second 2–3 weeks before the goal race to rehearse race-specific pacing and tactics; framed as low-cost because 5K racing requires minimal recovery. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete tune-up-race sequencing prescription)
- Race-strategy claims: for a flat 5K, go out hard in roughly the first 100m and then settle in, reasoning there's a ~20–30 second window before lactic acid meaningfully builds up — a notably different pacing instinct than the "don't go out too fast" advice typical for half/full marathons. For a 10K, success is about establishing even rhythm ("you can spot a 10K runner a mile away" by their pacing consistency), with a tactical move typically saved for the final ~2K. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — the 5K fast-start rationale differs from standard longer-distance pacing advice, though she frames it as distance-specific) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general pacing strategy, though the distance-specific nuance is a finer point)
- Individualizes programming by working backward from each athlete's "A goal" and by background — e.g., an athlete arriving from a low-mileage 800m background needs a different build than an established high-mileage marathoner; illustrates with an anecdote that at NCAA championships, milers and 10K specialists reached the same start line via entirely different training histories. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — individualized coaching is a common theme)

## Episode #269: Getting the Most from Your Coach
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode, Chris McClung (references Coach Cathy Casey, guest of episode #267, as the inspiration for this topic).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Practical tool note: the McMillan Running calculator changed its default output to a "training paces" tab that bakes in McMillan's own assumptions about workout purpose per distance; recommends instead using the "race times" tab (equivalent-performance times across distances) and pulling target paces based on whatever purpose your own coach has assigned to a given workout, since a coach's intended purpose for, say, a 1000m repeat may not match McMillan's. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a narrow but concrete tool-usage correction)
- Presents a three-pillar framework for getting the most out of a coach-athlete relationship: Trust, Communication, Ownership. (framework) (POSSIBLE GAP — this coach-athlete relationship framework is a distinct angle from physiology/training-methodology content and would likely be new to a site focused on exercise science)
- Trust pillar: must genuinely believe in the coach (credentials, knowledge, style) without necessarily agreeing with every decision; introduces the "dumb athlete" concept — willingness to execute a prescribed plan without over-analyzing it — as one valid athlete temperament, contrasted with a more inquisitive style that needs the "why" explained; stresses following prescribed paces/distances exactly rather than self-modifying out of distrust; and explicitly argues "no one workout matters in the grand scheme" — what matters is consistent execution across a training cycle and across years, so athletes should avoid overthinking any single session. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Communication pillar, four specific things to proactively communicate: (1) your personal "why"/purpose behind your goals, since it shapes how a coach motivates you; (2) injuries/niggles the moment they appear rather than after weeks of silence — explicitly named as his "number one pet peeve" as a coach, since early small issues are easy to manage while established ones force training disruption; (3) qualitative feedback on key workouts (how it felt), which he argues is more valuable to a coach than raw Strava/Garmin data alone, since it feeds into future workout design and race-plan construction; (4) questions whenever something is unclear — noted that written workouts sometimes contain simple transcription errors that only get caught because an athlete asked. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete, actionable communication checklist for a coached athlete)
- Ownership pillar: be "solution-oriented" — bring a proposed adjustment to the coach (e.g., "I have a vacation coming up, here's how I'd modify the plan, what do you think?") rather than only surfacing the problem, as a way of building independent in-the-moment decision-making for when the coach isn't present; do your own thinking on goal race times and race-day execution plans before finalizing them with your coach, since self-generated plans are argued to be more deeply internalized and easier to execute under race stress than plans simply handed down; and take "radical personal responsibility" for outcomes — crediting yourself (not just the coach) after a success, and honestly examining what you personally could have done differently after a shortfall, without blaming external conditions or the coach. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — "radical personal responsibility" and the solution-oriented-athlete framing are a distinct angle on athlete agency within a coaching relationship)
