## Episode #374: Defining (and Re-Defining) Success on Race Day
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung, founder/head coach of Rogue Running (Austin/Dallas run coaching company), elite-level marathoner (sub-3 personal experience, multiple Boston finishes) and 20-year coach.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central thesis: the sport has narrowed success to a single axis (finish time/PR), especially in the "Super Shoe era," and this narrow framing amplifies pressure into fear/anxiety that can hurt performance. (PRACTICE)
- Analogy: skateboarding/surfing culture treats failure as a built-in, celebrated part of the process (99 failed attempts before 1 successful trick); running culture doesn't extend runners the same grace. (ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — reframing "failure tolerance" as a deliberate psychological strategy, distinct from generic "growth mindset" content)
- Analogy: "every race is a different wave" — even the same course year to year has different weather/conditions/context, so comparing times across identical courses undervalues the situational variance (uses Austin's Capital 10,000 as an example: same course, very different weather two years running). (ANALOGY)
- Example: Grand Slam Track (Michael Johnson's series) — Grant Fisher won $100k for winning the 5K/3K combined format at times over a minute slower than his PRs, illustrating that "success" can mean beating a field/winning money rather than posting a fast time. (PRACTICE)
- Ten reframed definitions of success offered as a coaching tool: (1) showing up to the start line despite setbacks, (2) getting to the finish line at all, (3) having fun/maximizing the intended experience of a race (cites Eliud Kipchoge's emphasis on smiling as performance-relevant), (4) handling a "worst day" and minimizing damage rather than falling apart, (5) finishing strong/negative-split as its own win, (6) racing and beating people regardless of absolute time (cross-country team scoring given as an example where 6th/7th-place finishers still matter), (7) adjusting well to weather/conditions, (8) running a "beautiful race" (Boston described as requiring flow/adaptation over brute-force effort — "the difference between forcing it and using the force"), (9) precision of execution against a stated plan, (10) giving maximum effort regardless of clock time. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this multi-axis success framework as an explicit sports-psychology tool doesn't map cleanly onto generic "process over outcome" content; it's more granular and race-day-specific)
- Personal anecdote: 2016 Boston Marathon — McClung developed a calcaneus stress fracture around mile 21, walked the final 5 miles, and calls it one of his most fulfilling marathons despite it being his slowest. (PRACTICE)
- Claim: "when everything goes smoothly, you don't necessarily learn who you are" — adversity in a race is itself the value proposition of racing, not an obstacle to it. (PRACTICE)
- Anecdote/analogy: a prior coach told McClung that if the goal for a race is "having fun," you should pursue "max fun" with full intention/effort, just as you would pursue a fast time — framed as "give your all to your intention." (ANALOGY)

## Episode #376: Listener Q&A on Building Back, Finding Your Edge, and More

**Concepts & claims:**
- Building back after a long break (surgery, injury, life disruption): recommended sequence is days → distance → quality/speed — establish routine/days first, then add volume (~10%/week), only reintroduce speed once long run reaches ~10 miles. Cites his own episode #323 for full detail. Full rebuild timeline: 6-8 weeks to feel "normal-ish," 5-6 months for a full rebuild of routine/fitness before returning to marathon-specific training (recommends an intermediate half-marathon cycle first). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general return-to-running progression, though the specific "days→distance→quality" sequencing and 10-mile-long-run gate before speed work is a concrete, citable protocol)
- "Duality of mind" mental technique: let yourself dream about big long-term goals, but don't linger there — immediately pivot to "what's the next logical step today," repeating this dream/practical cycle to sustain motivation without becoming overwhelmed or discouraged by the gap to the goal. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a named, reusable mental technique for reconciling long-term ambition with day-to-day patience)
- On overcoming ego resistance to zone 2/easy running: analogy that lifters unquestioningly do many reps at submaximal weight to build strength, but runners resist the analogous idea of high-volume slow running — "for some reason, it breaks our brains." (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy-running/zone 2 importance is likely already covered, but the strength-training analogy for ego resistance is a fresh framing)
- Claim: measurable zone 2 pace improvement from consistent easy-volume training typically takes 5-6 months, and shows up more reliably in race performance (e.g., faster 5K) than in a persistent shift of zone 2 heart-rate pace itself. (SCIENCE — asserted from coaching experience/aggregate athlete data, not a named study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Advice against over-racing/over-distance racing (ultras, Ironmans, marathons) for a masters athlete (53F) trying to reclaim a sub-3 marathon: recommends a full year without racing beyond marathon distance, refocused on 5K/10K/half-specific speed work, plus mandatory strength training in the 40+ age range — explicitly says find a personal trainer who understands runners. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strength training for aging runners is probably covered, but the "give up a full year of over-distance racing to reclaim speed" prescription is a specific, somewhat counterintuitive tactical claim)
- On "full send"/mental willingness to suffer: recommends (a) reconnecting to a specific, deeply-felt "why" (purpose) rather than a surface-level goal, ideally explored with a coach/partner who can ask "second-order why" questions, and (b) practicing "going to the edge" more frequently via shorter, more frequent races (5K/10K/half) rather than only marathons, since marathons don't allow enough "at bats." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for general sports psych, but the "shorter races = more reps at practicing suffering" tactical argument is a specific angle)

## Episode #377: Six Ways to Be Patient to Reach Your Potential
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode, host Chris McClung, framed as an extended answer to a listener (April) who is nervous about a big fitness jump (half-marathon PR implying a marathon calculator projection dramatically faster than her prior marathon).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Six patience principles for reaching long-term potential: (1) take a long-term view — think in terms of decades (framed as "beyond rational planning horizons," e.g., 20 years), since aerobic development can continue improving for 10-15+ years if training is consistent and injury-free; (2) stay process-focused rather than outcome-focused (uses Hank Aaron's home-run-hitting career as an analogy: he focused on hitting mechanics/process, not homer counts, and the numbers followed); (3) let the process/data (training paces, workout feedback, prep-race results) dictate interim goals and race paces rather than starting from an aspirational target — "you have to train where you are to get where you want to go"; (4) recover as seriously as you train hard — recovery runs and down weeks are where fitness is actually consolidated, not built; (5) when in doubt, make the conservative choice (on volume increases, workout reps/paces, race-day target times); (6) when in doubt on easy days, slow down further — sets explicit target paces for recovery/easy runs (90 sec/mile slower than marathon pace for easy days, 2+ min/mile slower for recovery days as a baseline, adjusted further for heat). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/patience/recovery are probably covered generally, but the specific "20-year framing," Hank Aaron analogy, and exact recovery-pace offsets are citable specifics)
- Claim: aerobic capacity/base can keep improving for 10-15 years of consistent training, and additional gains are possible for 15-20+ years by optimizing smaller factors (neuromuscular, musculoskeletal, strength) once aerobic capacity plateaus. (SCIENCE — asserted, no named study/researcher) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Practical anecdote: an athlete who initially set a "just break 4 hours" marathon goal ended up running ~20 minutes faster than that in his first cycle because the training process revealed more potential than his own stated goal — illustrates the risk of self-limiting goals set before data exists. (PRACTICE)
- Strength-training deload comparison: a strength coach's practice of deloading 40-60% of volume every 6-7 weeks is contrasted with running, where the whole aerobic/musculoskeletal system gets loaded 5-6 days/week (vs. one muscle group once/week in lifting), so runners need more frequent deloads (down weeks every 3rd week, 2 recovery runs/week) to consolidate fitness. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — reasoned analogy, not a cited study) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific runner-vs-lifter deload-frequency rationale)

## Episode #378: Priming towards Your Fall Race
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode, host Chris McClung. Cites Arthur Lydiard as "the father of modern distance coaching" as the source of the periodized base/priming approach.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Seven components of a good "priming" (base) phase: (1) organize/write out the full training blueprint (phase lengths, peak volume, race logistics) in advance, while accepting it will change ("no perfect plan" — quotes Mike Tyson's "everybody has a plan until they get punched in the mouth"); (2) establish a consistent day-by-day routine (recovery, hydration, sleep) so weeks become repeatable; (3) build base volume now — argues foundational-phase miles matter as much or more than later miles because they set the ceiling for later fitness; (4) get logistical supplies sorted early (shoes, nutrition, hydration products, anti-chafe products) — likened to provisioning for a long road trip; (5) begin low-stakes "speed development" work (strides progressing to hill strides/fartlek-style efforts like a "3-2-1 fartlek": 3 min at marathon effort/3 easy, 2 min half effort/2 easy, 1 min faster/1 easy, repeated) meant purely for neuromuscular priming, never to failure/fatigue; (6) keep easy-run paces very conservative in this phase — specific guidance of at least 90 sec/mile slower than marathon pace for easy/long runs and 2.5 min/mile slower for recovery runs (add extra margin for summer heat); (7) adopt a "conservative-choice" mentality throughout this phase as a deliberate signal to the body that it is not yet time to peak. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/base-building generally, but the specific pacing formulas and the "peak is controlled partly by mental signaling, not just physical load" framing add specificity)
- Claim/framing: "you control your peak with your mind as much as with physical training" — conservative training signals (slower paces, fewer reps, more recovery) tell the body it's safe and not yet time to peak; conversely, precision and edge-pushing later signal it's time to sharpen. This is offered as the answer to athletes worried that an 18-20 week cycle is "too long" and will cause early peaking. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a psychophysiological framing of peaking as partly a deliberate "signal management" process, distinct from standard periodization theory)
- Strides technique detail: 80-100m stretch, thirds structure (build speed / hold speed / ease off), max 85-95% of peak speed, full walk-back recovery between reps — explicit correction that these are not "high school wind sprints." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Heat-adaptation timeline reiterated: expect 4-6 weeks to adapt to summer heat/humidity, with paces adjusted 30-40 sec/mile slower for equivalent effort; electrolyte supplementation framed as necessary for water absorption (without electrolytes, water "goes straight to the kidneys" without being absorbed by cells) — this specific mechanism claim also appears in episode #377/#380 intros. (SCIENCE — asserted, no study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general heat acclimation/hydration, though the specific "water can't be absorbed without electrolytes, so it just gets urinated out" mechanism claim is a concrete, possibly-oversimplified physiological claim worth fact-checking)

## Episode #38: Listener Questions with Jean and Ardith
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Co-host Steve (a Rogue Running coach, coaches an elite/competitive training group, cites experience with elite athletes and cross-training/triathlon coaching standards) alongside host Chris McClung. Guests Jean Ragotti and Ardith are longtime amateur runner-listeners (not professional coaches) — Jean a ~17-year runner and current Rogue athlete (qualified for and ran Boston), Ardith a multi-sport/triathlon-experienced runner who trained with Rogue's predecessor (RunTex) in the early 2000s and later moved to San Antonio.

**Concepts & claims:**
- "Statement of purpose" as a formal coaching exercise: Steve describes explicitly having elite athletes write down their running purpose in prose so the coach can invoke it verbatim during hard training moments ("you told me you wanted to do this for X reason") — described as making coaching decisions in crisis moments far easier once the athlete's foundational "why" is established and referenceable. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific technique of eliciting and using a written, coach-referenceable purpose statement as an in-the-moment motivational tool, distinct from generic "find your why" advice)
- Claim: purpose is often "situational" at the umbrella level but expressed differently race to race — e.g., an athlete drawing on "being a hero" as a race-specific activation of a broader purpose. (PRACTICE)
- Coaching technique example ("Zilker Crush" workout): athletes are given a choice (one set vs. two sets of a descending-distance/increasing-pace interval workout — 2K at half pace, 1600m at 10K pace, 1200m at 5K pace, 800m faster than 5K pace) and told they must decide before finishing, framed explicitly as a mental-training exercise in decision-ownership and reframing "stopping at one set" as not a failure. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — using workout structure itself, with athlete-directed choice points, as a deliberate mental-training/decision-ownership tool)
- "Command performance" concept: Steve argues athletes should have a defined goal race to work toward (rather than "having fun, no expectations") because vague or diffuse goals lead athletes to be dishonest with themselves about effort and motivation; without a stated purpose, athletes often register for races just to "have something out there" rather than out of genuine intent — called out bluntly as athletes being "full of shit" about their real motivations. (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the idea that low-pressure/no-goal racing is inherently healthier) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Suck" management technique: Steve assigns athletes three graduated pace tiers (e.g., based on a recent marathon time, plus 5-min-per-mile-equivalent faster tiers) for quality/threshold workouts, deliberately bounding how hard/uncomfortable training should feel and explicitly limiting "suck" to only 2-3 quality days per week/month rather than every run — reframes perceived difficulty as a controllable, quantified variable rather than a vague dread. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Claim from Joe Vigil (per Steve, sports scientist, longtime elite distance coach, worked with Colorado's Alamosa altitude lab and the U.S. Olympic Training Center lab): physiological studies suggested VO2max is most acutely stressed around the 2-mile/3K distance, and that trained athletes could theoretically run their mile pace for 2 miles, but rarely do because of pacing/tactical hesitancy rather than a physiological limit. (SCIENCE — named researcher/institution, but described as Steve's paraphrase/recollection rather than a citable published study) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Race-distance mental-training framework: 5K described as a compressed analog of the marathon (front-loaded uncertainty about pacing, same anxiety dynamics in a shorter window); 10K framed as teaching "going to the wall and coming back" (blow up, recover composure, push again); half marathon described as the "least valuable" distance for marathon-specific mental prep because it's comparatively easy pacing-wise, so should be deliberately structured (e.g., intentionally too fast/slow, or embedding 10K-pace segments) to extract training value. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a distance-by-distance mental-training taxonomy)
- "Miles Matter" — a listener's personal mantra/identity around loving easy, social, unhurried running — used as a jumping-off point for a broader discussion about needing to "embrace the suck" and confront a "fear of success"/self-limiting comfort zone despite claiming purely social motivations for running. (PRACTICE)
- Finding a coach: practical advice — ask senior staff at local running specialty stores who the well-regarded local coaches are, and ask them who coaches them; also check with local college track/XC coaches, who often know the coaching landscape even if unavailable themselves; multi-sport (triathlon) coaches are asserted to generally have stronger scientific/foundational coaching backgrounds than single-sport running coaches, per Steve. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT/opinion — an assertion about triathlon coaches being generally better-trained than running coaches, presented without evidence) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Coach-athlete "fit" concept: explicitly frames coaching success as partly about matching an athlete's needed level of touch/oversight (in-person accountability vs. self-directed/remote) to a coach's coaching style, not just credentials — "you have to know what kind of athlete you are." (PRACTICE)
- Masters/50+ training adjustments (from both Steve and Chris, ages 38 and mid-50s+ respectively at time of recording): (1) recovery becomes more important and athletes must be willing to back off further/more often than younger athletes, listening to the body rather than rigidly following prescribed workouts; (2) "extras" (strength, mobility, foam rolling, nutrition diligence) become mandatory rather than optional as you age — younger athletes can "cheat" on these and get away with it, older athletes cannot; (3) practical tools: color-coded training logs (marking each session's felt difficulty) to visually spot overtraining patterns, and daily resting heart rate tracking (specific protocol: alarm set 5 min before waking, snooze, lie still, count pulse for 15 seconds x4) to detect under-recovery — cites a personal baseline of ~48bpm fit / ~53bpm normal training stress / 57-59+bpm as a red flag requiring backing off. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — masters training and RHR monitoring are probably covered generally, but the specific color-coded-log technique and personal RHR thresholds are concrete, citable specifics) (POSSIBLE GAP for the color-coded training log technique specifically)
- Claim: well-established, aerobically mature runners (a "not aerobic babies" contrast to novices) get more benefit from replacing some easy mileage with additional threshold-zone work than from continuing to just add volume, since their aerobic base is already built. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- "Run a beautiful race" as a universal fallback goal/Plan B: Steve advocates that every athlete's Plan B (when the A-goal time is off the table due to conditions) should be "run a beautiful race" rather than a secondary time goal, arguing this framing can unlock performances beyond what pure physical-fitness-based pacing would predict — presented as a point of explicit disagreement with co-host Chris, who prefers training/racing strictly to physically-supported paces. ## Episode #380: Five Reminders in Prep for Your Fall Race
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode, host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Five late-cycle/taper reminders: (1) getting to the start line healthy outweighs any single remaining "capstone" workout or long run — pause/modify hard sessions rather than force them through injury or illness risk, since "the vast majority of the hay is already in the barn"; (2) be precise but don't chase the edge — during race-specific/taper workouts (example given: 6-10x800m at 10K pace with 90 sec-2 min recovery), resist the temptation to run faster than prescribed just because current fitness allows it; (3) no single workout (good or bad) determines race-day outcome — a bad late-cycle workout is an "outlier" on a season-long "scatter plot" of data and shouldn't be allowed to override overall trend/confidence; (4) don't introduce anything new in the final weeks (new strength routines, new gear, new diet, new activities) — "if you haven't done it to this point, it's too late... it's only going to hurt you"; (5) dedicate explicit time to mental preparation in the final weeks (not just final days) — reconnect to race purpose, write down a paced execution plan, use segmented visualization of race sections (including visualizing chaos at the start, tough course sections, and the finish-line clock), and prepare 2-3 "rhythm" mantras (early race) and "fight" mantras (late race) in advance. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — taper principles and visualization are probably covered generally, but the specific workout example, "scatter plot vs. outlier" framing, and rhythm-vs-fight mantra split are concrete specifics)
- Anecdote/quote (Lydiard): at an Olympics, competitors copied a workout they observed Lydiard's athletes doing, prompting Lydiard to remark they'd "just given the race" to his athletes — because the workout was calibrated specifically for his athletes' training stage and would over-stress athletes on a different plan/timeline. Used to argue against copying others' hard workouts indiscriminately. - Claim: visualization produces a neurological response similar to physically performing the action, cited as a reason detailed pre-race visualization "counts" as practice even for paces/efforts never physically executed before. (SCIENCE — asserted without naming a specific study or researcher) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — visualization/mental rehearsal is standard sports psychology, though worth flagging that no study is actually cited)
