## Episode #290: To Carbo Load or Not-
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung, founder/coach at Rogue Running (Austin, TX), coaching thousands of athletes across all levels.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Traditional pre-race pasta dinners are NOT effective "carb loading" — that's just eating a carb-heavy meal, not the physiological supercompensation process. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with common belief) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling, but the misconception callout is specific)
- Baseline diet leaves the body ~90% glycogen-loaded; true carb loading is a supercompensation protocol to reach ~100%, and requires 5–7 days, not one meal. (SCIENCE, general physiological claim, no specific study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Two named carb-loading protocols: (1) Depletion/"Ahlborg method" — after final long run (~7 days out), go low-carb/high-fat/high-protein until final sharpening workout (~4 days later), then load hard for the last 3 days before racing; (2) Non-depletion overcompensation method — starting 5 days out, eat 8–10g carbs/kg bodyweight/day (roughly 550–700g/day) for 4 days, then normal the day before. (SCIENCE — named methods/protocol details, but no researcher or paper cited by name) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific quantified protocols go beyond generic "eat more carbs" advice typically found in fueling content)
- Coach's explicit recommendation: don't carb load. Both protocols risk GI distress, bloating, and feeling "jittery"/"weird" during race week — too high-risk relative to benefit; based on his own experimentation. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with popular "carb load before your race" wisdom)
- Coined term "maranoia" (marathon paranoia) for taper-week anxiety spirals. (ANALOGY/term)
- Recommended alternative: eat completely normal foods/quantities race week; practice race-morning breakfast (90 min–2 hrs before, small, familiar) during training long runs; change nothing new. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general fueling practice)
- Race-prep checklist for final weeks: treat final long runs as "dress rehearsals" (test gear, food, pacing); don't add anything new to training or life (strength, yoga, house projects) within ~8 weeks of race day; reconnect mentally to original purpose/goal; prepare rhythm mantras (early-race calm) and fight mantras (late-race grit); taper 2–3 weeks; review training log pre-race to build start-line confidence. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for taper/mental prep broadly, but the "8-week no new things" rule and "dress rehearsal long run" framing are specific, actionable add-ons)
- Elite commentary: Kipchoge's durability/records attributed partly to never stacking marathons — sticking to ~2 peaks/year rather than racing back-to-back even with modern shoe technology; other athletes attempting quick doubles (e.g., Kira D'Amato, Sarah Hall) saw underperformance or injury. (SCIENCE/observation, not a controlled study — anecdotal pattern) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific claim that shoe tech hasn't changed the "2–3 peaks per year" limit could be a useful periodization data point)

## Episode #291: Is Your Training Polarized Enough-
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Car-engine analogy: aerobic capacity = engine size (number of cylinders), speed work = fine-tuning/revving the engine; you need both, developed in different ways, to reach potential. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — polarized training/aerobic base)
- 8 self-assessment questions for polarization, each with practical guidance:
  1. Are 80%+ of runs easy, with only ~1 quality workout/week for most runners (more reserved for advanced athletes)? (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
  2. Is there enough variation in run distances (long run, medium-long run, recovery runs distinctly different lengths)? Recovery runs recommended at minimum ~3 miles/30 min. (PRACTICE)
  3. Is easy/recovery pace slow enough? Specific quantified rule: long run/medium-long run should be ≥1 min/mile slower than marathon race pace or ≥90 sec/mile slower than half-marathon pace; recovery runs should be a further minute slower than that (i.e., ~2 min slower than MP / ~2.5 min slower than HMP). (PRACTICE — specific numeric heuristic) (POSSIBLE GAP — quantified pace-offset rule of thumb is concrete and may not be in generic aerobic-base content)
  4. Are strides included at least weekly? Format: 80–100m, build speed for first third, hold 90–95% peak speed middle third, float final third, 4–6 reps. Called "little inoculations of speed"/"allergy shots for speed" — benefits accrue over months/years, not from single sessions. (ANALOGY — "allergy shots" framing) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC but specific protocol/analogy may be new)
  5. Is speed work varied faster AND slower than race pace across a training cycle (not just at race pace)? (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
  6. Is there variety in race distances trained/raced (5K/10K work even for marathoners; longer distance work for half-marathoners)? Claim: best milers run 16–18 mile long runs weekly. (SCIENCE claim, unattributed) (POSSIBLE GAP — cross-training-distance-for-plateau-avoidance is a specific, actionable angle)
  7. Is there a real difference between peak-training blocks and post-race recovery/detraining windows? Recommends max 15–18 weeks/year at true peak (in 5–6 week blocks, 2–3 peaks/year) with 6–9 weeks/year of deliberately casual, unstructured training for detraining/reset. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit annual periodization windows/detraining framing may be underrepresented)
  8. Is training stress balanced against recovery, including life stress? Recommends using resting heart rate (or HRV) as a gauge — personal baseline: high-40s fully rested, low-50s in training, 55+ signals inadequate recovery/illness/life stress. (PRACTICE — self-tracking example) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — HRV/RHR monitoring likely covered, but concrete personal numbers may be a nice illustrative addition)
- Strength-training analogy used twice: nobody maxes their bench press every session — same logic (mostly light/high-rep, occasional max effort) applies to running pace distribution. (ANALOGY)

## Episode #293: Reframing Success as a Runner
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core reframe #1: stop measuring training runs by "how fast did I run" — instead ask "how slow can I run" (recovery/easy days) or "was I on target" (workouts). Going faster than prescribed on easy days is framed as counterproductive, not admirable. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with instinctive "faster = better" mentality) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic-base/easy-running principles, but explicit reframing language may be a fresh angle)
- Personal anecdote: ran every recovery run at his pregnant wife's much slower pace for an entire training cycle and still achieved a marathon PR that cycle — used as proof that recovery paces can be far slower than assumed without hurting performance. (PRACTICE/anecdote)
- Reframe #2: reject the idea of a "perfect training cycle." Recommends measuring consistency at getting ~90–95% of prescribed work done, and reframing missed/adjusted runs due to life circumstances (illness, poor sleep, family emergencies) as good decisions, not failures. Personal example: skipped a scheduled run to sleep after a child's overnight illness. (PRACTICE — theme recurs in ep. #294 as well)
- Reframe #3: judge workouts by what you learned, not by flawless execution. Coach states he gets "nervous" when a key workout goes perfectly, because imperfect sessions build the mental resilience needed for inevitable race-day struggle. Cites a Des Linden tweet: "sessions are for building fitness, races are for showing fitness." (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with the assumption that a flawless final long run predicts a good race; named quote from elite athlete)
- Reframe #4: don't be a slave to wearable data/algorithms (GPS pace, HRV scores, recovery scores) — prioritize subjective "how do I feel" over metrics, and let the body dictate recovery-run pace day to day. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with wearable-centric training culture) (POSSIBLE GAP — an explicit pushback on over-reliance on wearables/algorithms is a distinct, useful angle a data-heavy site might not otherwise include)
- Reframe #5: replace "am I suffering enough" with "am I having fun" as the measure of a good training approach — argues long-term (multi-year/decade) sustainability depends on enjoyment, not grit alone; suggests reconnecting to purpose, adding variety, or finding better training partners/coaches if the fun disappears. (PRACTICE)
- Reframe #6: judge races by absolute effort/execution in context, not by the finishing time alone. Personal anecdotes: 2016 Boston Marathon — had to walk the final 5 miles due to an undiagnosed heel stress fracture, his personal-worst marathon time, yet a race he's deeply proud of; 2015 "Martian Marathon" near Detroit — missed a PR by ~2 minutes in ~70°F heat, which he now considers possibly his best marathon performance given conditions. Both examples used to argue slower absolute times can represent equal or greater achievement than PRs, given context. (PRACTICE/anecdote — vivid, specific, useful for illustrating "process over outcome")
- Reframe #7: judge progress by the multi-year trend line/body of work, not any single race or workout. Explicitly rejects the common saying "you're only as good as your last race" as false. (DISAGREEMENT with a common running-culture aphorism)

## Episode #294: Slaying Your Pre-Race Demons
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Framing: pre-race doubts are universal (host has faced them across 21 marathons/~150 races) and should be acknowledged/named, not suppressed — the goal is coping skill, not elimination of nerves. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology, but framed usefully)
- Demon #1, "I didn't do enough": reframe missed/adjusted training as smart management rather than failure (recurring theme with ep. #293); recommends reviewing the training log and highlighting the 3–4 hardest completed sessions (not the perfect ones) as proof of readiness. Personal anecdotes: two different marathon PRs came after imperfect cycles (one managed around an injury, one where he "never felt sharp"). (PRACTICE/anecdote)
- Demon #2, "what if something bad happens again": coin-flip analogy — a prior bad race result doesn't statistically predict the next one's outcome. (ANALOGY) Concrete technique: physically write down past bad race experiences on paper and dispose of them ceremonially (burning, shredding, or throwing away) as a symbolic way to let go of carried baggage. Also recommends flipping "what-if negative" thinking to "what-if positive" and pairing it with full start-to-finish visualization. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY — the "burn the paper" ritual is a distinctive, memorable technique) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific release ritual for past-race baggage doesn't sound like generic sports-psych content)
- Visualization claim: mentally visualizing the race creates a neurological/muscle-memory effect similar to having actually performed it. (SCIENCE — asserted, no specific study or researcher named) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — visualization is likely already covered in existing sports psychology content)
- Demon #3, "fear of the unknown": reframe uncertainty about an unprecedented distance/time as exciting adventure rather than threat (compares it to traveling somewhere new); tactically, focus only on the smallest achievable next increment ("run a mile at a time") rather than mentally simulating the entire remaining race. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- Demon #4, "imposter syndrome": find a relatable proxy (someone similar to you who has achieved the goal) to internalize "if they can do it, I can too"; absent that, find someone who believes in you and can "speak truth" into your capability; write a list of concrete reasons you CAN succeed and review it daily to reprogram mindset — described as "being your own hero." - Demon #5, "I can't let someone down": reflect on personal history — points out that after his own worst-ever race (2016 Boston, walking the final 5 miles from an undiagnosed stress fracture), he received an outpouring of supportive messages rather than judgment, and argues this pattern holds for most people. (PRACTICE/anecdote/ANALOGY — the "timing mat as power-up" framing is a specific, exportable mental technique) (POSSIBLE GAP — likely a novel angle not found in generic race-day mental-game content)
- Demon #6, "can't handle nerves/pressure": distinguishes associative coping (channel nervous energy into productive pre-race planning — reviewing race plan, course map) from dissociative coping (distraction via books, movies) as two valid but different personal strategies. Race-day tactic: wear a "power uniform" — specific kit/gear that signals "it's game time" and boosts confidence — citing this concept from Kara Goucher's book "Strong." (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — named source is a real book, useful for cross-referencing since the site cites real books) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "power uniform" concept with a named source may be worth checking against existing content) Rhythm mantras used for early-race calm — personal example: focusing on breathing rhythm ("breathe in, breathe out") helped work through a rough cold-weather start in a marathon PR race.
- Demon #7, "what if I crack under pressure late in the race": fight mantras for the finish — personal example: visualizing a mental "Rolodex" of athletes he coaches finishing strong as inspiration during his own marathon PR. Additional late-race coping tools: "going fishing" (reeling in runners ahead one at a time) and counting to 30/60 seconds repeatedly as a bite-sized way to manage late-race pain. ## Episode #295: Giving Your All on Race Day

**Concepts & claims:**
- Framing comparison: Des Linden's 2018 Boston win (2:39, terrible rain/wind, reportedly nearly considered dropping out mid-race) vs. her 2011 Boston second-place finish (2:23) — argues context and effort matter more than absolute time, and that "giving your all" doesn't require feeling great or executing "heroically." (PRACTICE/anecdote — named elite athlete, verifiable results)
- Five tactics for "giving your all" on race day:
  1. Own that everyone (not just elites) has access to full effort — review your training log and specifically reflect on the hardest completed sessions, termed "anything workouts" (a workout so hard that completing it proves "you can do anything"). (ANALOGY/term)
  2. Don't grip too tightly to a specific finishing time — success is broader than the clock; illustrated via his own "equally proud" pairing of a 12-second PR (Houston 2018) and a stress-fracture-forced walk to the finish (Boston 2016, personal-worst time). (PRACTICE/anecdote — repeats theme from ep. #293)
  3. Make smart weather/course adjustments rather than blindly "toughing out" the original plan — courage is framed as adjusting, not stubbornly holding pace. Quantified rule of thumb given: add roughly 10 seconds/mile for every 5°F above 55°F (translated to a 30–40 sec/mile adjustment for the unseasonably warm NYC Marathon that year); roughly 20–30 sec/mile adjustment for a sustained 20+ mph headwind (as faced in Indianapolis). (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — specific quantified heat/wind pace-adjustment formula) (POSSIBLE GAP — this concrete formula for weather-based pace adjustment is actionable and may not be present in existing content)
  4. Sharpen the mental game: full-race visualization including the specific adversity expected (wind, heat); condition-specific mantras beyond the usual rhythm/fight mantras — e.g., a dedicated "wind mantra" or "heat mantra"; channeling energy from training partners/spectators/trackers; continuing to "go fish" (chase down runners ahead) throughout the race. - Secondhand Kara Goucher anecdote (relayed by host from a personal conversation): at the 2014 NYC Marathon, facing a headwind, she chose to stay with an overly ambitious pace group instead of dropping back to a more appropriate one, popped off the back around mile 13–14, and was later passed by runners from the group she'd abandoned. ## Episode #296: Back to the Basics of Recovery
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central argument: recovery is simpler than commonly assumed — the three foundational pillars (movement, sleep, fueling) matter far more than "fringe" modalities like ice baths, cryotherapy, or paid massage, whose supporting evidence he characterizes as still inconclusive. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with trendy recovery-modality culture) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery, but the explicit "don't overrate cryo/ice baths, evidence is thin" framing is a specific, checkable claim)
- Movement pillar: active recovery (easy running, walking, or low-impact cross-training) should follow every hard effort; never sequence two hard efforts back-to-back without active recovery between them — stated as the single most common injury pattern he sees as a coach. Recovery-run pace restated: 2–2.5 min/mile slower than marathon/half-marathon pace respectively, with "let the body dictate the pace" and no floor on how slow. Simple self-check: "does my body feel better after the run than before it started?" (PRACTICE — concrete heuristic) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Sleep pillar: recommends actively embracing napping (reframing it from "lazy" to legitimate active recovery) and basic sleep hygiene (dark, cool room; screens off ~1 hr before bed; avoid eating too close to bedtime). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery/sleep, though the napping-stigma reframe is a nice angle)
- Explicit skepticism of wearable sleep-stage tracking: claims the algorithms behind sleep-stage measurement in devices like Whoop, Oura, and newer Garmin watches are not yet very precise, and recommends trusting resting-heart-rate and HRV trend lines instead (trackable via a wearable or the free HRV4 phone-camera app). (SCIENCE/DISAGREEMENT — a specific, checkable claim pushing back on wearable sleep-stage accuracy) (POSSIBLE GAP — this explicit "don't trust wearable sleep scores" position is a distinct point that a data/wearables section might want to address directly)
- Fueling pillar: reframes carb-loading as most valuable AFTER a hard effort (not before a race, per ep. #290) — the post-workout/post-long-run window (~60–90 minutes) is described as the most underappreciated recovery lever, since the body is primed to absorb and restock glycogen then. Cites a "Sweat Science" article by science journalist Alex Hutchinson (that week) on the importance of post-exercise carb refueling.
