## Episode #336: Physical Therapy for Better Running with Dr. Morgan Riggins
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Dr. Morgan Riggins, DPT, orthopedic manual physical therapist (fellowship-trained, FAAOMPT candidate through the Manual Therapy Institute/American Academy of Orthopedic Manual Physical Therapy), former Mondo Sports Therapy clinician, now owner of Opal Movement Therapy in Austin. Former competitive swimmer (not a lifelong runner); ran her first marathon (Houston) as a novice, self-diagnosed her own Achilles tenosynovitis during training. Co-host James Dodds is a runner/coach who was her patient (foot/bunion surgery rehab).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Credential literacy for choosing a PT: look for a clinical Doctorate of Physical Therapy (now standard) plus a post-doctoral fellowship in orthopedic manual physical therapy (e.g., FAAOMPT or COMPT), which signals additional training in manual joint manipulation/mobilization beyond generalist PT school. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the site likely doesn't cover how to vet/select a PT or what credentials mean)
- Manual therapy defined precisely: joint mobilization/manipulation (sometimes with an audible "pop"/cavitation) to restore mobility to "jammed" joints, distinct from massage/cupping/soft-tissue work which is often mistakenly lumped under "manual therapy." Her training lineage is osteopathic (UK-origin technique via instructor Daryl Herbert), not chiropractic — goal is minimal force for maximum motion restoration. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Manual therapy + targeted exercise are described as "half the equation" each — joint mobilization without movement retraining/strength work, or vice versa, is insufficient. Example: an ankle sprain "jams" the joint; if never manually freed and never re-strengthened, it becomes a chronic weak link for years (host Chris McClung's own high-school ankle sprain still bothers him decades later because it was never rehabbed). (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Two-bucket framework for runners seeking PT: (1) crisis-mode runners 4-6 weeks from a race with acute symptoms, where the top priority is ruling out high-risk injuries (bone stress, major tendon pathology) before clearing them to race; (2) maintenance-mode runners who check in proactively (monthly/every 6 weeks) between injuries to prevent problems. (PRACTICE)
- Concrete strength-training prescription for runners: 2x/week, 30-40 min, heavy/loaded, single-leg-dominant work targeting six muscle groups — core, glutes, hamstrings, quads, soleus, gastrocnemius (e.g., rear-foot-elevated split squats, Copenhagen planks, single-leg hamstring curls, standing/seated calf raises). Explicitly states bodyweight double-leg squats don't load muscles/joints enough because running generates 3-5x bodyweight in ground reaction force per single-leg landing. Recommends NOT loading heavy legs the day before a long run or speed workout (e.g., lift Monday/Thursday if long run is Saturday). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strength training for runners — but the specific muscle-group checklist, single-leg-loading rationale tied to 3-5x bodyweight force, and lift/run scheduling advice may be a useful specific addition)
- Progressive-overload/regression ladder methodology for building strength from zero: e.g., glutes progress from double-leg bridge → staggered bridge → single-leg glute bridge → elevated hip thrust → weighted hip thrust → Smith-machine single-leg hip thrust; program should be re-escalated roughly every 6-8 weeks as muscles adapt (add sets, add load, or shift to a heavy/light split for ultra-distance athletes doing higher-rep endurance-strength days). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — strength training)
- "3 out of 10 pain rule" for running through minor injury symptoms: it's okay to keep running if pain stays around a 3/10 and doesn't escalate during the run; stop if it climbs to 6-8/10 or worsens the longer you run. Distinguishes injury-type by pattern: tendon pain often gets WORSE the next morning and doesn't resolve with a few days off (load/consistency-sensitive — doesn't like sudden changes in mileage/plyometrics); nerve pain doesn't like speed work or hills and tends to persist for very long periods (she notes chronic "plantar fasciitis" self-diagnoses lasting years usually have a nerve component); bone stress pain concentrates to one localized point, is achy, worsens with hopping, and follows a recent spike in training load. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — presented as clinical pattern-recognition, not a cited study) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a genuinely useful diagnostic framework a runner could self-apply, distinct from general "listen to your body" advice)
- Explicit, repeated claim: self-diagnosing running injuries via Google is essentially useless ("zero chance you're getting good information from Google if you have a running injury") — both guest and host agree even soft-tissue differentials (nerve vs. tendon vs. muscle in the same body region) are hard for expert clinicians to tease apart in real time. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back against DIY internet self-diagnosis culture)
- Case example: a new-to-running-group athlete self-diagnosed posterior tibial tendonitis via Google; PT exam correctly suspected navicular bone stress instead; imaging confirmed bilateral navicular stress reactions (one asymptomatic). Root-caused to inadequate fueling while cutting weight/body composition during first half-marathon build — illustrates RED-S-type mechanism (under-fueling → hormonal/bone-density risk → bone stress) surfacing through a PT visit. (SCIENCE — real case, not a named study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — RED-S/under-fueling — though the specific navicular bone-stress case and PT-to-RD referral pathway is a good concrete illustration)
- Previous bone stress injury is one of the single biggest risk factors for a future bone stress injury — not just training load. Recommends a holistic recheck (hormones/menstrual status, fueling, sleep, calcium/bone density, supplementation) for anyone with injury history, not just "ramp mileage back gradually." (SCIENCE, though presented as clinical experience/asserted rather than citing a specific study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "history of bone stress = highest risk factor" framing plus the multi-factor root-cause checklist could be a useful, currently-missing addition alongside existing RED-S content)

(PRACTICE/SCIENCE — physiologically grounded but not citing a specific study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the sitting-posture-driven anterior/posterior hip imbalance mechanism and the activation-vs-strengthening distinction is a specific, actionable framework that may not be on the site)

(PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general biomechanics/injury framing, but worth checking depth of coverage)

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core framing: "you have to detrain in order to retrain to a higher place" — fitness is not linear; a post-peak-race valley (deliberate short-term detraining) is necessary to reach a higher peak later, not just an unfortunate side effect to minimize. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/tapering — but the explicit "must detrain to retrain higher" framing plus the parasympathetic/sympathetic nervous-system framing may add nuance)

(PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the site may cover physical recovery/tapering more than this structured mental post-race reset protocol)

- Specific recovery timeframes stated as rules of thumb: full body recovery from a half marathon takes ~2 weeks (~1 day per mile); full marathon recovery takes ~3 weeks — continuing beneath the surface even after soreness resolves. (SCIENCE — asserted, no study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery)
- Volume-rebuild rule of thumb: don't increase weekly mileage by more than 4-5 miles/week when rebuilding after the reset window; sequence is add days → then distance → then speed (starting speed reintroduction with strides, not full workouts). (SCIENCE — physiological mechanisms named generically, no specific study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base/volume)
- "More is more" #2 — training variety across distances: recommends a 12-18 month rotation of one marathon cycle, one half-marathon cycle, and one 5K/10K cycle (not race-day dabbling but full dedicated training blocks) to avoid plateauing from over-specializing in one distance. Also cites Des Linden's memoir describing repetitive, non-varied Hansons-style marathon cycles (same structure, just more volume/faster paces each time) as contributing to overtraining and a thyroid issue. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — named athletes/anecdotes, not peer-reviewed studies) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "train other distances to get better at your primary distance" principle plus the Des Linden overtraining/thyroid anecdote as a cautionary tale on lack-of-variety-induced overtraining)

- "More is more" #4 — fueling: recommends erring toward MORE food intake in the 90-minute post-workout window (carbs+fats+protein) for both muscle repair and glycogen resynthesis, framing post-run carb timing as more important than pre-run carb-loading. ## Episode #344: The Truth about Zone 2 Training
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Solo episode, host Chris McClung only — no guest.

**Concepts & claims:**

- Key myth-busting claim #1: Zone 2 training is real and effective, but is systematically UNDER-dosed in popular advice — claims most social-media guidance ("do 3-4 zone 2 sessions a week for 45-60 min") is too little; argues meaningful longevity/fitness benefit requires more frequent (5-7x/week) and longer (1-2+ hour) easy sessions. (SCIENCE/DISAGREEMENT — asserted opinion pushing back on popularized advice volume, not citing a specific study) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit critique of how much zone-2 volume is "enough" vs. common shorthand advice)
- Key myth-busting claim #2: Zone 1 is neglected in the "zone 2" discourse but may be equally or more important — described as primarily a recovery zone (promotes blood flow/healing between hard days) but also contributes an aerobic-development ripple effect on higher zones. Introduces the physiological claim that resting/entering zone 1 can actually DROP blood lactate below resting baseline levels, which has an "anti-aging" and lactate-processing benefit for higher-zone performance. Also mentions a "zone zero" (walking-effort) concept attributed to coach Alan Cousins, with claimed fat-oxidation/metabolic benefits. (SCIENCE — lactate/blood-lactate mechanism described in some physiological detail but without naming a specific paper; zone-zero concept attributed to a named coach's assertion) (POSSIBLE GAP — zone 1 vs. zone 2 distinction and the "zone zero"/walking-effort concept seem like a genuinely underserved angle relative to typical zone-2-focused content)
- Key myth-busting claim #3: Most runners doing "zone 2" training are actually drifting into zone 3 — the "no man's land" that's too hard for full recovery/aerobic-ripple benefit but not hard enough for real lactate-threshold adaptation. Gives concrete numeric examples using the 220-minus-age max-HR formula and 60-70% zone-2 ceiling (e.g., age 40 → zone 2 ceiling ≈126 bpm) and states from coaching experience that "very few people" doing easy/long runs on Strava stay below these thresholds — most cheat toward the high end. ## Episode #345: Managing Sickness and Training
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Solo episode, host Chris McClung only — no guest. (Brief personal aside: host's father-in-law passed away from leukemia-treatment complications; not substantive training content, excluded below.)

**Concepts & claims:**
- Reframe: training through sickness is a lose-lose — you can't productively absorb/adapt to training stress while sick (body's resources are diverted to immune response), AND continuing to train risks extending the illness. Mindset shift recommended: taking the day off should be seen as "gaining" recovery time, not "losing" fitness. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery — but the explicit two-part rationale (can't absorb training + risk of prolonging illness) is a clean, reusable framing)
- Hard-and-fast rule: never train with a fever of any kind, even a low-grade fever, and even if reduced back to "normal" via Advil/Tylenol — medicating away a fever doesn't clear you to train. (PRACTICE)
- Data-driven sickness thresholds using wearables: resting heart rate elevated 10+ bpm above personal baseline (especially overnight/morning) = sign of oncoming or ongoing illness, don't train; HRV (heart rate variability) dropped to 50% or more below normal baseline = red flag, wait until HRV recovers to at least ~75% of baseline before resuming; absent wearables, a phone-camera pulse app or manual pulse check can substitute. Also weighs in subjective "how do you feel" as a fourth, non-data signal — when in doubt, don't train. (SCIENCE — physiologically reasonable HRV/RHR sickness markers, presented as the host's coaching rules of thumb, not citing a specific study) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific quantified RHR/HRV thresholds for "too sick to train" is a concrete, actionable framework not typically covered in general recovery content)
- COVID-specific caution: recommends an extra 3 days off training even after RHR/HRV/fever/symptom criteria all normalize, because COVID (even mild/asymptomatic cases) can cause lingering cardiac/respiratory effects beyond symptom resolution. Cites an unnamed pulmonologist ("Dr. Shafar," a podcast super-listener/virtual-group member involved in COVID research) stating early COVID strains took an average of ~79 days for resting heart rate to normalize, versus flu's average ~5 days of RHR elevation. (SCIENCE — a named individual's research is referenced, though not a formal citation — treat as a claim to verify) (POSSIBLE GAP — COVID's specific and prolonged cardiovascular effect on returning athletes, with the 79-day RHR-normalization figure, is a specific and probably-missing data point)
- Five-step sickness protocol: (1) full stop on training while acutely sick — no exceptions; (2) hydrate and fuel well with whole, anti-inflammatory foods (processed/inflammatory food adds an extra burden during illness) even if appetite drops; (3) very gentle movement only once you're up for it (casual walking, gentle mobility/yoga) — nothing that raises heart rate — to aid circulation/mood without taxing recovery; (4) watch heart rate carefully on return, keeping early return-to-run efforts in zone 1 (often under 120-130 bpm), including walking if needed to keep HR down; (5) build back based on days missed — 2-3 days off: return near 100% with a check-in test run; ~1 week off: 75% volume week 1 (no speed work), 100% week 2; ~2 weeks off: 50%/75%/100% over 3 weeks; 3+ weeks off: restart essentially from a base-building phase, likely necessitating giving up the target race. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery/return-to-training progressions generally exist on running sites, but this specific illness-duration-tiered return schedule is a concrete, reusable protocol)
