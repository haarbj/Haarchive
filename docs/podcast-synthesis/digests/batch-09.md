## Episode #197: When to Press and When to Rest
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode hosted by Chris McClung, founder/head coach of Rogue Running (Austin, TX), ~20 years running experience, coaches recreational through sub-elite athletes.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core framing: "rest" doesn't have to mean a full day off — it can mean a shorter/easier run instead of a scheduled medium-long or workout, still promoting blood flow and healing without adding taxing stress. (PRACTICE)
- Doping side-effect on clean athletes: relayed from a conversation with Brad Stolberg (Clean Sport Collective podcast) — the harm of doping isn't just that cheaters win undeservedly, but that clean athletes see dopers recovering "supernaturally" fast and try to match that training load, leading to injury/burnout because the human body (undoped) has a real, tighter stress-rest ceiling. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT-adjacent — an argument for why "just work harder" advice is dangerous) (POSSIBLE GAP — doping-and-training-culture angle is not typical evergreen running-science content)
- Analogy: weightlifters intuitively know not to bench press or curl the same muscle group daily (stress/rest cycling), but runners often fail to apply the same logic to running volume/intensity. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery principle, but the specific weightlifting analogy may be a fresh framing device)
- Host's personal anecdote: stress fracture training for his first marathon, and a multi-year 10K plateau (chronically running same 38:xx despite hard training) attributed to chronically imbalanced stress/rest, not fitness. (PRACTICE)
- Season-level rule #1: early in a training block (i.e., more than ~6 weeks out from marathon, 5 from half, 3-4 from 10K, 2-4 from 5K), always default to the conservative choice — fewer reps, easier pace, less mileage — both to avoid injury and to avoid peaking too early. Save "riding the edge" for the defined peak window. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization basics, but specific window guidance is concrete/actionable)
- Season-level rule #2: if you have a full 7-day stretch where every single run feels bad/sluggish (not just a couple of off days), that's a hard signal to back off for a cycle rather than push through, because "there's no such thing as a perfect training cycle" and catching fatigue early salvages the season. (PRACTICE)
- Season-level rule #3: three consecutive runs with lingering pain (not ordinary soreness) is the threshold for intervention — modify volume/pace/reps and consider prehab before it becomes a full injury. References host's own heel injury, which he attributes to ignoring this exact rule too long. (PRACTICE)
- Season-level rule #4: missing more than 4 consecutive days of training (illness, life events) requires a deliberate, gradual rebuild (easy run first, conservative next workout) rather than jumping straight back into full training; missing 2-3 days generally doesn't require this. (PRACTICE)
- Within-workout framework: know the *purpose* of a given workout before deciding how many reps to do. Turnover/speed-development workouts (big rest, focus on neuromuscular recruitment) should be stopped well before fatigue-driven form breakdown, since going into the "red" defeats the workout's purpose; aerobic-strength workouts intentionally embrace some fatigue and pressing through it is more the point. (PRACTICE)
- The "one more rep" rule: workouts should generally end while the athlete still feels capable of one more rep at pace — a practical, teachable heuristic for gauging whether to add or cut reps. (PRACTICE)
- Concrete stop signals within a workout, given in order: (1) form breaking down (hunched shoulders, leaning, arms/legs "out of whack"), (2) pace trending the wrong direction due to fatigue (not a one-off lapse in focus), (3) complete lack of motivation/desire mid-workout that isn't resolved once moving (distinct from normal pre-workout reluctance) — treated as a possible signal of accumulating fatigue or stress the athlete isn't consciously aware of. (PRACTICE)
- Recommends executing most workout reps "in progression" (start slightly slower than target, dial in, finish slightly faster) rather than flat pace throughout — aids warm-up/injury prevention, pace-feel calibration, and reinforces negative-split race execution habit. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — negative splitting, but the "progression within a single workout" mechanic as a teaching tool is a specific, actionable framing)
- Overarching decision rule: "when in doubt, back off" — a conservative choice is never wrong because it preserves long-term health/eligibility to train, whereas overreaching risks burnout or injury that removes the athlete from the sport entirely. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #199: Periodize Your Strength Training with Travis Goyeneche
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Travis Goyeneche — strength and movement coach based in Austin, TX (Of The Lion Fitness, True Grind Systems); degree in exercise sports science; trains Olympic-level track and field athletes including Mike Rogers (US 4x100m relay gold medalist, Doha 2019 Worlds); collaborator with Rogue Running on both their base-training strength program and new in-season strength program.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core philosophy: train movement patterns, not muscles — the body should be coached as an integrated unit (squat, hinge, push, pull, carry, and combinations like crawling) rather than isolating muscle groups, because isolation training doesn't transfer to real athletic output. Framed around how infants move (unconscious, whole-body, efficient) as the "natural" baseline humans lose. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY — baby crawling as the model of natural, efficient movement) (POSSIBLE GAP — most running-education content covers "strength training for runners" generically; explicit movement-pattern-vs-muscle-isolation philosophy, with named foundational patterns, is a more specific/actionable frame)
- Recommends returning to fundamental movement patterns (squat, hinge, push, pull, carry) at the start of every off-season/reset period, even for elite/veteran athletes — because the body adapts collagen/tissue stiffness specifically to the narrow range of motion used in one's dominant sport pattern (e.g., a runner's stride), and needs periodic "resetting" to regain full range before loading it further. (SCIENCE — asserted physiological mechanism, no named study; presented as practiced clinical experience) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Two core self-assessable movement screens for runners: (1) bodyweight squat — checking "loading order" (hips back first, then knee bend, torso stays vertical, heels stay down) rather than compensatory patterns (knees cave, heels rise, back rounds); (2) hinge (like an RDL) — hips reach back with flat feet, minimal knee bend, watching for rounded shoulders/poor postural strength. Both used to identify "limiters" (e.g., ankle mobility) before they show up as running injuries or performance plateaus, since the body will unconsciously compensate around them until the compensation itself breaks down. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete, teachable self-screening protocol most running-education content doesn't cover in this operational detail)
- Anecdote/analogy: uneven tire wear on a car as a metaphor for compensatory movement patterns — if one part absorbs disproportionate load due to a limiter elsewhere, it wears out and creates a costly problem ("axle" = bigger injury). (ANALOGY)
- Four-pillar periodized in-season strength structure for a ~24-week cycle: (1) movement (smallest block, light added load), (2) strength (~8 weeks, hypertrophy/tendon-strength focus), (3) power (~8 weeks, faster/ballistic movements — kettlebell swings, jumps, throws), (4) sport-specific (single-leg work at faster tempos mimicking running mechanics, e.g., 2-second step-ups become explosive pop-ups) — then a taper into competition. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — periodized strength training specifically synced to a running training calendar, with named phase lengths and exercise examples, is a level of specificity distance-running sites often lack even if they cover "strength training" generally)
- In-season strength dosing: drops to 2x/week (from 3x/week in off-season base-building), roughly 50-60% of off-season exercise volume, ~45 minutes per session (warm-up + ~4 strength exercises + core + mobility). Should be scheduled on non-long-run, non-hard-running days when possible (e.g., run Mon/Wed/Fri/Sat, strength Tue/Thu) to avoid "double-dipping" stress on the same day as a key running session. (PRACTICE)
- Upper body/posture matters directly to running mechanics, not just "vanity": kyphotic (rounded-forward) shoulder posture and "tech neck" can tilt the pelvis, which increases resting tension on the hamstrings, causing chronic tightness; a weak/tight lat (which spans armpit to hip) affects pelvic alignment and shoulder stability during arm drive. (SCIENCE — anatomical mechanism asserted from coaching experience, not a cited study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific lat-to-pelvis-to-hamstring chain and posture-driven hamstring tightness is a more granular mechanistic claim than typical "don't skip upper body" advice)
- Single-leg/unilateral training (step-ups, lunges) builds "unilateral loading" capacity and proprioception, directly addressing the fact that running is essentially a repeated single-leg balancing task (~1,000+ single-leg landings per run); if stabilizing muscles around the hip/knee don't do their job, joints absorb the load instead, contributing to knee pain. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE hybrid, asserted not study-cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general "runners need single-leg strength work" is probably covered, but the direct proprioception/unilateral-loading rationale may add depth)
- Simple, accessible equipment recommendation: kettlebells + dumbbells + a step/box (park bench works) covers most needs; pull-up bar/rings/TRX as a nice-to-have; cloth hip-circle resistance bands for glute activation drills (preferred over rubber bands, which pull leg hair). (PRACTICE)
- Taper approach for strength (final weeks before a race): keep the same routine/frequency but cut volume roughly in half (fewer sets/reps, lighter or bodyweight loads), preserving movement quality/"purity" while reducing physical stress — mirrors the coach's running-taper philosophy of keeping routine constant while dropping volume/intensity, to avoid destabilizing the athlete with novel stimuli close to race day. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — tapering, but this is the strength-training-specific taper protocol, which is more niche)
- Entry point guidance: an already-active, pain-free runner doesn't need to complete the "movement fundamentals" program before starting the in-season strength program, since the in-season program itself starts with a movement-focused block; movement fundamentals work is recommended but not a strict prerequisite unless pain is present. (PRACTICE)
- Emphasized principle carried over from the running side: athletes should self-regulate — "when in doubt, do less rather than more," stay within their own progression lane, use a fallback hierarchy of exercise difficulty (e.g., drop from single-leg box jumps down to a reverse lunge with knee drive, down to a basic squat) if a given exercise variant feels beyond current capacity. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #20: Overcoming Limits
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Steve (Rogue Running co-host; former University of Texas collegiate coach; his own high-school/college racing background is discussed). Fifth installment in an ongoing mental-training series (see also episodes 6, 11, 13, 18).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Note: roughly the first third of the transcript is race-recap/current-events banter (Capital 10K results, 2017 London Marathon results, doping speculation about Mary Keitany, Kipchoge/Bekele history, Nike Breaking2 preview) — discarded as noise per instructions.
- Tool #1 (carried from prior episode, briefly extended): visualization is useful not just for full-race prep but in smaller doses before any single hard workout — mentally rehearsing what's coming. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — visualization is a standard sports-psych tool, though episode 18 (not in this batch) covers it more fully)
- Tool #2: "Problem-Solving [Motherfucker]" (PSMOFO) technique — roughly a week before a race, write down (by hand) one specific anticipated problem in each of three distinct phases: (1) logistics (travel/hotel/expo, from getting on the plane to race start), (2) race execution/strategy (something during the race itself), (3) "the devil on your shoulder" — the specific negative self-talk expected in the final miles (e.g., "my legs go heavy at mile 20 and I can't close"). Athletes are told NOT to solve the problems immediately — they sit with the written problem for days, then solve/write out the solution on the plane to the race, then symbolically tear up and flush the paper before landing. Coach deliberately doesn't ask to see the actual problems/solutions — the point is building general problem-solving capacity and reducing pre-race anxiety, not solving that literal scenario. Used with both age-group marathoners and elite short-distance athletes (who reportedly show disproportionately more nervousness relative to race length). (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY — "wrestling with the angel," biblical Jacob reference, for the devil-on-shoulder phase) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific structured, three-phase, write-then-destroy pre-race ritual is a concrete, actionable technique distinct from generic "visualize positively" advice)
- Practical example for "devil on shoulder" moments mid-race: physically change something for 30 seconds (cadence, pace) rather than only trying to out-think the negative spiral — a physical circuit-breaker, not just a mental one. (PRACTICE)
- Tool #3: Resilience training, explicitly borrowed from Nassim Taleb's concept of "antifragility" (book: Antifragile, 2012) and from the U.S. Army's post-2006/2007 Comprehensive Soldier Fitness "Resilience Training" program (developed with University of Pennsylvania, built on positive psychology — the guest states he is personally skeptical of positive psychology as a field but finds this application useful). (SCIENCE — named source/book/program, though application to running is the coach's own extrapolation, not a cited running study) (POSSIBLE GAP — Taleb/antifragility and military resilience-training as a direct source for a running mental-training framework is a genuinely distinct citation base from typical sports-psych content)
- Resilience training = deliberately, *voluntarily* choosing hard/uncomfortable training scenarios (heat, unusual start times, no nutrition on long runs) so the athlete has already "been there" mentally and physically before race day. Key caveat stated explicitly: this only "counts" if voluntarily chosen — showing up to a regularly scheduled 5:30am hot-weather group run because it's on the calendar does not build resilience credit; deliberately choosing an extra-hard, self-selected challenge does. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the idea that just "showing up" to hard scheduled training is equivalent to resilience-building)
- Concrete resilience-training example: an annual (fall-only) 30-mile training run with zero nutrition before Boston prep — stated rationale is ~15% physiological (training fat-oxidation capacity) and ~85% psychological (deliberately inducing the "dark place"/impaired cognition state — inability to do math, losing track of pace/strategy — that athletes may hit late in a marathon, so they've already practiced functioning through it). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE hybrid — physiological rationale asserted, psychological rationale explicit and primary) (POSSIBLE GAP — a deliberately depleted, no-fuel 30-mile simulation run specifically for psychological rehearsal of cognitive impairment is a distinctive, fairly extreme practice not likely covered under generic "fueling" or "long run" content)
- Related example: intentionally sending an athlete out too fast in a build/tune-up race (beyond what's "smart") to force them to problem-solve blowing up in real time, on the theory that being forced into the deep end reveals coping mechanisms they wouldn't otherwise discover. (PRACTICE — coach notes this is used "carefully")
- Resilience also has a reactive/rebound component, not just proactive hardening — "rule of three" example: if you're 1 minute ahead at the marathon half, you may end up 3 minutes behind by the finish; a resilient athlete's mental model is "maybe I can be the one who breaks that rule" / "the cake fell, but I can still make a good dessert" rather than catastrophizing. (PRACTICE) (ANALOGY — "the cake fell" / dessert-salvage framing)
- Co-host's personal story: as a high schooler who "choked" (faked a fall) at a state cross-country meet, then committed to doing 3 sets of 30 pull-ups every single night for ~2-3 years (through high school graduation) as a self-imposed, running-unrelated discipline exercise, crediting this directly with the mental toughness that let him win a state 3200m title later — used to argue that resilience-building disciplines don't need to be running-specific; any voluntarily sustained hard commitment builds transferable grit. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — cross-domain/non-running discipline-building as deliberate mental training is a distinct angle from standard sports psychology content)
- Related anecdote (secondhand, about marathoner Dick Beardsley, per the host): reportedly punched bruises into his own quads between runs while training for the 1982 Boston Marathon "Duel in the Sun" against Alberto Salazar, partly for a claimed physiological rationale (toughening for downhill pounding) but mainly as a self-belief/toughness ritual. (PRACTICE — presented as folklore/anecdote, host caveats "apparently")
- Tool #4: "Overcoming limits" — philosophical framing that most self-perceived limits are self-imposed or environmentally imposed stories rather than real physiological ceilings; process is to (1) articulate the big dream without pre-filtering by perceived limits, (2) write down concrete things believed to be blocking that goal, (3) triage into "low-hanging fruit" (sleep, diet, training consistency — things within personal control) vs. harder structural issues, and systematically knock down the manageable ones first. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly pushes back on the idea that life circumstances like having kids/a busy job are valid permanent excuses for not improving, reframing them as "convenient stories"/self-victimization)
- Named example used to illustrate "limits are often stories, not physiology": Paula Radcliffe's 2:15:25 marathon world record (set in the early 2000s, still discussed as potentially doping-clouded but framed here primarily as an example of ignoring perceived physiological limits). (SCIENCE-adjacent claim, but used rhetorically rather than as a scientific citation)
- Host's personal example: after a Lululemon-sponsored goal-setting session asked "why not me?", he realized he'd built a self-limiting narrative around post-third-child/business-owner busyness being a permanent excuse for not getting faster; fixing one concrete constraint (missed Monday runs) by recruiting an accountability running partner led to PRs at every distance from 10K to marathon the following year. (PRACTICE)
- Explicit recommendation to seek professional help (sports psychologist or general therapist) once self-guided "low-hanging fruit" work is exhausted and deeper psychological blocks remain — paired with a stated general skepticism of sports psychologists as a field ("frequently opportunistic," "not practical enough"), but an explicit carve-out that they matter for the harder, non-low-hanging-fruit work. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — qualified pushback on sports psychology as typically practiced) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sports psychology / mental training generally, though the antifragility/military-resilience sourcing and the specific PSMOFO ritual are less likely to already be covered)

## Episode #201: Training by Effort
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode by Chris McClung (Rogue Running head coach).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central thesis: effort is the "true" training variable; pace and heart rate are merely proxies that can diverge significantly from actual physiological effort (e.g., same pace in very different heat/humidity conditions produces very different effort/aerobic stimulus — concrete example given: 8:00/mile at 50°F/50% humidity vs. 80°F/85% humidity). Practical implication: don't be "a slave to the watch," especially on easy days. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — mechanism widely accepted in exercise science, though no specific study cited; presented as coaching principle) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — effort vs. pace training is a fairly standard concept, though the specific "3-4 minutes slower than marathon pace on hot days is fine" framing may be a useful concrete anchor)
- Full 8-zone effort model (host's own categorization, explicitly not universal — notes other coaches use 5-7 zones), organized under 3 broader categories:
  - Aerobic capacity (2 zones): Zone 1 "active recovery" (≥2 min/mile slower than marathon pace, sometimes 3-4 min slower; goal is finishing feeling better than you started; fully conversational) and Zone 2 "easy run" (roughly 1-2 min/mile slower than MP; the main aerobic-capacity-building pace; also fully conversational; should also leave you feeling as good or better than when you started).
  - Aerobic strength (3 zones): Zone 3 "steady"/aerobic threshold (roughly the effort sustainable for ~2 hours, between marathon and half-marathon effort — the point lactate begins accumulating but is still manageable/usable as fuel); Zone 4 "tempo" (~half-marathon pace/effort — the "sweet spot" where lactate is created but can still be efficiently used/cleared; called out again as the single most broadly prescribed workout type); Zone 5 "lactate threshold" (roughly one-hour race effort, ~10K-to-half-marathon pace — the point past which lactate accumulation outpaces clearance and starts a "spiral"; typically trained in 10-30 minute continuous chunks).
  - Speed and power (3 zones): Zone 6 "VO2 max" (roughly 3K-to-10K pace, sustainable ~2-3 miles, maximal oxygen utilization before going anaerobic); Zone 7 "anaerobic capacity/threshold" (short, fast, high-rest efforts training the body's ability to do glycolytic/non-oxygen-dependent work — noted as most relevant for 400m/800m-type athletes, used sparingly by distance runners, mainly late-cycle for finishing-kick work); Zone 8 "neuromuscular power" (term credited to cycling coach Andy Coggan — very short, all-out efforts like build-up 100m sprints or short hill reps with large recovery, aimed at "grooving new neuromuscular pathways"/muscle fiber recruitment rather than aerobic stimulus per se). (SCIENCE/PRACTICE hybrid — physiological zone boundaries are standard exercise-science concepts; the specific 8-zone taxonomy, naming, and "steady" terminology are the host's own synthesis) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training zones/lactate threshold/VO2max are almost certainly covered by an existing site, but this specific 8-zone breakdown with named sub-zones like "steady"/aerobic threshold as distinct from tempo, and the explicit anaerobic-capacity vs. neuromuscular-power split, may add useful granularity)
- Named concept: "aerobic no-man's-land" around marathon goal pace (MGP) — running too much volume specifically at MGP effort lands between the aerobic-capacity-building zone and the aerobic-threshold/strength-building zone, accomplishing neither goal efficiently; MGP work should mostly be reserved for race-specific pace-familiarization sessions, not general aerobic development, to avoid "wasting miles." (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — implicitly pushes back on the common approach of running lots of miles at goal marathon pace during training) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "MGP no-man's-land" caution is a more nuanced/counterintuitive point than generic zone-training content typically includes)
- Analogy: aerobic capacity = "size of the engine" (adding cylinders via more mitochondria, capillaries, red blood cells, lung capacity — all developed via easy, high-volume, low-intensity running), aerobic strength/speed work = "tuning" that engine once built; argues bigger engine > finely tuned engine for endurance events, so easy-mileage volume should dominate. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — but a clean, reusable teaching analogy)
- Cites Matt Fitzgerald's book *80/20 Running* explicitly to support the claim that ~80% of training should occur in the two aerobic-capacity zones (easy/recovery), reinforcing a strength-training parallel: "higher reps, lower weight" (more volume, lower intensity) most of the time. (SCIENCE — named author/book cited directly) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — 80/20 principle is probably already covered)
- Physiological explanation of the "stomach flipping"/nausea sensation near anaerobic threshold: attributed to the body shunting blood away from the GI tract toward working muscles to maximize oxygen delivery as it nears its aerobic ceiling. (SCIENCE — asserted mechanism, not source-cited) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific, concrete physiological explanation for a commonly-experienced but rarely-explained race-day symptom)
- Five practical tips for effort-based training discipline: (1) do occasional truly "watchless" runs, or runs with the watch face covered (e.g., duct tape) while still logging data, to force feel-based pacing; (2) for track/interval workouts, use lap-split timing rather than real-time GPS "instant pace" (explicitly called "incredibly inaccurate" for interval work, especially on a track where GPS struggles with curves); (3) analyze pace data only *after* a workout is complete, correlating it retrospectively with how effort felt, rather than reacting to live per-mile splits mid-effort; (4) run almost every workout/race in progression (deliberately start slightly conservative, build to target, finish slightly faster) both to dial in effort and to avoid early-workout overcooking; (5) give yourself a pace range (e.g., ±5-10 seconds around a target) rather than a fixed number, since a specific pace is a proxy and conditions vary day to day. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific mechanics of "duct tape over the watch," lap-split-only tracking on the track, and after-the-fact-only data analysis are concrete, actionable behavioral techniques not commonly spelled out this explicitly)

## Episode #202: Total Stress Load + Stress Inventory

## Episode #203: The Practical Side of Dealing with Injury
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Sherri — a listener from Tallahassee, FL; recreational marathoner (four marathons completed, 4:28 PR), not a coach or medical professional; brought on to have her real injury situation coached live on air by Chris McClung as a teaching example.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Diagnostic framing for groin/hip pain in distance runners specifically: host states that true groin strains/tears are typically sprint- or power-sport injuries, and that groin-area pain in a distance runner is more often referred from one of four other sources — adductor tightness, a bulging lumbar disc (referred pain), irritation of the obturator nerve (runs from core/abdomen down the leg), or pelvic malalignment/pelvic bone stress (worst case). Explicitly recommends ruling out a specific alignment issue given the athlete's recent fall on a hiking trip. (SCIENCE — presented as pattern-recognition from coaching experience and injuries observed over years, explicitly caveated "I'm not a doctor, but I am a running coach" — not citing a specific study) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific differential list for runner's groin pain is a concrete, actionable diagnostic framework more specific than generic "see a doctor for pain" advice)
- Practitioner-selection guidance: emphasizes finding a practitioner (MD, PT, chiropractor, DO, massage therapist, ART practitioner) who specifically understands *running* injuries and overuse/performance issues, as distinct from general medical training, which is oriented toward illness rather than performance-related musculoskeletal issues; recommends building a "team" of such practitioners over time and sourcing referrals via running clubs/run specialty stores. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general "see a specialist" advice, though the running-specific practitioner-network framing adds some specificity)
- Core operating rule for training through injury: "if it doesn't hurt you, it's typically okay" — pain (not fatigue or discomfort in general) should be the primary guide for what to modify or avoid, applied to strength, yoga, and running alike; avoid painkillers as a way to dull the signal since pain is functioning as diagnostic information. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general "listen to pain" advice, common in running-injury content)
- Specific numeric pain-tolerance rule for continuing to run while managing an injury: pain ≤3 out of 10 that does NOT worsen during the run is generally an acceptable threshold to keep running through (with the caveat that this should be confirmed with a practitioner); above a 3, or pain that escalates during a run, should mean stopping/backing off. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the explicit numeric pain-scale threshold, ≤3/10 non-worsening, is a specific actionable rule of thumb beyond generic "listen to your body")
- Additional caveat surfaced through the listener's own question (notable because it complicates the pain-scale rule above): if an athlete notices they are *compensating* in their running form to keep pain manageable (e.g., favoring one leg, which then causes new pain elsewhere — in this case the opposite knee), that's a sign to stop running even if raw pain intensity is still below the "3" threshold, because compensation patterns create secondary injuries via a "trickle-down effect." Host shares his own recent example: running through a heel nerve issue caused compensatory form changes that then caused a new knee problem. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT-adjacent — a meaningful caveat/refinement to the numeric pain-threshold rule stated moments earlier, showing these podcast rules of thumb are not absolute) (POSSIBLE GAP — compensation-pattern awareness as a distinct, secondary check beyond raw pain scoring is a nuanced, specific addition)
