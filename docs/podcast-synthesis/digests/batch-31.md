## Episode #382: Back to the Basics with James Dodds
**Guest(s) and credentials:** James Dodds — Rogue Running coach, introduced here as the podcast's new permanent co-host. Came to Rogue as a beginner runner (first marathon ~5:00) and improved to a 3:36 marathon and sub-40 10K under Rogue's system; now coaches Rogue's "Rogue Rising" virtual group and one-on-one athletes. Chris McClung — host, Rogue Running founder/head coach, co-author of the new book "Running the Rogue Way." This episode is a reflective, off-the-cuff conversation (new co-host format launch), not yet the structured book walk-through of later episodes.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Peaking is the training principle athletes most often claim to believe but don't actually live out. The tell: 6–8 weeks before an A-race, a hard workout goes poorly and the athlete panics ("if marathon pace for 2 miles felt that hard, how can I hold it for 26?") — which reveals they've mentally discarded the concept of tapering/peaking from their own reasoning. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — tapering/peaking itself, but the specific psychological failure pattern is a finer-grained angle)
- "C-race" concept: athletes should have only 2–3 max "A" races per year and use lower-stakes "C" races to practice without judging themselves on outcome. Fully embracing a C race means accepting whatever happens without letting it update your belief about your A-race potential. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Recommended mental technique: pre-write your response to a bad C-race outcome ahead of time ("I am not allowed to judge myself based on this result") so that when self-doubt ("the devil on your shoulder") shows up afterward, you already have a rehearsed counter-thought. Described as a practiced, repeatable skill, not something that works instantly. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Running by feel is called a "dying art," being displaced by GPS-watch dependence. Training paces named after race distances (e.g. "10K pace") are effort/physiological-development proxies, not literal predictions of race performance — but the naming convention itself misleads athletes into expecting an exact correlation. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific critique of pace-naming conventions is a fairly novel framing)
- Analogy: as safety technology improves (NFL helmets, car airbags/brakes, cushioned running shoes), people behave more recklessly and lose touch with felt risk/effort — applied to GPS watches making runners disconnect from internal effort cues. Presented as a general behavioral pattern (essentially risk compensation / the "Peltzman effect"), asserted without naming a specific study. (ANALOGY / SCIENCE-asserted-without-citation) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Anecdote: an athlete assigned 8x800m at "10K pace" couldn't hit the target splits but insisted the prescribed pace was wrong rather than accepting that his current fitness didn't match his assumed 10K pace — illustrating how attachment to a pace label can override actual physiological feedback. (PRACTICE)
- Explicit distinction: "training paces and race paces don't always match" — a training pace prescription is not a promise of race-day capability, especially depending on where in a training cycle (peaking curve) the athlete currently sits. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)

## Episode #383: Training Fundamentals, Part 1

**Concepts & claims:**
- "Do the work"/consistency is framed as fundamentally different from skill-based expertise domains: running improvement is mostly about physiological adaptation (bone density, capillary density, mitochondrial size/number, lung capacity, red blood cell count) that only comes from sustained time on feet across years/decades, not something you can "test out of" or shortcut with technique alone. (SCIENCE — physiological mechanisms asserted without specific studies cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Claim that visible progress toward a goal (even small steps) triggers dopamine release, reinforcing the training habit loop over time. Asserted generally, no specific study named. (SCIENCE-asserted) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC, though the explicit neuroscience/habit-loop framing may be a finer angle)
- Explicit critique of Runner's World's old "Run Less, Run Faster" program: stripping training down to just a long run and speed workouts (cutting recovery volume) can work short-term especially from a low fitness base, but leads to injury or plateau over time. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — direct pushback against a named commercial training methodology) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Background on Arthur Lydiard: New Zealand coach who in the 1950s self-experimented with ~200 mile weeks (necessarily run very easy) and got faster from volume alone; later coached Olympic medalists in both 1500m/mid-distance and marathon-adjacent events using largely the same volume-based approach. Podcast dates this to "the 52 or 54 Olympics" — this is likely a misremembered date (Lydiard's famous medalists, e.g., Peter Snell and Murray Halberg, actually medaled at the 1960 Rome Olympics) and should be fact-checked before reuse. (SCIENCE/historical claim — flag date inaccuracy) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC, Lydiard is probably already cited, but verify exact facts)
- Lydiard quote (via Dodds): "Miles are like money in the bank." (ANALOGY)
- Specific numeric benchmark: recommends ~2,000 miles/year (40+ mpw sustained across 52 weeks) as a marker of consistency for an everyday athlete aiming to approach their potential — explicitly framed as an aspirational, longer-term target, not a starting point. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete annual-mileage benchmark framed this way is a specific, actionable detail)
- Explicit caveat: "Miles matter, but only as much as you can recover from those miles" — mileage increases must be matched against realistic recovery capacity/life schedule, not pursued as a blanket goal regardless of context. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- "Slow down to get faster" analogized directly to strength training: just as a lifter trains at submaximal weight/higher reps most of the time and only occasionally tests a 1-rep max, a runner should do most miles slow to build the aerobic system and reserve high intensity for a small fraction of training. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly — polarized/easy-running physiology — but the specific strength-training analogy is a quotable teaching device worth preserving verbatim)

- Quality/speed work recommended at roughly 10–20% of total mileage, with 80–90% easy running. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overlaps with 80/20 polarized training)
- Cites Dr. Andy Galpin (named exercise physiologist) on the idea that "one day's worth of data is worthless" — don't judge training success/failure from a single day's performance or feel. (SCIENCE, named source) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC generally, specific framing may be useful)

## Episode #385: Racing Fundamentals, Part 1
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Same hosts/coaches (Dodds & McClung), starting Chapter 2's racing-side principles (first three of six).

**Concepts & claims:**
- "Trust your training": facing race-day uncertainty, deliberately focus the mind on what you do know (you did the mileage, the workouts, the long runs) rather than ruminating on unknowns (what will mile 23 feel like). Framed explicitly as a technique to occupy/quiet an anxious, racing mind. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly under sports psychology, though this specific "focus on the known" mechanism is a usable, concrete technique)
- "There's no such thing as a perfect training cycle — and there shouldn't be": responsively modifying a plan (cutting a run short when sick, overly fatigued, or traveling) is framed as the *correct* behavior (evidence of listening to your body), not a failure to feel guilty about. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — explicit pushback against perfectionist training mindsets) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Focus on what you did do, not what you didn't do": bank-money analogy — if you go into a room with $800 available and come out with $760, you focus on the $760, not the missed $40. Applied to a runner who cut a planned 20-mile long run to 18: focus on the aggregate ~120 miles across all long runs completed, not the 2 miles "missed." (ANALOGY/PRACTICE)
- Trust extends across all prior training cycles/years, not just the current one — an experienced marathoner carries forward resilience and fitness built over a decade of training, so one imperfect recent cycle matters less in the aggregate. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Anything workouts": a coach's term for a cycle's signature hard workouts/long runs that, once completed, become concrete psychological reference points ("if I did that, I can do anything") to draw on for race-day confidence. Practical tip: literally circle these on a training calendar/log and review them before race day. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific, concrete pre-race ritual)
- "Execute the plan": quotes University of Colorado coach Mark Wetmore (via Chris Lear's book "Running with the Buffaloes," which chronicled the Colorado team, including Adam Goucher's collegiate title era), paraphrased: "There are no miracles in running on race day... either you've done the work or you haven't." (PRACTICE/quote — named coach and book source) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC possibly, but the specific citation is a good addition)
- "A bad plan is still better than no plan": encourages even beginners to draft a rough race plan (course notes, open pacing/fueling questions) rather than winging it, since a written plan — even an imperfect one — gives something concrete to iterate on afterward. (PRACTICE)
- General claim: negative-splitting (starting conservatively, building into the race, finishing strong) is optimal for most distance races 5K and up, said to be supported by both physiology and large-scale race-result statistics — asserted broadly here without specifics (data discussed in more depth in Ep #386). (SCIENCE-asserted, broad) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overlaps standard race-pacing advice)

(PRACTICE, named source: Alan Cousins) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Race without fear of failure": cites Brené Brown's point that fear and courage necessarily coexist — courage is the overcoming of fear, not its absence. (SCIENCE/quote, named source: Brené Brown) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly under sports psychology, specific citation may be useful)
- Reframing of "failure": defined explicitly as quitting/giving up altogether, not as missing a goal time — if you learn from a race and try again, it isn't a failure by this definition. (PRACTICE)
- Skateboarding/skimboarding analogy: extreme-sport athletes attempt tricks dozens or hundreds of times per session, failing repeatedly without self-flagellation, because the sheer volume of attempts normalizes failure as part of the process; runners get far fewer "at-bats" per year (races), so should deliberately adopt a similarly failure-tolerant mindset. (ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — a fairly novel framing for distance runners specifically)

## Episode #387: Miles Matter Redux
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Same hosts/coaches (Dodds & McClung), Chapter 3 of the book — a deep dive on training volume specifically.

**Concepts & claims:**
- FVI progression model: build Frequency (number of running days/week) first, then Volume (distance on those days), then Intensity (speed/quality work) — always in that order, whether building from zero or rebuilding after a break/injury/illness. Explicit rule: don't add formal speed workouts until the athlete can complete a 10-mile long run. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly as "progressive overload," but the specific ordered FVI framework and the numeric "10-mile long run" threshold before adding speed work is a concrete, actionable, likely under-covered detail) (POSSIBLE GAP for the specific threshold rule)
- Minimum-frequency guideline: at least 4 running days/week (of a 7-day cycle) recommended as the floor to send a consistent-enough adaptive signal; 5–6 days recommended for half/full marathon training; advanced athletes may add doubles for extra frequency, but this isn't implied for beginners. (PRACTICE)
- Volume-building numeric rules of thumb: don't increase weekly mileage by more than ~10%/week (recommends converting this to concrete numbers rather than literal percentage math — e.g. +2-3 mi/week early on, +3-4 mi/week later); and don't increase peak weekly mileage by more than ~20% from one full training cycle to the next (e.g., 30 mpw peak this cycle → ~36 mpw peak next cycle). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — the 10%/week rule is a widely known heuristic; the 20% cycle-over-cycle rule is a less commonly discussed corollary) (POSSIBLE GAP for the cycle-over-cycle rule specifically)
- Strides defined precisely: ~20 seconds or 80-100m, structured in thirds — build speed (1st third), hold ~85-90% of peak speed (middle third), relaxed float to a stop (final third) — with a full walk-back recovery between reps; 4-6 reps, once per week, recommended for all levels from beginner to advanced. Purpose: keep fast-twitch motor units/neuromuscular firing active without providing an aerobic or anaerobic training stimulus. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC generally — strides are common running advice — but the precise thirds-based execution cue is specific/actionable)
- "Allergy shots for speed" analogy for strides: a single session does nothing on its own, but consistent small weekly doses accumulated over months/years produce lasting neuromuscular/form-efficiency benefits. (ANALOGY)
- Cites Dr. Andy Galpin's "Galpin Snacks" concept — 4x20-second hard efforts once per week, a general (non-running-specific) exercise-science recommendation the hosts map onto running strides. (SCIENCE, named source: Dr. Andy Galpin) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific cross-disciplinary exercise-science reference)
- Cites Coach Alan Cousins' term "a-lactic workouts" ("without lactic acid") to describe strides/short accelerations that are deliberately not intended to create a lactate/anaerobic training stimulus. (SCIENCE terminology, named source: Alan Cousins) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Fast-twitch vs. slow-twitch explainer: elite marathoners such as Eliud Kipchoge, despite running under 5-minute miles for 26.2 miles, are physiologically slow-twitch dominant — "fast/slow twitch" describes muscle fiber recruitment type, not absolute speed capability. (SCIENCE, standard exercise physiology) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Specific numeric pace-offset guidelines relative to goal race pace: normal easy/long-run/medium-long-run days should be at least 1-2 min/mile slower than marathon goal pace (or 1.5-2.5 min/mile slower than half-marathon goal pace); recovery days (the day after a long run or hard workout) should be at least 2-3 min/mile slower than marathon goal pace (or 2.5-3.5 min/mile slower than half-marathon goal pace). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific numeric formula tied directly to goal race pace, more precise than a general "easy = conversational pace" framing)
- "Creation / recycling / destroying" three-tier framework: below aerobic threshold = "creation" (building mitochondria/capillaries); at/around the first physiological inflection point (onset of light lactate accumulation) = "recycling" (buffering lactate, not building new capacity); deep anaerobic effort = "destroying." Presented as a simplified mental model for choosing daily training effort. (ANALOGY/SCIENCE hybrid — simplifies real threshold physiology into a memorable 3-tier metaphor) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC as far as aerobic/lactate threshold physiology goes, but this specific teaching metaphor may be a fresh way to present it) (POSSIBLE GAP for the metaphor itself)
- Introduces "expand your comfort zone" as a deliberate reframe of the generic "get outside your comfort zone" self-help advice — for endurance runners, sustainable long-term growth comes mostly from gradually expanding what volume/effort feels comfortable and recoverable, rather than habitually pushing into discomfort. (DISAGREEMENT — explicit pushback against common self-help framing) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Missed-day philosophy: don't try to "make up" a missed run. Briefly diagnose why it was missed (illness/acute stress = normal exception, no action needed; avoidance/procrastination = a pattern worth addressing), then resume the plan on the next scheduled day. Overall consistency should be judged at ~90-95% adherence, not 100%. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC, general habit-formation advice)
- Personal case study: after ~18 months without formal speed work (during which the host trained purely on easy volume + strides, building to 60-70 mpw over ~12 weeks for an upcoming multi-day trail stage race), he paced an athlete to a half-marathon PR (1:27) despite not having recently trained at that specific goal pace — offered as a live example that pure aerobic-volume training "wakes up" previously-developed speed/paces without dedicated interval work, particularly when there's a training history to draw on. (PRACTICE/anecdote)

## Episode #388: Find Your Peak
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Same hosts/coaches (Dodds & McClung), Chapter 4 of the book — periodization and peaking.

**Concepts & claims:**
- "Expand your comfort zone" (developed fully here, foreshadowed in Ep #387): explicit reframe of the common "get outside your comfort zone to grow" advice — for distance runners, sustainable long-term progress comes from gradually widening the range of volume/effort that feels normal and recoverable (raising the baseline), rather than habitually pushing to discomfort. (DISAGREEMENT/PRACTICE — explicit pushback against generic self-improvement framing) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Default periodization structure (20-week training cycle): Base/Priming phase (build aerobic capacity, prime neuromuscular/fast-twitch recruitment) → Aerobic Strength phase (endurance capacity plus muscular strength via hill work) → Race-Specific phase (race-pace-specific work plus sharper speed) → Taper (pull back volume/intensity to arrive rested). Only in roughly the final 3-4 weeks of the race-specific phase should an athlete be truly training "at the edge"; earlier phases should stay deliberately conservative — explicitly framed as partly a choice, not just a physiological given. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization structure is almost certainly already covered, though the specific 20-week/4-phase Rogue framework and "staying away from the edge is a choice" framing add detail)
