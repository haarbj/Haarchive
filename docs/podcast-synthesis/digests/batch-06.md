## Episode #165: Predictions for 2020 + Injury Tips
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode with host Chris McClung (owner/coach, Rogue Running, Austin TX; competitive marathoner). The "predictions for 2020" segment (shoe regulation, Olympic medal counts, Boston qualifying standards, Kipchoge racing NYC) is pure current-events speculation tied to a specific season and is not extracted. The back half, on managing his own personal injury, is substantive.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Where you feel pain is usually not the root cause of running-related soft-tissue injuries — the pain site is often just the "weak link" that manifests symptoms while the actual dysfunction is elsewhere (his own case: felt like a hamstring strain, but was actually the sciatic nerve becoming impinged/stuck to adjacent fascia, sending errant signals into the hamstring and causing it to lock up). Cites plantar fasciitis as another common example where ankle mobility/kinetic-chain issues upstream are often the real driver. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general injury advice — but the specific nerve-entrapment/fascia mechanism and "nerve glides" as a targeted remedy is a more specific angle) (POSSIBLE GAP for the nerve-glide/fascial-entrapment mechanism specifically)
- Use pain as an active guide during recovery, not just a stop signal: if pain trends better over the course of a run, that's a sign continued movement may be helping; if it trends worse, stop. Emphasizes this requires practitioner guidance and doesn't apply to acute injuries (fractures/tears). (PRACTICE)
- Build a "portfolio" of trusted practitioners (he names a massage therapist, a chiropractor, and a physical therapist he rotates between) before you're injured, so you can triage quickly when something comes up — find people the local running community actually recommends, regardless of formal credential type. (PRACTICE)
- Recovery from soft-tissue/nerve injuries should be an active process (nerve glides, mobility/hip-opening work, targeted strength rehab, pain-free cross-training like the stationary bike), not passive rest — he contrasts this with acute fractures, which may genuinely require inactivity. Frames total inactivity as likely to prolong a soft-tissue injury (scar tissue can further entrap a nerve if left unaddressed). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — mechanism asserted, no study or clinician named)

## Episode #166: 8 Common Training Mistakes
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode with host Chris McClung.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Mistake 1 — Not running enough: recommends a floor of 5 days/week for half-marathoners and 6 days/week for marathoners (except cross-training-heavy athletes like triathletes); argues additional easy days reduce injury risk (more recovery capacity between hard efforts) rather than increasing it, and that added easy volume drives the classic aerobic adaptations (more mitochondria, red blood cells, capillaries, improved O2 uptake) over months/years. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE, mechanism asserted generically) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base/mileage building)
- Mistake 2 — Skipping recovery days, or nominally doing them but running them too fast: gives a very specific numeric heuristic — recovery-day pace can be as much as 2–4 minutes/mile slower than marathon race pace; the test of a good recovery run is finishing feeling better than you started, not hitting a certain pace. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC, but specific pace-differential numbers are a useful concrete addition)
- Mistake 3 — Going too fast all the time (on easy days and inside prescribed workouts): argues this both mistrains the aerobic system (wrong zone) and raises neuromuscular/injury risk; describes his own history of a stress fracture and a multi-year 10K plateau caused by chronic overpacing. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT with the "more effort = better" instinct)
- Mistake 4 — Not varying pace across different types of easy runs: proposes three distinct easy-pace bands relative to marathon (MP) / half-marathon (HMP) target pace — medium-long run: MP+30sec to +1:30/mi (or HMP+1:00 to +2:00); long run: MP+1:00 to +2:00/mi (or HMP+1:30 to +2:30); recovery run: MP+2:00/mi or slower (or HMP+2:30 or slower). Argues lack of variation signals over-focus on pace rather than effort. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP for the specific numeric pace-banding system, even though "vary your easy pace" as a concept is likely covered)
- Mistake 5 — Running marathon pace for entire long runs: claims marathon pace sits in a physiologically "no-man's-land" that is suboptimal for aerobic development when sustained over a full long run, and raises injury risk; recommends confining race-pace running to small, prescribed chunks within a long run (2–4 times per training cycle) rather than extending duration at MP to "prove" race readiness. (SCIENCE — claim asserted without citing a specific study/researcher) (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly pushes back on the common athlete belief that "if I ran 20 miles at goal pace, I can run 26.2 at goal pace")
- Mistake 6 — Being "a slave to your watch": recommends practicing pace-by-feel — e.g., running the first interval of a workout without looking at the watch until it ends, then calibrating, or doing full easy runs without a watch — to build the ability to self-regulate effort without GPS, especially important given GPS pace can be unreliable in cities/canyons. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly, though the specific "don't look until the end of the interval" drill is a concrete technique)
- Mistake 7 — Letting injuries linger until forced to fully stop: reiterates the proactive-triage advice from ep. 165; recommends the book "Running Rewired" (Jay Dicharry) for self-directed prehab/rehab. (PRACTICE)
- Mistake 8 — Not choosing a peak race: argues athletes should periodize around only 2–3 "peak" races per year, with other races explicitly designated as either hard efforts at shorter distances or execution/pacing-practice opportunities feeding the peak, rather than racing frequently without a clear hierarchy. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization/tapering)

## Episode #168: Recover like a Champion
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode with host Chris McClung; references his personal use of an Oura Ring for sleep tracking.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Two distinct purposes of recovery, often conflated: (1) preparing the body for the next hard session, and (2) actual fitness-building itself happens during recovery, not during the stress of the workout — draws a direct analogy to resistance training, where muscle tissue is broken down during the lift and only rebuilds stronger during the rest period; argues runners "get greedy" with recovery pace/effort and short-circuit this consolidation process. (SCIENCE/ANALOGY — bench-press-and-repair analogy) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — supercompensation/adaptation — but the framing may be a useful teaching analogy to reuse)
- Proposes a simplified 3-pillar recovery framework — movement, sleep, food as fuel — as covering "95–98%" of what matters, with other modalities (massage, cryo, float, compression boots) as optional accessories only worth layering in once the three basics are dialed in. (PRACTICE)
- "Motion is lotion for the muscles" — quote attributed to a prior guest, Marilyn Faulkner. "Movement equals blood flow equals healing" — his own paraphrase; explains veins lack the muscular wall arteries have, so movement (not just heart pumping) is needed to return blood from extremities. (ANALOGY/SCIENCE — basic circulatory mechanism, correctly described at a lay level)
- Distinguishes mobility work (joint range of motion) from flexibility/stretching work — cites evidence that stretching is not necessarily beneficial for recovery unless an athlete already has an established stretching routine that makes them feel better, whereas mobility deficits (his example: a poorly rehabbed high-school ankle sprain) can directly contribute to injury elsewhere in the kinetic chain. Recommends "Running Rewired" (Jay Dicharry) for self-administered mobility testing and corrective exercises. (SCIENCE — stretching claim asserted without a named study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the mobility-vs-flexibility distinction and self-testing approach)
- Sleep quantity guidance: target ~8 hours/day or ~56 hours/week in aggregate (allows napping to count toward the weekly total); he personally added 2–3 short (30–45 min) naps/week to boost total sleep. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, general — no specific study named)
- Sleep quality metrics from wearable data (Oura Ring): sleep latency optimal range is 5–15 minutes to fall asleep; sleep efficiency (time actually asleep vs. time in bed) target 85–90%; also tracks "restfulness" (time in deep/REM cycles). (SCIENCE, self-reported consumer-wearable metrics, not peer-reviewed citations) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific, actionable sleep-quality benchmarks)
- Sleep hygiene checklist: keep the room fully dark (block ambient electronics light, not just windows); optimal sleep temperature ~65°F/18°C (adjusted personally to ~67–68°F); avoid spicy/heavy meals near bedtime; avoid vigorous exercise within 1–2 hours of bed; avoid screens/blue light within an hour of bed (or use blue-light blockers/night mode); alcohol (2+ drinks) measurably degraded his own sleep scores. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC generally, but this specific checklist with numbers is a concrete, importable list)
- Food-as-fuel: aim for a balanced meal (carbs, protein, fat — not fixated on exact macro ratios) within roughly 60–90 minutes post-long-run/workout; separately, prioritize rehydrating to the point of returning to a normal urination pattern, especially in hot/humid conditions after long runs. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — post-workout fueling window)
- Claims a whole-food, minimally-processed diet is associated with lower baseline inflammation, freeing the body to focus its inflammatory/repair response on training-induced damage rather than diet-induced inflammation. (SCIENCE — general claim, no study cited) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Of the "extra" recovery modalities, massage (including self-massage/foam rolling) is singled out as having the best evidence base; other modalities (cryotherapy, float pods) are characterized as unproven, "try and see." Ice baths specifically are flagged as having a shifted scientific consensus in the last 3–5 years — evidence now suggests they may blunt/short-circuit the body's normal training-adaptation response, so he recommends avoiding them except in specific circumstances. (SCIENCE, general claim, no study named) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the common assumption that ice baths are a straightforwardly good recovery tool) (POSSIBLE GAP if the site doesn't already address the ice-bath-blunts-adaptation research)

## Episode #169: Blood Chemistry and Performance
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Natalie Picchetti — practitioner at Health Plus ATX (Austin), holds a master's in functional medicine and human nutrition (University of Western States); former elite 800m/1500m runner (University of Georgia; trained with Rogue Athletic Club; competed internationally for Canada, including the 2008 Olympic Trials). Works alongside her husband, Dr. Noah Moose (chiropractor, prior podcast guest). This segment is a re-aired clip from a members-only training-program episode.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Standard clinical lab reference ranges are constructed by taking a general population sample, computing the mean, and using ±2 standard deviations (covering ~95% of "healthy" people) — these ranges are built to flag disease, not to optimize athletic performance, and vary lab-to-lab with no universal standard. Argues athletes, as a non-representative subpopulation, often need tighter/different target ranges to catch "pre-disease" red flags before they manifest as performance problems or injury. (SCIENCE, asserted by a functional-medicine practitioner, no peer-reviewed citation given) (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly contests the sufficiency of standard "normal" lab ranges for athletes) (POSSIBLE GAP — likely not covered by typical exercise-physiology content)
- Personal narrative: as a collegiate/elite athlete she experienced escalating unexplained fatigue, pain, and nausea with normal-looking bloodwork; at the 2008 Olympic Trials she ran while unknowingly internally bleeding from a ruptured liver hematoma, again with blood values described as normal. Illustrative anecdote for the limits of standard panels — flagged here as narrative context, not independently verifiable. (PRACTICE — cautionary anecdote)
- Anemia is not just an iron-deficiency issue: red blood cells can be undersized OR oversized (macrocytic), and B12/folate deficiency (not iron/ferritin) can cause enlarged red blood cells that don't carry oxygen efficiently — this pattern often goes undetected because cell size may not flag as lab-high even when suboptimal for a distance athlete. Her own case was B12/folate-driven, not iron-driven. (SCIENCE, asserted) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Vitamin D: conventional labs may flag "normal" starting around 30 ng/mL, but the athlete-optimized target discussed here is 50–80 ng/mL, justified by immune-function and recovery benefits; also raises that genetic factors can impair vitamin D absorption, requiring higher-than-typical supplementation to reach target levels. Host's own anecdote: a vitamin D level of 23 (with a genetic absorption issue) was identified as a contributing root cause of a heel/calcaneal stress fracture; supplementing to a "normal" lab value (upper 30s/low 40s) was deemed insufficient by the practitioner, who pushed for 50+. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, asserted numeric targets, not linked to a named study) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Inflammation blood markers named: hs-CRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein) as a general inflammation/tissue-damage marker, and homocysteine, described both as a marker of vascular-damaging inflammation and, when elevated, as a signal of underlying B-vitamin (e.g., B6/B12) deficiency. (SCIENCE, named lab markers) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Low stomach acid / impaired digestion is described as common among athletes and can limit protein assimilation, indirectly impairing recovery — framed as a target for testing/intervention even though it isn't a diagnosable "disease." (SCIENCE, asserted) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Chronically elevated training stress can suppress immune function (linked to elevated stress hormones) and is associated with a higher rate of upper-respiratory infections in athletes; recommends checking immune-related blood markers to catch this trend early. (SCIENCE, general claim; overtraining/immune-suppression link is a fairly established concept) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overtraining syndrome/immune suppression — though the blood-marker diagnostic approach may be a fresh angle) 
- Reactive hypoglycemia in athletes: blood sugar spikes then crashes 2–3 hours after a meal, provoking a stress-hormone response and progressively driving carbohydrate dependence while reducing the body's efficiency at fat oxidation (a disadvantage for endurance performance, since fat yields more sustained energy for long efforts). Suggested interventions: eating every 2–3 hours to prevent big swings, and short-term carbohydrate restriction to "resensitize" and improve fat-burning efficiency. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, asserted mechanism) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Diagnostic methodology point: blood values should never be interpreted in isolation — cross-reference related markers to narrow down a root cause (e.g., small red blood cells → check ferritin/serum iron; if those are normal, check homocysteine to infer a B6/B12 deficiency instead of assuming iron-deficiency anemia by default). (PRACTICE — diagnostic reasoning approach) (POSSIBLE GAP)

## Episode #170: The Mental Side of Injury
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Cindy Kuzma — journalist (Runner's World and other outlets), 22 marathons run, history of multiple running injuries (Achilles tendinopathy, high hamstring tendinopathy, several stress fractures). Carrie Cheadle — sports psychologist; bachelor's in psychology, master's in sports psychology; university professor of sports psychology; former competitive climber/snowboarder/trail runner with her own significant knee-injury recovery history. Co-authors of the book "Rebound: Train Your Mind to Bounce Back Stronger from Sports Injuries" and co-hosts of the "Injured Athletes Club" podcast. Recorded as a live event at Rogue Running, Austin.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Pushes back on the popular claim that endurance performance is "90% mental, 10% physical" — Cheadle argues it's closer to an even split for endurance athletes, using a bicycle-wheel-and-spokes analogy: mental training is one spoke among several (nutrition, hydration, physical training), and a wheel is only "true"/stable if all spokes have roughly equal tension. (DISAGREEMENT — explicit pushback on a commonly repeated stat) (ANALOGY — wheel/spokes)
- Pre-race tactic: write 8–10 personal "race affirmations" (positive statements phrased as already true) while in a calm state beforehand, to use as a psychological trigger/cue when race-day nerves hit. (PRACTICE)
- Second-person self-talk (e.g., "you do hard things" rather than "I will do this") is described as more effective than first-person self-talk, with the claim that recent research supports this. No specific study or researcher is named. (SCIENCE, claim attributed to unnamed "research") (POSSIBLE GAP)
- In-race technique dubbed "RIG" (rhythmic cognitive-behavioral technique): repeating a word or counting footstrikes/steps in a fixed pattern to get through moments of extreme discomfort ("psychology of suffering") when distraction techniques stop working. Distinguished from ordinary distraction because it's used specifically once the body is "yelling" at the athlete to stop. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific named technique)
- Before attributing race-day suffering purely to "mental toughness," check physical/controllable factors first — oxygen, hydration, fueling — since perceived pain/suffering has a strong mind-body feedback loop. (PRACTICE)
- The brain actively allocates effort and protects the body (references the book "Endure" for the underlying model); giving the brain a concrete near-term endpoint (e.g., "run to that tree," then reassess) rather than dwelling on the full remaining distance can reduce perceived effort and anxiety. (SCIENCE — references a specific book without naming the underlying researcher) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site covers the psychobiological/central-governor model of endurance, though this specific application is a useful concrete technique)
- Mantras work better when personally/idiosyncratically meaningful rather than generic — illustrated by Kuzma's mantra "No Deal," derived from a story a race pacer told her: at the point in a race where it hurts and you're tempted to ease off, imagine you're being offered a "deal" (like on the show Deal or No Deal) to stop hurting now — but the "you" at the finish line doesn't want that deal. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- Injury should be reframed as part of a larger athletic story rather than examined "under a microscope"; both authors report that many injured athletes they've interviewed say they wouldn't wish the injury on anyone but are "better athletes because of it." (PRACTICE)
- Injury grief is described as genuinely parallel to bereavement for athletes with a high "athletic identity" — loss of community/social outlet, identity, physical capability, and stress-management outlet — but explicitly non-linear (doesn't resolve stage-by-stage; setbacks can retrigger earlier stages). (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, references general grief-process literature without naming a specific model or study) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Recovery is now your sport": recommends applying the same structured goal-setting and logging behavior used for training to rehab itself — example given of a triathlete (Fiona Ford, author of "Back on Track") who tracked range-of-motion progress the same way she'd tracked power output. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Injured athletes often feel isolated from training friends but should recognize they may need to be the one to proactively reach out (grab coffee, etc.) rather than waiting to be reached out to; also suggests intentionally finding other injured/rehabbing people to do rehab activities alongside (e.g., aqua jogging together). (PRACTICE)
- Curiosity as a coping strategy: adopting a "scientist studying yourself" stance shifts the athlete's sense of control (focus on what can be influenced) and creates emotional distance from the injury, making reactions easier to examine objectively. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Psychological flexibility" — the ability to adapt perception/response to circumstances in the moment — is likened to a fitness marker: just as how quickly heart rate returns to resting after a sprint reflects physical fitness, how quickly you can mentally adjust to changed circumstances reflects "mental fitness." Cheadle names it as one of the book's top ("Level 3"/advanced) mental skills. (ANALOGY/PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Self-compassion (treating yourself as you would an injured friend) is framed as equally important to "hardiness"/toughness during recovery, with research cited generally (no specific study named) as supporting self-compassion's role in the recovery process. (SCIENCE, general claim) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC broadly, since self-compassion in sport psych is fairly mainstream, but the injury-specific framing may be new)
- "Contrasting" technique, attributed to Joe Holder (former college football player, now a well-known Nike trainer): mentally rehearsing both a positive and a negative version of an upcoming stressful event (e.g., a doctor's appointment, or a return-to-run attempt) ahead of time, planning your intended response to each, so you remain composed enough to actually process new information in the moment rather than being flooded by anxiety. (PRACTICE — named technique and source) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Fear of re-injury is described as a normal, protective brain function (not a character flaw) that can persist for years after an injury is physically resolved; recommends breathing techniques plus deliberately building "smaller competencies" incrementally (testing the body in progressively more demanding/unstable conditions) so the brain gets repeated confirmation the body can handle the load. (PRACTICE)
- Host connects this to his own recent nerve injury (from ep. 165): frames it as a genuinely psychosomatic feedback loop where the nervous system had to be repeatedly and gently "convinced" (via nerve glides and other rehab) that the limb was safe before symptoms resolved — ties structured physical rehab directly to breaking a fear/pain feedback loop. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — mind-body feedback loop)
- "Obstacle to opportunity" reframing ladder: shifting self-talk in small increments — "this sucks" → "this is going to be hard" → "this is a challenge" → "where's the opportunity" — is described as a practicable, incremental mindset-shift technique applicable beyond injury to everyday stressors. (PRACTICE)
- Recommends building a written "pre-performance plan" that maps desired emotional/behavioral states across time segments leading up to a race (not just the race itself), specifically to counter the common "I'm either going to PR or DNF" all-or-nothing anticipatory anxiety pattern — the idea being that without an explicit plan for where to direct attention, the brain defaults to habitual (often anxious) patterns. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)

## Episode #173: Bouncing Back from a Bad Race
**Guest(s) and credentials:** None — solo episode with host Chris McClung. (Opening segment on COVID-19/Boston Marathon uncertainty and an International Women's Day tribute segment are time-bound/non-generalizable and are not extracted.)

**Concepts & claims:**
- Proposes a three-phase framework for processing a disappointing race: (1) immediate grieving phase, roughly 3–7 days, during which the athlete should feel all emotions without judgment, journal/write down thoughts for later processing, and explicitly avoid planning, self-analysis, or signing up for a "redemption" race; (2) an analysis phase — reviewing race-day execution (splits vs. plan, pacing errors) separately from uncontrollable external factors (weather, course conditions, start delays) and reviewing training-block consistency, distinguishing genuine training problems from bad luck; (3) a rebuild/re-approach phase before racing again. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — as a named, structured framework with specific day-count ranges, this may go beyond generic "process your emotions" advice)
- Encouragement from others after a bad race should be treated "like a gift" — accept it graciously even if not emotionally ready to internalize it, rather than rejecting or resenting well-wishers; can be explicitly deferred ("I'm not ready to hear that yet, but thank you"). (ANALOGY/PRACTICE)
- Explicitly advises against making wholesale training changes based on one bad race — favors small "tweaks around the margins," and introduces the idea that "training results" and "race results" are related but don't always sync: a genuinely good training block can still produce a bad race outcome (e.g., due to weather), and a bad race doesn't necessarily indict the training plan. Illustrates with his own 2015 Martian Marathon (ran in rising heat, felt like a failure at the time, later recognized in hindsight as possibly his best-executed race given conditions). (PRACTICE/SCIENCE-adjacent reasoning) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "training result vs. race result" distinction as an explicit reframe)
- Cautions against rushing into a "redemption race" too soon — physiological rebuilding after a marathon requires a full new training block; specifically flags signing up for another marathon ~4 weeks later as a common mistake, with somewhat more latitude for shorter distances. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — marathon recovery timelines — partially)
- Pre-redemption-race mental prep: get 1–2 confidence-building "prep races" or strong workouts to create a positive recent memory before the goal race; explicitly name/acknowledge fear rather than suppress it, citing Brené Brown's line that "courage is not the absence of fear" — fear and courage necessarily coexist; stay present-focused (address only the next controllable step, e.g., mile-1 pacing, rather than pre-worrying about mile 20); and "assume the power position" — referencing Kara Goucher's book "Strong" and the broader concept of power poses/a "power uniform" (wearing gear associated with past success) to physically/psychologically access a felt sense of strength before a race. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY, named sources: Brené Brown, Kara Goucher's "Strong") (POSSIBLE GAP for "power posing" pre-race application specifically)
- Uses his own 10-year gap (2004–2014) between marathon PRs to argue that a single result (or lack of one) doesn't reflect the "body of work" — during that window he still set PRs at other distances, built mileage/aerobic foundation, and had legitimate life disruptions (career change, having three children) that affected marathon-specific outcomes without reflecting a lack of overall progress. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #174: Races and Life on Hold... Now What
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Jen Howard-Brown — Rogue Running coach (11 years coaching a satellite group), trained with Rogue as an athlete for ~14 years, has run 21 marathons including all six World Marathon Majors. Most of this episode is COVID-19 business-operations and social-distancing guidance specific to March 2020 and is not extracted; a few generalizable training/motivation concepts are captured below.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Host's personal anecdote: after having his first child, he ran only ~5 times in 10 months; upon resuming, he then trained for roughly 5 months with no goal race on the calendar (deliberately holding off registering) before eventually racing a marathon. He credits this "training block without a race" as one of the most formative periods of his career — it removed taper/peak disruption and let him focus purely on rebuilding consistency and aerobic foundation. (PRACTICE)
- Frames an enforced break from racing (illustrated by COVID cancellations, but generalizable to any unplanned no-race period) as an opportunity to build aerobic base and address chronically neglected weaknesses (strength, mobility, agility) without the ego/pace pressure that comes from training alongside faster partners or chasing a specific goal time. Host's own anecdote: performing NFL combine-style agility drills (as a lost bet) and being struck by how poor his multi-directional agility was after years of exclusively forward-motion running training. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — base building — the "no ego, no ego pressure" framing and agility/mobility gap point are a smaller specific angle)
- Motivation tactics for training through an uncertain/goal-less period: "remember your why" (the deeper reason you run, beyond any single goal race); build in accountability via a specific partner or coach check-in (not just a general training group); use short-horizon (daily/weekly) checklists rather than long-term goal-focus to reduce anxiety when the future timeline is unclear. (PRACTICE)
- Describes an older Rogue Running experimental training protocol ("guinea pig group"): a strict heart-rate-capped run/walk method where athletes ran until hitting a preset (low) heart-rate ceiling, then walked until heart rate dropped by roughly 20 bpm before resuming running, repeated throughout a run for 4–5 months of solo training. The coach credits this block with years of subsequent fitness gains, attributing it to the extended pure-aerobic-base emphasis rather than the specific protocol mechanics. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a specific MAF-style/heart-rate-capped run-walk base-building protocol that may not be covered by generic "easy running builds aerobic base" content)
- Suggests creating short-term "carrots" when goal races are unavailable: informal Strava segment challenges against training partners, or solo time trials (e.g., a 2–3 mile time trial on a track or flat loop) as a low-stakes way to check fitness and stay motivated. (PRACTICE)
