## Episode #398: Taper and Post Race Recovery
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts only — Chris McClung (founder/head coach, Rogue Running, Austin; 22 marathons finished) and James Dodds (Rogue Running coach), working through their own coaching book's chapters on taper and post-race recovery (phases 4 and 5 of their 5-phase training model).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Taper rule of thumb by distance: 3-week taper for marathon, 2 weeks for half, 1 week for 5K/10K. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Practical mileage-reduction method: cut back 1 mile per run per week (outside the long run) during taper, rather than using percentage-based math — simpler and avoids athletes obsessing over precise percentages (e.g., "this is 28% not 25%"). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, simple alternative to percentage tapering)
- It takes ~10 days (sometimes longer) for a hard workout's fitness to be "usable" by the body — cited loosely to "complexity theory" from Professor Neil Theise, though the hosts stress they're using the idea illustratively, not rigorously. (SCIENCE — named researcher, but application is informal) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — tapering/supercompensation)
- Individualizing taper length: James leans toward giving less-experienced/lower-mileage athletes a shorter recovery window and higher-mileage/more experienced athletes more; Chris says he personally performs better with a *shorter* (2-week) marathon taper because he's a high-mileage runner whose body "craves" the volume and gets stale/wonky on a full 3-week taper. (DISAGREEMENT — the two coaches' personal preferences diverge, both stress this is only for experienced athletes to experiment with, not a starting default) (PRACTICE)
- "Extreme taper" exception: if an athlete is sick or injured, prescribe bare-minimum mileage regardless of standard taper volume, prioritizing arriving at the start line healthy over hitting taper-volume targets. Chris used this himself before Houston Marathon 2024 after contracting bronchitis. (PRACTICE)
- Taper workouts deliberately avoid variety — unlike other training phases, where the coaches like mixing things up, taper workouts return to the same familiar handful year after year for psychological comfort. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a contrarian point: variety is usually framed as universally good, but here consistency is deliberately prescribed for the taper specifically)
- Specific marathon taper workout sequence: 3 weeks out = 8K–10K pace work; 2 weeks out = 2x2 miles at race pace; 1 week out = 3x1 mile at race pace (or alternative "sanity workouts" like cruise 400s or a 2-min-on/3-min-off tempo fartlek). (PRACTICE)
- Golf analogy: James's junior golf coaches taught "don't practice 3-foot putts before a round" (fear of missing before you start), but James inverted this — he needed to *see* himself make an easy putt before starting. He applies the same logic to prescribing race-pace work in race week, wanting athletes to feel the rhythm they'll run on race day rather than avoiding it. (ANALOGY) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the conventional taper advice to avoid race-pace-adjacent practice right before a race)
- Final-week workouts explicitly framed as not mattering for fitness — their only purpose is confidence and "keeping the mind from going crazy" ("sanity workout"). (PRACTICE)
- Travel/taper logistics: for destination races, get in short easy runs on arrival days to "flush travel junk" from sitting on planes/cars ("sweating off the plane"), syncing final taper days to travel schedule rather than a rigid formula. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — travel-specific taper advice not commonly covered)
- Strength training during taper: keep the routine/frequency (e.g., twice weekly) but pull back reps/weight/volume; in the final week, swap lifting for mobility/bodyweight work rather than dropping the time slot entirely. (PRACTICE)
- "Taper madness"/"taper crazies" managed via two strategy families: associative (channel anxious energy into productive prep — race plan, nutrition/hydration plan, visualization, logistics) and dissociative (distraction — books, shows, friends, meditation). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports psychology, though this associative/dissociative dichotomy is a specific usable framework)
- Bird-flying-past-the-window meditation metaphor: thoughts/fears are like birds flying through your view — you can attach meaning to them or just let them pass without judgment. (ANALOGY)
- Premeditatio malorum (Stoic concept, named explicitly) — deliberately playing out a fear to its worst-case conclusion ("what if I forgot my shoes, what if it rains, what if someone bumps me") as an anxiety-management tool when simple observation/dissociation isn't working. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — named philosophical concept) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific technique not typically covered on running-focused sites)
- Explicit disagreement with Steve Magnus's "give yourself only 24 hours" rule for processing post-race emotions — Chris thinks 24 hours is too short after 6 months of preparation; feelings may reasonably linger days to a week, with the only hard rule being "don't make major decisions" during that window. (DISAGREEMENT — names Magnus explicitly)
- Post-race physical recovery rule of thumb: roughly 1 day of recovery per mile raced (~3 weeks marathon, ~2 weeks half, ~1 week hard 5K/10K). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Duel in the Sun anecdote: Salazar beat Beardsley by seconds at Boston but was completely wrecked afterward, while Beardsley (who lost) was fresh enough to jog 10 miles easy the next day — used to illustrate how wildly individual recovery need is, independent of race outcome or fitness level. (ANALOGY/story)
- Walking explicitly promoted as an underused, legitimate recovery modality — "smash the paradigm" that walking doesn't count; recommend walking (not running) in the days immediately after a marathon, holding off running until day 3. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — walking-as-recovery is rarely given this much explicit emphasis)
- Cited study (unnamed researchers, referenced only as "a study... on a 100-miler, I think Leadville") found that calorie burn the day after a 100-mile race, while completely stationary, was nearly as high as calories burned during the race itself, because the body was in active repair mode; day two was still elevated above baseline. (SCIENCE — study referenced but not rigorously cited/named) (POSSIBLE GAP — a striking, fact-checkable data point about post-exertion metabolic cost)
- Post-marathon running build-back schedule: 2 running days week 1, 3 days week 2, 4 days week 3, with no quality/speed work reintroduced until after the 3-week window; new baseline is set wherever mileage lands at end of week 3. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #399: The What, Why, and How of Down Weeks
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts only — Chris McClung and James Dodds continuing their book walkthrough (Chapter 14, "the anatomy of the down week").

**Concepts & claims:**
- Down week defined: every 3rd week in Rogue's programming, athlete keeps the same routine (same run days, same activities) but reduces weekly volume ~15–25%, while still doing a (lower-volume) quality workout and long run. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC in general periodization terms, though this specific "every-3rd-week, 15–25%, same routine" prescription is a concrete recipe)
- Rationale for a strict 2-up/1-down cadence (rather than 3-up/1-down or 4-up/1-down): stated as primarily empirical/experiential — deviating from this ratio across their large group-training population has, in their observation, produced more injuries, tied to how they specifically sequence long runs, medium-long runs, quality, strides and strength within the 2 up weeks. No formal study cited. (PRACTICE, explicitly acknowledged as "experiential" not "evidence-backed")
- Reframe: a 3-week (21-day) cycle with 2 long runs approximates the "10-day microcycle" some elite coaches favor (which would yield 2 long runs in 20 days) but fits a normal calendar week structure that most non-elite athletes' lives are built around (jobs, weekends). (PRACTICE/reasoning) (POSSIBLE GAP — a novel bridge between elite 10-day microcycle theory and standard 7-day-week recreational programming)
- During the base/priming phase, the "down week" may not actually reduce volume (athlete may still be building) but is still labeled and treated as a down week mentally/routinely, to establish the cadence habit early. (PRACTICE)
- Six "success factors" for effective down weeks: (1) regular, non-negotiable cadence — don't let athletes self-select down weeks ad hoc, because people are often unable to accurately sense when they need one (their adaptability masks accumulating fatigue); (2) reduce volume primarily from the long run (-25% to -50%) and medium-long run (-~20%), leaving recovery/quality days mostly intact; (3) maintain routine — keep every normal running day and other activities (e.g., strength) in place, just at lower dose; (4) maintain quality but shift to shorter, faster, more-recovery-heavy formats (e.g., 200m-on/200m-off at 5K pace) rather than dropping speed work; (5) reinvest the time saved (from lower volume) intentionally into recovery — naps, massage, foam rolling, home-cooked meals with people, not into a new project or other hard activity; (6) don't introduce anything brand-new during a down week (new workout types, new classes, etc.). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "reinvest saved time into recovery, and specifically don't try anything new" framing is a distinctive, actionable rule not typically spelled out this way)
- Explicit "real talk": down weeks often do NOT feel good while you're in them — you may feel worse, not better, mid-week, because the body is still in rebuild mode; a shorter (e.g., 12-mile) long run in a down week can paradoxically feel like more of a slog than the preceding 20-miler due to lowered mental preparation/expectation. Managing expectations is framed as critical. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a counterintuitive, underappreciated point that could correct athlete expectations)
- "A watched pot never boils" analogy — the benefit of a down week shows up on the other side (days later, into the next up week), not while you're checking in on day 3 or 4 of the down week itself. (ANALOGY)
- Anecdote: former Rogue coach Jeff Knight's rule permitting an occasional full substitution of a walk (or a skipped run) for a scheduled recovery run during a down week, aimed at the "high-achieving, must-be-perfect" athlete personality who otherwise won't give themselves permission to deviate. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #401: 9 Truths about Training Paces
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts only — Chris McClung and James Dodds.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core distinction drawn between training pace and race pace: they are not necessarily the same number, and pace itself is described as merely "a proxy for effort" rather than the actual target — effort is the real input, pace/HR are just ways to triangulate toward it. (PRACTICE/framework) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC in general, but the explicit "pace is an output, not an input" framing is a specific reusable nuance)
- "Karate belt" analogy: progressing from a white belt (needing pace charts, HR monitors) to a black belt runner who can regulate purely by feel/effort without needing any external pace target at all. (ANALOGY)
- Explicit claim that pace is not a definitive measure of success even on easy days — running an easy/long run faster than last time is not inherently "better," and coaches shouldn't let athletes use pace as self-affirmation. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — pushes against a common Strava-driven instinct to treat faster splits as automatic progress)
- "Training is there for development, not affirmation" — direct quote emphasizing this reframe. (PRACTICE)
- Recommended method for determining training paces: triangulate from (1) time trials (a 2-mile time trial specifically recommended as a practical VO2max-style proxy), (2) recent race results, (3) how workouts actually feel at a given pace, and (4) stated goals — with an explicit warning that these are directional, not to be treated with false precision. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — pace calculators/prediction are standard, but "how it feels" as an explicit co-equal input, and the "don't be an accountant about it" caution, add nuance)
- Explicit warning against basing today's training paces on your *goal* race pace rather than your *current* fitness — cites a common failure pattern of an athlete who is currently, e.g., a 3:42 marathoner deciding to "just practice" a sub-3 marathon's 6:52 pace in workouts; described as a common form of overreaching that stalls progress. Framed as deliberately counterintuitive advice: "train where you are to get where you want to go." (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly pushes back on a widespread "rehearse goal pace" approach)
- Star Wars Death Star trench-run "wait for it, wait for it" scene used as an analogy for pace patience — timing your effort progression rather than rushing to goal pace. (ANALOGY)
- Recommends working within a pace range (roughly 5–10 seconds per side of a target, formalized in their system as three adjacent "pace rows" derived from a training-pace chart keyed to a 2-mile time trial or recent 5K) rather than a single pinpoint number — both to reflect physiological reality (effort is achieved across a range) and to give athletes psychological flexibility across weather/condition changes. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific "three-pace-row" operational system is a concrete, reusable tool)
- Adjusting for heat/humidity: claims a humid morning can require a 20–30 second/mile pace adjustment to hold equivalent effort — bigger than most runners intuitively account for. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE-ish — plausible physiologically but no citation given)
- "James Heat Indicator Score" — an informal DIY heuristic of simply adding temperature (°F) + relative humidity (%) to get a comparative "rigor score" (e.g., 85°F + 80% humidity = 165 "rigor," vs. 95°F + 45% humidity = 140 "rigor," implying the ostensibly cooler-sounding but muggier day is actually harder). Not a validated index (unlike WBGT), but presented as a simple mental tool. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a novel, simplified practical heuristic distinct from formal heat-stress indices)
- Steve Magnus quote/idea, named explicitly: "heat and humidity are the poor man's altitude training" — reduced blood flow to working muscles under heat stress mirrors oxygen-deprivation adaptation at altitude. (SCIENCE — named source) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — heat adaptation, but the specific analogy/quote is worth preserving)
- Distinguishes "reasons" from "excuses": heat/humidity-driven pace adjustments are legitimate physiological reasons, not athletes making excuses. (PRACTICE)
- Training paces are explicitly non-linear across a season/career — they can get faster due to fitness gains, but can also legitimately get slower on paper due to seasonal heat adjustments, injury setbacks, or "detrain to retrain" resets entering a new cycle; multiple weeks of consistent data should be gathered before adjusting paces, not single-workout reactions. (PRACTICE)
- Reframing question for athletes fixated on hitting exact splits: "are we trying to win the workout, or win the race?" — i.e., don't optimize for how a workout looks on paper/Strava at the expense of the actual training stimulus needed. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #402: All About that Tempo
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts only — Chris McClung and James Dodds.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Notes that the word "tempo" is used inconsistently across coaching philosophies — some equate it with lactate threshold, and James recounts an athlete using "tempo" the way the word is used in music (any run with an upbeat rhythm), causing a real coaching miscommunication until clarified. (PRACTICE/clarification)
- Rogue's specific definition: tempo is an *effort*, not a pace, sitting in the middle of the continuum between aerobic threshold (AT) and lactate threshold (LT) — a zone where lactate is elevated but the body can still efficiently recycle/reuse it, as opposed to LT itself (on the edge of control) or beyond LT (lactate spirals out of control). (SCIENCE — standard exercise-physiology concept, presented as house framework without a specific citation) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — lactate threshold is standard content, though the explicit "AT-to-LT continuum with tempo as the recycling sweet spot" model is a specific teachable nuance)
- "Fidgety effort/pace" — a named term for the specific psychological discomfort of tempo work: you don't get the relaxed, "forever" feeling of an easy run, nor the satisfying release of an all-out sprint; you're stuck in the controlled middle, which produces restlessness. (ANALOGY/named concept)
- Four named types of tempo workout: (1) **True tempo** — continuous 20–30 min or 3–4 miles, the most commonly prescribed version across coaching philosophies generally; (2) **Broken tempo** — same effort broken into smaller chunks (e.g., 5–10 min segments or 1–2 mile reps) with brief recovery, often used to build toward a true tempo; (3) **Mixed tempo** — combining tempo with faster work, including three named signature workouts: the "Michigan" (alternating road tempo intervals with faster track reps, attributed to legendary University of Michigan track coach Ron Warhurst), the "Julia" (a hard fartlek to generate fatigue, followed by 20–30 min of tempo), and Hills+Tempo; (4) **Long tempo** — 6–10 continuous miles, explicitly reserved for experienced athletes only, described as rare/advanced and "tricky" to use safely. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific named/attributed workout structures, especially the Michigan workout credited to Ron Warhurst, are citable methodology beyond generic "tempo run" content)
- Explicit point that long tempo eligibility is about training *experience*, not being "elite" — some genuinely elite/pro runners could injure themselves attempting a long tempo without having built the specific volume base for it; the hosts frame elite-caliber training methods as accessible to anyone who builds to them gradually. (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the idea that certain workouts should be gatekept purely by athlete caliber/pace)
- Tempo workout placement by training phase: priming phase (broken tempo, submaximal, used to teach/practice the effort itself), aerobic-strength phase (true tempo/mixed tempo as bread-and-butter), race-specific phase (tempo effort maps closely to half-marathon race pace; can be blended with marathon pace for marathoners, or made course-specific — e.g., simulating Newton Hills fatigue for Boston), taper (fartlek-style tempo as a low-stakes "sanity workout"). (PRACTICE)
- Execution guidance: start a tempo effort conservatively (starting closer to aerobic-threshold effort) and gradually work into the target zone rather than starting too hard; "stay present" and self-correct in real time if you overshoot, rather than waiting until the next workout to fix it; the deeper skill is consciously relaxing into the effort (breathing, releasing tension in shoulders/face/arms) so the same pace feels progressively easier over repeated sessions — actual fitness gain shows up later as being able to hit a faster pace at the same effort, not by pushing harder in the moment. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — "relaxation into effort" as a deliberate trainable skill, plus real-time mid-workout self-correction, are underemphasized technique points)
- Group tempo dynamics: running tempo shoulder-to-shoulder with someone of similar ability and syncing rhythm together can make the effort feel easier (a positive "entrainment" effect), in contrast to groups where people subtly race/"half-step" each other and inadvertently drift too fast. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- Named taxonomy for describing threshold-continuum efforts to athletes: aerobic threshold = "comfortably hard" (working but feels sustainable ~forever); tempo = "working, but in control" (not comfortable); lactate threshold = "working, on the edge of control." Also promotes practicing "rhythm mantras" (specific words/phrases used to stay calm and controlled) during these workouts so the same mental tools transfer to race day. (PRACTICE — useful teaching taxonomy; mantra practice is a sports-psych technique) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general mental training, though the specific mantra-practice-in-training-to-transfer-to-race-day framing is a nuance)
- Notes that non-tempo-labeled workouts (e.g., a descending-ladder "1864" workout — 1600m/800m/600m/400m with standing recoveries) can still produce similar lactate-buffering physiological benefits even though they aren't formally called "tempo." (PRACTICE)

## Episode #403: The Art of the Long Run
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts only — Chris McClung and James Dodds.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Opening quote, attributed to coach Bill Squires (a Boston-area coach associated with multiple fast Boston Marathon performances): "the long run puts the tiger in the cat." (ANALOGY, named source) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — long run fundamentals, but this specific quote is a good verbatim addition)
- Three physiological reasons for the long run: (1) **Aerobic capacity** — building larger lung capacity, more/more-efficient red blood cells, expanded capillary beds, more mitochondria; claims a *non-linear* benefit where later miles of a long run build more aerobic capacity than early miles, once the body shifts into a "remodeling" mode after an initial warm-up period. (SCIENCE — asserted, not tied to a specific study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "later miles disproportionately matter" claim is a specific, fact-checkable nuance beyond generic "long runs build aerobic base" messaging); (2) **Neuromuscular resilience** — reframes cramping as primarily a neuromuscular "shut it down" signal (a fight-or-flight-type nervous system response combined with electrolyte imbalance) rather than a purely electrolyte-driven event, describing this as an evolving scientific understanding. (SCIENCE — references "science we're learning," no specific study named) (POSSIBLE GAP — worth fact-checking against current cramping literature; corrects a common misconception if accurate); (3) **Fueling adaptation/metabolic flexibility** — builds efficient fat + carb burning, with a claimed spillover benefit to everyday (non-running) metabolic flexibility. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — fat adaptation)
- Pizza-dough-stretching analogy for gradually expanding the body's physiological "governors" (neuromuscular/aerobic limits) without tearing — directly tied to progressive overload; jumping distance too aggressively "rips the dough." (ANALOGY)
- How long: baseline target of 90 minutes–2 hours as a marker every serious runner should be able to hit weekly, tied to typical glycogen-depletion timing, independent of specific race training. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Marathon-specific claim: recommend 4–6 runs of 20+ miles per training cycle (stated as more than many other programs, which they say typically target only 2–3). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, distinguishing numeric claim vs. common program norms, worth flagging as a differentiator)
- Half-marathon: build long runs to 14–16 miles of over-distance; claims even 5K/10K specialists and elite milers benefit from weekly long runs up to 18 miles. (PRACTICE)
- How fast: "slower than you think" — 1–2 min slower than marathon pace, or 90 sec–2.5 min slower than half-marathon pace; conversational/"can sing lyrics" test; should finish feeling strong, not destroyed. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Warns that going too fast on a long run is easy to do *accidentally* (heat, fatigue, humidity creep) and explicitly lists diagnostic warning signs: finishing beat up/destroyed, heart-rate creep that is exponential/escalating late in the run (vs. a mild expected creep), and bonking (a sign of over-reliance on glycogen rather than fat oxidation). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific bundle of "how do I know I went too fast" diagnostics is actionable and not typically enumerated together)
- Fueling formula for long runs: start taking in fuel around the ~90-minute glycogen-depletion mark ("precede the bonk") — e.g., first gel at ~60–75 minutes for a 2-hour run, then every ~30 minutes after; formerly recommended 35–40 minute intervals for those newer to fueling to reduce GI risk, then progressively shortening the interval as tolerance improves. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — carb fueling during exercise, though the specific timing/"work backward from your duration" method is a usable nuance)
- Hydration figures given: average female sweat loss ~25 oz/hour, average male ~32 oz/hour in warm conditions; recommend replacing ~80% of losses during a marathon/half; also recommend a *daily* baseline electrolyte habit (not just during runs), reasoning that overnight sweat loss during sleep alone creates a daily hydration deficit. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — specific figures given without citation) (POSSIBLE GAP — worth fact-checking the sweat-rate figures; the "daily electrolyte habit, not just in-run" framing extends beyond typical race-day hydration advice)
- Pre-run fueling: 200–300 calories the morning of a long run, easily digestible carbs plus a little fat (example given: a frozen waffle with butter) — framed explicitly as sending the body a metabolic "safety signal" to avoid triggering a stress/flight-or-fight metabolic response before a long effort. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the "pre-fueling as a safety signal to the nervous system" framing is a specific psychophysiological angle not commonly stated this way)
- Long-run workouts ("simulators"/"dress rehearsals"): embedding race-pace segments into long runs on already-tired legs, escalating from simple (e.g., 2–3 miles at marathon pace mid-long-run) to elaborate combinations mixing race pace with faster surges. Names a signature workout, the "McClung Special" (progressive warm-up building to race pace over 4–5 miles, a hard fartlek segment in the middle alternating 5K pace/easy, then a 4–5 mile progressive close). These simulators are also used to rehearse full race-day logistics (gear, shoes, nutrition/hydration plan) and, notably, on courses deliberately chosen to mimic the target race's terrain/elevation profile (named examples: a "CIM Simulator" and "Boston Simulator" route matching early downhills and the Newton Hills). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the terrain/elevation-matched "simulator route" concept, paired with full logistical dress-rehearsal, is a specific and actionable methodology)
- "Church of the Long Run" naming rationale: (1) the communal/accountability aspect of training together, likened to a religious congregation, and (2) the "bread and butter"/fundamentals repetition aspect, likened to a religious sacrament. (ANALOGY)

## Episode #404: Do You Medium Long Run?
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts only — Chris McClung and James Dodds.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Opening reframe: "what's the most important run of the week?" is answered as "the run you struggle completing the most" — for many athletes this is the medium-long run, since long runs and quality workouts carry built-in motivation/scheduling priority (bolded on the calendar, done on weekends), while the medium-long run is an unglamorous midweek run people are more likely to skip or shortchange. Chris shares a personal anecdote of struggling to complete his own Wednesday recovery runs and using an "advent calendar" countdown to hold himself accountable. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — framing the medium-long run as the psychologically neglected "unsung hero" run is a genuinely underserved angle most sites likely don't emphasize)
- Medium-long run defined as the second-longest run of the week; Rogue explicitly argues for this instead of a second quality/speed workout per week, which some other programs prescribe. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT — explicit contrast with two-quality-day-per-week training philosophies) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Framed as the "third leg of the stool" — a secondary aerobic stressor beyond the long run and the quality workout, raising total weekly training stress productively without adding intensity. (PRACTICE)
- Placement rule: must have at least one day of spacing from both the quality day and the long run (never scheduled immediately adjacent to either). (PRACTICE)
- Length guidance: target 50–75% of the long run's distance, ultimately building to 10–14 miles (up to 16 in some cases) for experienced athletes; for beginners, "earmark" a placeholder day even before it's actually a true medium-long run, and grow it gradually, no more than roughly 1 mile of growth per up-week pairing (mirroring the broader 2-up/1-down cadence). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific 50–75%-of-long-run ratio is a distinctive, actionable ratio not commonly stated this way)
- Pace guidance matches the long run: "low and slow," 1–2 min slower than marathon pace or 90 sec–2.5 min slower than half pace; strides are recommended to always follow the medium-long run specifically (recruiting fast-twitch fibers on already-tired legs). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy-pace guidance generally)
- Explicitly defends the show's "run slow" messaging against listener criticism that they "only talk about running slow" — argues easy running is not the goal itself but what unlocks the capacity to do more/better quality work (a polarization argument). (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — direct response to external critics)
- "Extra work"/pickups for advanced athletes once volume has plateaued: 30/60/90-second gentle accelerations embedded within a medium-long run, defined by three explicit criteria — faster than easy pace, progressively faster through the set as the body warms up, and always kept "in control" (not sloppy/max-effort). Framed as something you "earn the right" to add only after maximizing distance/volume at your current level, not as a default add-on. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, actionable, criterion-based pickup protocol)
- Time-vs-distance flexibility: in adverse conditions (e.g., summer heat requiring much slower paces) or time-crunched weeks, recommends targeting a fixed *duration* rather than a fixed mileage, to preserve correct training effort/effect rather than tempting the runner to speed up just to hit a mileage number in limited time. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a practical reframing tool for balancing schedule constraints against training effect)
- Missed-run guidance: explicitly advises against compressing a missed medium-long run right next to a quality day to "catch up" — preserve the hard/easy sequencing even if it means reducing the distance or skipping the run entirely that week. (PRACTICE)
- For chronically time-crunched athletes: offers a self-honesty framework (distinguish "am I really prioritizing this" from "I am genuinely capped by real responsibilities") and suggests creative workarounds like run-commuting to unlock more time on feet. (PRACTICE)

## Episode #405: The Art and Science of Recovery Runs
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts only — Chris McClung and James Dodds.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Stress vs. distress framework, explicitly attributed to Tim Ferriss's *The 4-Hour Workweek*: voluntary, purposeful training stress ("eustress") the athlete chooses to take on, versus involuntary life "distress" (tragedy, relationship/work chaos) outside their control. Total physiological stress load includes both — if life distress rises, training stress (via "pressure release valves") must be dialed back to compensate, even if the running volume stays nominally the same. (SCIENCE-ish/PRACTICE — named source Tim Ferriss) (POSSIBLE GAP — an explicit, actionable framework for factoring life stress into training load decisions; likely underexplored on most running-education sites)
- Simone Biles quote (paraphrased, named source): recovery/taking a break is not a pause from progress but where the progress actually happens. (ANALOGY, named source)
- Three purposes of the recovery run: (1) promote healing via blood flow ("motion is lotion"); (2) still builds aerobic capacity even at very easy effort, "banking miles"; (3) improves running form/economy simply through accumulated repetition/mileage. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — recovery-run purpose generally, though the specific 3-reason breakdown, especially "form/economy from volume," is a nuance)
