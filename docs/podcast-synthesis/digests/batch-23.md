Individual sweat rates can vary 4-5x between people (example given: ~32 oz/hr for an average-size male vs. ~120 oz/hr for a heavy sweater). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — base building/periodization, though the explicit "detrain to retrain" framing is a specific teaching point)
- Foundation/pyramid analogy: "the bigger the foundation, the higher the peak" — argues priming-phase runs matter as much as later peak-phase runs, not less. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Priming phase = two components: (1) establish weekly routine/structure (1 speed day, 1 long run, 1 medium-long run, 2 recovery days) while building volume by 3-4 mi/week max, concentrated on the quality day, medium-long run, and long run; (2) low-stress speed development work to "wake up" fast-twitch fibers safely. Recommends running noticeably easier than normal early on (30-45 sec/mile slower) due to detraining plus rising heat/humidity. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Specific priming-phase speed workout menu (progression-based, full recovery, control over speed): strides (80-100m, divided into build/hold/decelerate thirds, 85-95% peak speed, 4-6 reps after an easy run, full walk recovery); 200/300/400m reps with full recovery, deliberately starting at RPE 5-6/10 and building intensity across reps; timed hill reps (30/60/90-second efforts on a moderate 4-6% grade, easy jog-down, added in progression, not weekly); 30-second or 1-minute fartlek pickups done "by feel" rather than by pace. (PRACTICE, concrete workout templates) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific, actionable workout prescriptions beyond generic "add some speed work")
- "Level up" list — explicitly instructs picking only 2 of the following 5 per training cycle:
  1. Build volume — not just total weekly miles, but specifically add miles to the medium-long run (claimed "exponential"/outsized aerobic benefit in the later miles of a run vs. earlier miles) or add more/longer over-distance long runs (more 20-milers for marathon, or 14-16 mile long runs for half). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — "medium-long run has outsized/nonlinear aerobic benefit" as an explicit claim, and framing it as "the unsung hero" of half/marathon fitness)
  2. Strides for the whole cycle (not just priming), 4-6 reps once weekly, ideally after a medium-long run or recovery run. Uses the analogy "strides are like allergy shots for speed" — one dose does nothing, but consistent doses over months/years produce adaptation. (ANALOGY, memorable) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific framing may be a distinct teaching device worth preserving)
  3. Twice-weekly strength training, started early in the cycle (adaptation reportedly takes ~6 weeks, so it must begin during priming, not later). Gives a minimal starter routine: prisoner squats, pushups, planks, single-leg deadlifts, lunges — 20-30 minutes, 2x/week, bodyweight to start. Cites an unnamed claim that "science tells us it takes six months to establish a consistent workout routine." (SCIENCE — vague, no study named; PRACTICE for the routine itself) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific 5-exercise minimalist starter list is a concrete, actionable addition)
  4. Add or modify speed workouts: emphasizes periodizing speed work deliberately (different focus at different points in the cycle) rather than repeating generic workouts; suggests some runners should cut from 2 speed workouts/week down to 1 (paired with a true medium-long run) for better long-term gains. Uses an engine analogy: easy volume = "adding cylinders" to the aerobic engine, speed work = "fine-tuning" it — argues a big low-revving V6/V8 engine beats an over-tuned 4-cylinder. (ANALOGY; DISAGREEMENT — explicitly frames cutting speed-work frequency as counterintuitive but beneficial) (POSSIBLE GAP — the engine analogy and the "2 workouts → 1" recommendation are a distinctive teaching tool/claim)
  5. Add race-pace work inside long runs, 2-3 times per cycle spaced ~3 weeks apart during the final 8-9 weeks, to practice running goal pace on tired legs; also frames these as dress-rehearsal opportunities for race-day fueling, hydration, and gear. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — long-run pace work and dress-rehearsal framing are fairly standard)

## Episode #318: 5 Misperceptions of 5K-10K Training
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Solo episode — host Chris McClung (coach, Rogue Running) only.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Misperception 1: 5K/10K training takes away from half-marathon/marathon fitness. Claims it's actually additive — raw speed developed at shorter distances raises the pace "ceiling"/asymptote that otherwise limits how comfortably a runner can run near-threshold paces late in a half/full marathon. Recommends at least one dedicated 5K/10K training block per year even for marathon-focused runners, since speed is progressively harder to reclaim the longer a runner stays away from it. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — physiological "ceiling" claim asserted, no study named) (POSSIBLE GAP — the argument that marathoners need periodic short-distance blocks specifically to prevent long-term speed/ceiling erosion may not be covered)
- Reframes "the kick" at the end of a 5K/10K: argues it is not pure top-end speed but the combination of raw speed and aerobic strength/staying power that allows a runner to actually deploy speed late in an aerobic race. (DISAGREEMENT — explicitly pushes back on the common assumption, sometimes reinforced by race commentators, that the kick is just about raw speed) (POSSIBLE GAP — this mechanistic reframing of "the kick")
- Misperception 2: mileage and long runs must be dramatically lower in a 5K/10K training block. Argues weekly volume/structure should stay close to marathon/half-marathon baseline; only the long run shortens (e.g., 12-16 miles instead of 20+ for a marathoner). Cites Nick Willis (multiple-time Olympian, Olympic bronze medalist miler) reportedly doing 18-mile long runs weekly at peak mile training as evidence that even milers benefit from high aerobic volume. (Names a real, identifiable elite athlete, though presented anecdotally rather than as a cited study) (POSSIBLE GAP — the claim that even short-distance specialists need substantial long runs is a distinct point)
- Misperception 3: training structure looks completely different for 5K/10K vs. longer distances. Argues the weekly skeleton (1 speed day, 1 long run, 1 medium-long run, 2 recovery runs) and phase periodization (priming → aerobic strength → race-specific → ~1-week mini-taper) stay the same; only speed-workout content changes. States strides become "absolutely critical" (weekly, 4-6 reps) specifically in 5K/10K blocks. (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — periodization; the "structure is distance-invariant" framing is a specific point)
- Misperception 4: all quality workouts in a 5K/10K block must be at 5K or 10K race pace. Describes specific named "speed endurance" workout templates for the aerobic-strength phase: "The Cluster" (4×400m @5K pace w/60-90s rest → 1-mile repeats @half-marathon pace w/60s rest → 4×400m @5K pace w/60s rest — minimal recovery, blending paces); "The Michigan" (alternating road miles at tempo effort with 800m track repeats at 10K pace, short easy jogs between) — explicitly attributed to Ron Warhurst, longtime University of Michigan distance coach; "1-1 fartlek" (1 min on at 5K effort / 1 min easy, shortening recoveries as it progresses); "30-30" workout for VO2max development (30 sec on at 5K-effort-or-faster / 30 sec "float," not full recovery, within 1-2 mile segments). Race-specific phase adds a "5-3-1" fartlek: 5 min@10K pace/2 min easy, 3 min@5K pace/2 min easy, 1 min faster-than-5K/2 min easy, repeated. Recommends a tune-up 5K race ~3-4 weeks before an A-race 5K/10K. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — named, specific workouts, one attributed to a real, identifiable coach) (POSSIBLE GAP — these named workout templates, especially the Ron Warhurst attribution, are concrete and fact-checkable content likely missing from general site coverage)
- Misperception 5: 5K/10K success requires heavy doses of anaerobic (faster-than-race-pace, e.g., mile/3K/800m pace) training. Argues these are "the riskiest paces we run" and should be used cautiously — only 1-2 sessions max, 3-4 weeks out from race day, since anaerobic fitness doesn't persist and this training is taxing on the musculoskeletal/neuromuscular system. Recommends less experienced runners skip this type of work entirely. (DISAGREEMENT — pushes back on the assumption that faster racing requires proportionally more near-max-speed training; also functions as a safety/risk caveat) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit risk-calibration guidance for anaerobic training intensity, which may be under-covered)
