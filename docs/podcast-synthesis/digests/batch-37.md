## Episode #75: The McMillan Calculator and More with Greg McMillan
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Greg McMillan — exercise physiologist (master's in exercise science), creator of the McMillan Running pace calculator (mcmillanrunning.com), author of *You Only Faster*, former post-collegiate elite group coach (coached Ben Rosario, among others, before Rosario went on to found/coach NAZ Elite), ran collegiately at University of Tennessee during its early-90s NCAA-championship era alongside Todd Williams, under coach Doug Brown; graduate advisor was Russ Pate (multi-time top-10 Boston finisher, Olympic Trials qualifier, PhD under Bowerman-era Oregon program).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core philosophy quote from McMillan's own graduate advisor, Russ Pate: "Exercise science is just going to tell you why the things work that coaches and athletes have already figured out... it will not provide some breakthrough answer we don't already have. It's just: is the right training being applied to the right athlete?" (PRACTICE/SCIENCE hybrid — explicitly framed as a named researcher's view) (POSSIBLE GAP — a useful, quotable framing of the science-vs-art-of-coaching tension)
- McMillan's three core physiological pillars for long-distance (half/full marathon) training: (1) muscular/musculoskeletal durability built through consistent weekly volume and long runs; (2) metabolic/physiological preparation to run efficiently at goal pace for the race duration, including fueling and hydration practice at pace; (3) mental preparation via controlled exposure to race-day suffering so race day isn't a "new experience." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base/fueling likely covered, but the explicit three-pillar framing plus "race day should not be a new experience" is a nice synthesis)
- Genesis and evolution of the McMillan Calculator: began as a personal Excel/paper-binder tool circa late 1990s built for McMillan's own use coaching a wide range of athlete abilities where he lacked intuitive pacing feel; publicly launched ~2001 as an add-on to an article, went viral organically; has undergone roughly five iterations, adding Ultra distances, a "goal fitness vs. current fitness" input (to prevent people from overtraining by inputting goal times as current fitness), and "Speedster" vs. "Endurance Monster" toggle buttons for runner-type bias. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if calculators are already referenced, but the internal design rationale — especially the goal-vs-current-fitness distinction — is a useful specific mechanism)
- Explicit warning about calculator misuse: the calculator's predicted paces are based on CURRENT fitness, not goal fitness, and are calibrated to give "the right amount of stimulus to take a next step," not to already be at goal-race shape; entering a goal time as if it were current fitness leads to overtraining. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — subtle but important operational nuance distinct from "don't run too fast on easy days")
- Calculator accuracy is highly dependent on individual specialization: the further an athlete's target event is from what they've actually trained/raced, the less predictive the calculator becomes (e.g., a high-schooler with fast 400/800 times but no endurance work will look "wrong" at 2-mile predictions; a marathon-only runner with no 5K/10K speed work will look "wrong" at short-distance predictions). McMillan describes this as the calculator "illuminating" a weakness rather than being incorrect. (SCIENCE — study/data-driven claim, McMillan cites ~20 million users' aggregate usage data informing recalibration) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — individual variability in training response, but with a concrete, specific mechanism tied to a named tool)
- "Fast finish" / "close" long runs as a signature Lydiard-lineage workout: ending long runs with 20-45 minutes at faster pace, explicitly framed as serving BOTH a physiological purpose (race-specific fatigue resistance) AND — more importantly per McMillan and the hosts — a psychological/confidence purpose: athletes who begin to look forward to the challenge of the fast finish, treating it "almost like a game," are the ones who handle late-race suffering well. McMillan cites elite marathoner Brett Gotcher lying on the ground recovering after fast finishes at altitude. (PRACTICE, with SCIENCE-adjacent framing since McMillan links it to physiological adaptation but emphasizes the mental component is "just as important") (POSSIBLE GAP — the explicit dual physiological+psychological framing of a fast-finish long run, and the idea that an athlete's own excitement/anticipation toward the workout is itself a readiness signal, is a distinct, actionable coaching heuristic worth flagging even if "long runs with race pace segments" is likely already covered)
- Race-specific goal-pace workouts recommended: continuous marathon-pace runs for marathoners; for half-marathoners, multiple repetitions at goal pace within a long run (e.g., 3x3 miles at half-marathon pace). Must be preceded by base-building "prep work" — citing Lydiard's stated progression: "do the training so you can do the training so you can finally do the training that you need to do to achieve your goal." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — race-pace specific work, though the layered Lydiard quote about training-to-train is a nice explicit statement of periodization logic)
- On coaching individualization: McMillan describes Arthur Lydiard giving "slightly different answers to what appeared to be the same question" from different runners — the mark of a coach reading individual cues rather than applying rigid dogma. Both McMillan and coach Joe Vigil are described as having a rare ability to make an athlete feel like "an N of one" and to motivate people to believe they can do more than they thought. (PRACTICE, ANALOGY — coaching-as-individualized-listening) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if coaching philosophy is covered generally, but this specific Lydiard anecdote is a good illustrative story)
- Belief/confidence as a distinct coaching lever, separate from physical readiness: McMillan states that much of coaching's value is simply serving as "the vessel that says yes, you can do that" — reinforcement the athlete needs because they are anxious, not because they lack objective data. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC)
- Book recommendations from McMillan specifically for further reading: Arthur Lydiard's own writing, Jack Daniels' book (praised for blending science), Jerry Lynch's *The Total Runner* (psychological focus, out of print), Matt Fitzgerald and Steve Magness (praised as good synthesizers of older material), David Martin and Peter Coe's *Better Training for Distance Runners* (heavy science, praised especially for chapters on periodization construction), and Vigil's own book. (PRACTICE — bibliography, not itself a claim, but useful as a citation-mining lead) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site already cites these — worth cross-checking Martin/Coe and Lynch specifically since they may be less commonly cited than Daniels/Lydiard)
- Virtual/online coaching nuance: McMillan notes coaching remotely relies heavily on "reading between the lines" of athlete-reported feedback (e.g., diagnosing a bad race retroactively by reconstructing missed workouts, illness, and disrupted fueling) rather than direct visual/auditory observation of the athlete during the session. ## Episode #77: Training Paces and Goal Times
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest — hosts Chris and Steve only (Rogue Running coaches), answering a listener question (Tracey) about determining training paces vs. goal race paces.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Key terminology distinction drawn out explicitly: "training pace" (what you run in workouts to achieve a physiological adaptation) vs. "race pace" (what you actually execute on race day) are NOT always the same number, even though they're often loosely conflated (e.g., "marathon goal pace" is often really "marathon training pace" until late in a cycle). Race pace can end up faster OR slower than the training pace used throughout the cycle. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this training-pace/race-pace distinction as an explicit conceptual split is a subtle point that's easy to conflate, and may be worth an explicit callout even on sites that already cover pace-based training)
- Pace vs. heart rate as training-effort metrics: pace is used because it's a simpler proxy than heart rate (which requires proper protocol, e.g., Phil Maffetone's method, HR max testing, a monitor) but both are ultimately just proxies for whether an athlete is hitting the correct physiological effort/target. Heart rate monitors are explicitly dismissed as irrelevant for RACING purposes (you can't out-kick a competitor while staring at a HR readout), though potentially useful as a training-only tool. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC if the site covers HR training, but the "training-tool-only, useless-for-racing" framing is a specific practical stance)
- Time-trial protocol for establishing training paces: hosts strongly prefer a 3200m/2-mile time trial over a 5K, citing "the inflection point" — described as the track/lactate-threshold-testing distance where athletes reliably cross into rapid lactate accumulation in physiology-lab pin-prick/VO2 testing — as more physiologically meaningful and more psychologically manageable (a hard 5K requires pacing strategy that's tough for the newly initiated; 8 laps is more "grindable" than 12.5). (SCIENCE claim attributed to unspecified "exercise physiology" literature/terminology, not a named study — flag as an assertion rather than sourced research) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific claim that 3200m is "the" physiologically magic distance for lactate inflection testing is a concrete, checkable claim worth flagging for fact-check)
- "Date pace" defined: the most recent all-out race or time-trial result, used purely as an anchor/starting point — explicitly NOT meant to predict the ultimate race outcome, only to prevent overtraining by giving a conservative floor. Coaches describe having seen large aberrations where an athlete starting from a "3:20 date pace" time trial ultimately ran a 3:05 marathon because the time trial undersold a strength (e.g., an endurance-type/slow-twitch runner underperforms short time trials relative to marathon potential). (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — date pace as a concept, though the emphasis on systematically erring conservative "so no one overtrains" is a clear operational rule)
- "Three-pace range" system (a named, semi-branded Rogue Running technique credited to co-host Steve): instead of a single target pace, give athletes a range spanning from goal pace to two conservative fallback paces (e.g., marathon example: 3:00 / 3:05 / 3:10 finish-time equivalents), scaled to interval length by distance (5-minute windows for marathon-pace segments, 2.5 minutes for half-marathon pace, ~1 minute for 10K pace, ~30 seconds for 5K pace, per rough estimates given). The stated purposes: (1) prevents overtraining/undertraining by giving flexibility for how an athlete feels that day; (2) gives self-diagnostic information over time — consistently hitting the fast end of the range signals readiness to bump the whole range faster; consistently at the slow end signals a more conservative race approach; (3) directly targeted at the psychological problem with pure "effort-based" training, which the host describes as too vague/faith-based ("like reading philosophy... you're pretty sure it's right, you don't really know") for many athletes to trust. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a distinctly practical, exportable pacing-prescription framework, worth flagging as a concrete technique a synthesis pass could adapt)
- Easy-run pace guidance benchmarks given by the host, anchored to marathon training pace (MTP): recovery runs should be at least ~90 seconds/mile slower than MTP (and can be much slower); long runs at least 1-2 minutes/mile slower than MTP; other "medium-long" easy runs 30-90 seconds/mile slower than MTP. The overriding rule for all easy running is conversational effort (can speak in full sentences), with pace numbers offered only as a secondary reference/comfort tool for GPS-focused runners. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general easy-day guidance, though these are notably specific numeric anchors relative to MTP that could sharpen an existing generic "run easy on easy days" section)
- Explicit warning against "cheating" the pace system by inputting goal-fitness numbers as current fitness (paralleled to "people cheating the VDOT system"), described as a common cause of overtraining/injury/burnout. (PRACTICE) (DISAGREEMENT-adjacent — reinforces/duplicates the McMillan-episode point about goal-vs-current fitness inputs; the two episodes agree, so no actual disagreement, but flag as a repeated theme across this batch worth treating as one strong signal rather than two separate ones)
- On self-coached athletes specifically: hosts argue that people training alone (without training partners/groups to socially calibrate "good day vs. bad day") need to lean MORE heavily on hard pace/effort rules rather than pure feel, because they lack the external calibration a training group provides. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific piece of advice for solo/self-coached runners, a likely underserved audience segment)

## Episode #8: Austin Marathon & Half Marathon Race Strategy
**Guest(s) and credentials:** James Dodds — a Rogue Running coach (nicknamed "the Reverend" for his motivational, philosophically inflected mental-training talks), longtime Rogue athlete/employee, has run the Austin Marathon multiple times and paced multiple groups there; joins hosts Chris and Steve for a course-specific race-strategy breakdown of the Austin Marathon/Half Marathon.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Course-sectioning race-plan methodology: break a specific course into distinct segments (6 for the full, 4 for the half) each assigned (a) a specific pacing instruction relative to goal pace and (b) a specific mental "mode"/mindset to adopt, changed section-to-section like "hats you put on and take off." (PRACTICE, ANALOGY: mental modes as changeable "hats") (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a distinctive, transferable race-planning METHOD (pace + mindset per course segment) that goes beyond generic "start slow, negative split" advice and could be presented as a general race-planning technique independent of the specific Austin course)
- Specific pacing prescriptions given (Austin course, illustrative but the STRUCTURE is the transferable part): marathoners start 45-60 sec/mile slower than goal pace in the opening climbing section, working down to 15-20 sec/mile slower by mile 3; on downhill sections, "let gravity do the work" rather than forcing pace, gaining back only 5-10 sec/mile to protect quads from eccentric damage that will be needed late; flat middle miles are for locking into goal pace exactly; a climbing "false flat" section calls for staying within ~5 sec/mile of goal pace and NOT reacting to feeling good by surging early; final downhill miles are for progressively "emptying the tank," moving from 5-10 sec/mile faster up to 20-30 sec/mile faster than goal pace. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general negative-split/even-effort race strategy, though the "quads on downhills — protect them early, use them late" reasoning is a specific, useful, and possibly underemphasized causal mechanism)
- Explicit advice to NOT start with your official target pace group/pacer team, even if their target matches your goal: because certified marathon pacers are trained to run even SPLITS, but a hilly/rolling course requires uneven EFFORT (slower on climbs, faster on descents) to achieve the same finishing time — so starting with your pace group on a non-flat course guarantees you're running the wrong effort profile. Recommends starting 1-2+ pace groups back from goal and using pacers only as a reference, not a companion. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this "don't trust the pace group on a hilly course" mechanism is a specific and non-obvious tactical point, likely underrepresented on a site oriented toward general training physiology rather than race-day execution tactics)
- Mental framework per section, given by coach James Dodds, using named external references: (1) opening miles — adopt calm "Tibetan monk" mindset (citing Sakyong Mipham's *Running with the Mind of Meditation*: "with aggression you may accomplish some things, but with gentleness you can accomplish all things... gentleness is like water"), explicitly distinguishing this "gentleness" from weakness — it requires prior strength/maturity, not passivity; (2) rolling/downhill-to-flat transition miles — "all business," execution-focused, described via a personal cautionary story of pacing off a stranger's shirt (labeled with a faster goal time) and blowing up 8 miles from the Chicago finish, concluding "be a slave to the plan"; (3) climbing miles — "do what you said you would do," a responsibility/discipline framing citing Jocko Willink's *Extreme Ownership* (Navy SEAL hostage-rescue anecdote: plan disruptions don't excuse abandoning the mission) — discomfort here is reframed as NOT yet the "real" suffering/wall, just boredom to be endured; (4) late-flat/enjoyment miles — deliberately "enjoy the journey," give out high-fives, talk to strangers, cites a Rogue athlete (Gabriel Trinidad) who ran Boston interviewing strangers about why they run as one of his most meaningful race experiences; (5) final miles — "know your why," urges athletes to identify a deep personal motivation ("big gaping hole," citing a documentary quote about strongman Eddie Hall) ahead of race day since this can't be processed mid-race. (PRACTICE, ANALOGY — multiple: Tibetan monk/water vs. fire; SEAL hostage rescue; Harry Potter locket/Boggart scene as metaphor for facing personal fears) (POSSIBLE GAP — assigning a DIFFERENT named mental technique/mantra to each specific race segment, rather than one overall mental-prep approach, is a structurally distinct and exportable framework)
- Explicit worked examples contrasting outcomes from starting-pace discipline: "Runner A" (targeting 3:30) ran conservatively (40 sec/mile slow at mile 3, cumulative +1:10 slow by mile 20) then negative-split hard to finish 16 seconds UNDER goal; "Runner B" (targeting 4:10) started 2:45 fast by mile 3 and 5:45 fast by mile 13, faded to still +1:44 fast by mile 20, but ultimately finished 5:39 SLOWER than goal despite still being ahead of pace at mile 20 — illustrating that even a large mile-20 time cushion from early overpacing gets erased and reversed by late-race blowup. (PRACTICE — coach's real athlete data, not a study) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete before/after pacing case studies like this are persuasive and specific in a way generic "don't go out too fast" warnings aren't)
- Notable direct quote on marathon-day decision-making: "You have a decision to make... and that decision happens now, but you also have to reaffirm it on the starting line" — the idea that committing to a smart pacing plan must be actively RE-committed to amid start-line chaos/adrenaline, not just decided once in training.
