## Episode #33: The Alchemy of Running, Science or Magic?
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Kevin Leahy — lawyer by profession, self-described "philosopher," executive/performance coach for individuals in a corporate context, husband of a Rogue athlete and long-time observer of Rogue Running culture (not a runner or exercise scientist himself). Co-hosted throughout by Rogue coaches Chris McClung and Steve Sisson (Rogue Running founders/coaches).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Coaching success is athlete-specific and often not what the athlete initially states — coaches describe spending significant early time uncovering an athlete's true "purpose" before setting goals, because stated goals are frequently borrowed from others or what the athlete thinks the coach wants to hear. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is about intake/onboarding methodology rather than generic goal-setting)
- Steve Sisson: redefines coaching success not as hitting a specific time/result but as "transcendence" — helping an athlete surpass what they believed themselves capable of, which may or may not correlate with the literal race outcome. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- "Ingredients" framework for new athletes: coaches list raw ingredients an athlete brings to training — commitment, coachability, focus, ability to suffer, accountability, staying injury-free, motivation, and needing to see an "end" (a concrete goal) even if the process is what actually matters. Athletes typically don't know their own ingredients; part of the coach's job is surfacing them. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific onboarding/diagnostic framework)
- Steve Sisson's onboarding method for his advanced group: deliberately "ignores" new athletes for roughly two weeks and has them build rapport with existing teammates first, using the team's read of the newcomer (their "continuum of bullshit") as diagnostic information before he engages directly. Explicitly stated as appropriate only for his advanced-level group, not beginners. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Claimed gender difference in coachability: women are described as significantly easier to coach than men because they are more willing to ask for help and set ego aside; the coaches' program is 65-70% women, attributed to this dynamic. - "Aerobic baby/aerobic infant" analogy: nearly all new runners, even if they've been training solo for years, have an "infantile" aerobic system (mitochondria, capillary density, blood oxygen-carrying capacity, lung efficiency) that takes years to mature; prior team-sport or swimming background gives a head start. (ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base development, though the "aerobic baby" framing itself is a strong verbatim-preservable teaching device)

- Two-mile time trial protocol: Rogue requires new athletes to run a standardized 2-mile time trial within 2-3 weeks of starting a program (not required for advanced groups) specifically to establish a physiologically defensible pace basis, even though a coach explicitly states "anybody in the science of marathoning would tell you a two-mile time trial is not indicative" of marathon fitness — it's a deliberately chosen proxy distance, not a true predictor. (PRACTICE, with an explicit self-aware caveat about its scientific limits) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific protocol and its stated rationale)
- Periodization revision based on observing elite athletes: Rogue originally used strict slow-to-fast macrocycle progression, but shifted to include faster-paced neuromuscular loading (5K pace and faster) early in base-phase training, because pure late-cycle-only fast work was causing injuries — the neuromuscular system needs recruitment/loading earlier in a cycle so it's not asked to jump to race pace cold at the end. (SCIENCE — described as observed pattern from elite-level training data, not a named study) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific periodization sequencing nuance beyond standard base-to-peak progression)
- Deliberately "unnecessary" physiologically-justified-by-experience workouts: long runs alternating half-marathon pace/10K pace early, then reintroducing marathon pace late in the same run so athletes practice running on fatigued/"beat up" legs simulating miles 20-22+ of a marathon; and a session of 4x400m all-out with full recovery specifically to induce a rapid, marathon-like lactate/fatigue state in the final rep despite the workout's total volume being tiny (~1 mile of hard running). Framed explicitly as "no book or coach out there" justifying this — purely built from observed practical results, not textbook exercise physiology. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE — explicitly framed as theory-vs-practice divergence) (POSSIBLE GAP — a genuinely specific, unusual workout-design rationale: training the "what will the race require" late-race physiological/psychological state directly, rather than just building fitness generically)

(DISAGREEMENT with assuming lab research generalizes to age-group runners) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Coaching-philosophy split on "the stick vs. the carrot": Steve Sisson deliberately puts athletes into physiological/psychological distress early in a training cycle (when they're more willing to endure it) to diagnose strengths/weaknesses, reserving confidence-building/strength-focused work for later in the cycle — stating "the stick is way more important than the carrot" for most athletes because "the brain gets in the way" of purely physiological effort. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, debatable philosophy about deliberately stressing athletes early rather than building confidence first)

(DISAGREEMENT) (POSSIBLE GAP — this is a genuine, explicit disagreement between two coaches about whether "belief" adds real performance beyond fitness, potentially interesting for a sports-psychology section)
- Coach-athlete communication as an X-to-Y technique: rather than directly telling an athlete they should raise their goal, one coach describes waiting for the athlete to "bring the higher goal to him" so the athlete has ownership/complicity in setting a stretch target, rather than being told what to expect. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP)
- Group/team training environment described as "iron sharpens iron" — much of an athlete's development happens through peer interaction during long runs/workouts (informal peer coaching, camaraderie, modeling), not just coach-athlete one-on-one contact, since coaches get comparatively little direct time with each athlete. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general benefit of group training, though the "iron sharpens iron" framing and the specific point about coaches having very limited 1:1 contact time is a bit more textured)

(PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sports psychology/meaning-of-running, common theme)

## Episode #330: Dreaming of the Marathon with Sarah Perry
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Sarah Perry — everyday/recreational runner and first-year internal medicine resident physician (Tulane, New Orleans), two-time half marathoner, non-elite listener who wrote in with a training question. Coached live on-air by Chris McClung and James Dodds (both Rogue Running coaches).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Direct pushback on VO2 max as the metric recreational runners should chase: coaches state VO2 max has a strong genetic component, is better correlated with 2-3 mile hard-effort performance than half/full marathon performance, and that wrist-based watch VO2 max estimates are unreliable and not something they've ever needed to use to help athletes reach their goals. (DISAGREEMENT with popular VO2-max fixation) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — VO2 max, though the explicit "ignore your watch's VO2 max number" advice is a specific, actionable nuance)
- Direct claim that wrist-based (optical) heart-rate readings are frequently inaccurate, especially in absolute terms, and that when a runner's felt effort/breathing doesn't match a high heart-rate reading, the watch — not the runner's perception — is likely wrong; chest straps are recommended for accuracy. Framed as an "epidemic" of a recurring complaint from athletes. (SCIENCE — asserted from coaching experience, not a cited study) (POSSIBLE GAP — recurring, specific technology-trust issue across multiple episodes in this batch)
- One-quality-day-per-week principle applied even to newer/every-level runners: coaches argue two quality workouts/week (as many app-based plans default to) is suboptimal versus one quality day + a medium-long run + a long run, because aerobic capacity building matters more early on than "fine-tuning" via multiple hard days. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — polarized training, though the specific "most default plans over-prescribe quality days" critique of generic training apps is a pointed, usable claim)
- "Frequency, volume, intensity" as a prioritized hierarchy/levers framework for runners with inconsistent life schedules (e.g., medical residents): commit first to frequency (number of days), then volume (mileage), then intensity (quality workouts) — in that priority order — so that when life constrains time, you drop intensity before volume, and volume before frequency. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — specific triage framework for structuring training under real-world time constraints, useful for coaching content beyond generic "be flexible" advice)
- On sleep-deprived training days: explicit recommendation to prioritize sleep over a run, and if a run/movement is done anyway on very little sleep (e.g., 3 hours), keep it to walking only rather than any run effort, because the body shouldn't be additionally taxed in that state. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific clinical-style rule for the sleep-deprivation/training-load interaction, notable given it comes from coaching a medical resident)
- Recommends specific easy-day pace offsets: easy run pace should be at least 90 seconds/mile slower than recent half-marathon race pace, and recovery-day pace (after a hard day) should be roughly 130 seconds/mile slower or more. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — easy-pace guidance, though these are concrete numeric anchors)
- "Limiter" framework (attributed to James Dodds, referencing Arthur Lydiard's "baking a cake" and Renato Canova's "making soup" analogies): athletic performance depends on multiple ingredients simultaneously — lactate processing ability, raw speed/turnover, connective-tissue/joint durability, oxygen-carrying capacity (aerobic layer), and mental/psychological factors — and coaches must identify which ingredient is the actual "limiter" for a given athlete rather than assuming it's always aerobic capacity or always VO2 max. (PRACTICE, attributing framework to named coaches Lydiard/Canova) (POSSIBLE GAP — a structured "limiters" diagnostic framework, potentially a genuinely different angle from generic aerobic-base content)
- Direct psychological confrontation technique: coach explicitly challenges an athlete's self-description as "not competitive," pointing out the athlete's high-achievement career (medical residency) as evidence of a suppressed competitive/strength identity, and asks the athlete point-blank "do you believe you are strong?" as a technique to make an athlete consciously own a self-concept before training toward it. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific belief-reframing coaching technique tied to identity, distinct from generic goal-setting)
- Language-reframing technique: replacing self-deprecating language ("my unrealistic goal," "I'm slow," "I'm not a runner") with affirming language ("my goal," "there's no slow, only degrees of fast"), citing Bill Bowerman's quote "If you have a body, you're an athlete" as a teaching device. (PRACTICE/quote) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — sports psychology/self-talk, though the specific Bowerman quote and "no slow, only degrees of fast" phrasing are notable verbatim candidates)
- Year-round training-consistency argument against the "sign up for a race, train, stop, repeat" cycle: host describes his own early running years of stopping between races and plateauing as a result, and recommends maintaining a similar weekly skeleton (long run, one quality day, recovery/easy days) year-round even without a race on the calendar, just with reduced specificity/volume in off-cycle periods, because ramping from zero repeatedly is what causes long-term plateaus. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general consistency/base-building advice, though the specific "keep the same weekly skeleton even off-season" framing is a useful nuance)
- Cross-training-distance principle: recommends periodically training specifically for shorter distances (5K/10K) even for marathon-focused runners, because faster short-distance fitness "lowers the asymptote" for what feels comfortable at marathon pace — explicitly stated as "the faster you can run a 5K, the faster you can run a marathon." (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — cross-distance training, a recurring theme in this podcast)

## Episode #331: Break Through a Performance Plateau
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No guest — solo episode with host Chris McClung, coach/owner at Rogue Running.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Defines a true "performance plateau" narrowly: not a single bad race, not even two consecutive bad races if explainable by bad luck/weather, and not being stuck at only one race distance while still PR'ing at others. A genuine plateau requires (a) inability to consistently hit prescribed workout paces/volumes and (b) lack of race improvement across multiple distances, sustained over a 6-18 month window. Introduces the distinction between "training results" and "racing results," which don't always "sing together." (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, somewhat rigorous operational definition of "plateau" that pushes back against runners over-diagnosing themselves)
- Eight causes of plateau, presented as a structured list: (1) not following sound fundamentals — running too fast/too many hard days instead of ~80-85% easy volume; (2) racing/peaking too often — body can only peak 2-3 times/year, and treating every race as an all-out effort produces a flat line of "mini peaks" instead of a rising peak-trough-peak trend; attributes to triathlon coach Alan Couzens (spelled "Cousins" in transcript, corrected later in Ep. 334 as "Couzens") the framework of doing "too many A races, too many B races, and not enough C races"; (3) pressing training paces beyond current fitness — must "train where you are," using a ~20 sec/mile pace range rather than a fixed pace; (4) insufficient recovery/imbalance between training stress and life stress — gives a specific personal example of cutting his own weekly volume by 15-20% (from ~80 to 65-70 miles) to restore balance with sleep/parenting/work demands, and introduces the idea of identifying personal "pressure release valves" (recovery pace, medium-long-run volume, quality-day reps) to flex under life stress; (5) racing the same distance repeatedly, becoming one-dimensional — cites Josh Kerr's 2023 World Championships 1500m gold as partly attributable to having raced a half marathon in his build, giving him "staying power" late in a fast race; (6) nailing training but not racing — attributed to under-practiced race-day mental skills, recommending "C races" as low-stakes practice for executing a race plan and referencing the show's own Episode 150 for mental race-day tactics (mantras, "going fishing" — chasing runners ahead as a pacing tool, neutral counting); (7) grinding without breaks — describes a specific 3-week post-marathon recovery protocol (light running allowed, but no structured workouts for 2 weeks minimum, ideally 3) and recommends periodic multi-month "off-script" phases (e.g., trail racing, triathlon, running trips) for those who've been in continuous structured training for years, citing his own 14-year continuous-training pattern since his son was born; (8) failure to pursue "marginal gains" once aerobic development is essentially maxed out (claimed to take 15-20 years of consistent training) — citing his own recent shift toward heavy, low-rep strength training for explosiveness/power as an example of a late-career marginal-gains lever. (PRACTICE, with several SCIENCE-flavored claims presented as pattern-observation rather than cited studies) (mix of LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC for the general periodization/base-building fundamentals, and POSSIBLE GAP for the structured 8-cause diagnostic framework itself, the A/B/C race categorization, and the specific post-marathon 3-week recovery protocol)

## Episode #334: Running and Longevity

(SCIENCE — attributed to a named source, Peter Attia) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general anti-ageism-in-training message, but explicit citation of Attia and the spiral mechanism is a specific, attributable claim)

**Guest(s) and credentials:** Jeff Knight — exercise/sports scientist (biochemistry graduate degree, transitioned into University of Texas sports science program/human performance lab), former Rogue Running coach and training director (~2010-2015, wrote much of Rogue's core curriculum), later worked in wearables product development at Under Armour (Connected Footwear, Health Box, MapMyRun/Endomondo/MyFitnessPal-era product science) and currently works on wearable sensor technology for diabetic foot-health monitoring (pressure/temperature/accelerometer insoles), also applied in biomechanics research.
