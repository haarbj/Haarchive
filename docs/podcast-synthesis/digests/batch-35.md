## Episode #57: Listener Questions, Round 2
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts Chris McClung (Rogue Running coach, formerly UT Austin women's cross-country assistant coach) and Steve Sisson (Rogue Running co-founder/coach) answer six listener-submitted questions themselves.

**Concepts & claims:**
- For a busy triathlete trying to speed up his 5K split, prioritize the long run (12-14 miles, easy, 7:30-8:00 pace, no fast finishes) above interval/tempo work; his limiter is aerobic endurance/fatigue resistance, not raw speed, since he can already run 6:15 pace off the bike. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — aerobic base/long run priority)
- For triathletes specifically, recommend "brick" workouts (run off the bike at goal race pace for 800m-1mile) to train the transition — legs feeling "wobbly" and heart rate spiking are transition-specific problems that only transition-specific practice fixes. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — triathlon transition training is a different angle than pure running content)
- Coach's rule of thumb for half-marathon training: the race demands "the aerobic strength of a marathoner with the speed of a 10K runner" — build via 18-mile long runs (even for milers — cites Nick Willis doing 18-mile long runs), plus 10K pace work, "critical velocity" (~15K pace), and true half-marathon-pace tempo work. (PRACTICE, "critical velocity" term explicitly credited as borrowed from another coach, name forgotten) (POSSIBLE GAP — "critical velocity" as a distinct named pace zone between 10K and tempo)
- Specific "find your edge" workout for half-marathon racing: alternate blocks of ~5-6 min at 10K pace with ~3-4 min at half-marathon pace (repeat 2-3x), intentionally teaching the athlete to push past their sustainable pace and then recover into goal pace — trains the physical and mental skill of blowing up slightly and clawing back, which happens in every hard half/full. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a named, structured "controlled overreach + recovery" workout for race-simulation, distinct from standard tempo/interval prescriptions)
- Race-day pacer skepticism: "Don't trust the pacers" — pace groups are run by humans who may misjudge pace (author has seen a "faster" pace group running slower than a nominally slower one on course); use a pace group only as a loose reference, never blind trust, and always verify against your own watch/plan. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — concrete pacer-skepticism guidance not typically covered in general training content)
- Coaching trick: for athletes who may not stick to a smart race plan on their own, deliberately assign them to a pace group ~5 minutes slower than their real goal for the first 10 miles as a guardrail, trusting a stranger pacer more than the athlete's own instinct in that scenario. (PRACTICE)
- College recruiting advice for a high-school runner: the athlete (not the parent) must drive the process — contact programs directly; evaluate target programs by looking at 5-10 years of results at the relevant competition level (national/regional/local) and discipline; prioritize whether the coach is the head coach (final say) vs. an assistant (limited power over team decisions like who travels); ask "how do you decide who travels to a meet" as a proxy question for coaching philosophy/fairness; consider long-term-development factors like altitude programs (Adams State, Western State Colorado) for athletes with multi-year development horizons; academics matter because almost no one goes pro in running. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — college-recruiting guidance is a different content category than physiology/training)
- For cultivating a young child's interest in running, don't push running itself — encourage broad aerobic sports (soccer, basketball, lacrosse, ultimate frisbee) for years, then "get out of the way" and let running emerge naturally; host cites his own soccer background as the source of his aerobic base. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — youth cross-training/aerobic-development-through-other-sports angle)
- Race-strategy critique of a listener's real half-marathon result: he started 13+ sec/mile too fast (7:37 first mile vs. ~7:50 goal pace) and faded by nearly a minute/mile in the final three miles — reinforces starting slightly slow and progressing/negative-splitting rather than banking early time. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — pacing strategy)

## Episode #58: The Taper + Post Race Recovery
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts Chris and Steve, drawing on their own coaching/racing experience (Chris recounts his own recent Houston Marathon PR in the intro as a lived example).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Reframing "taper" as "peaking" (term credited to coach Greg McMillan) — avoids the mental image of "getting smaller" and instead frames the phase as building toward a peak. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — tapering, though the reframe itself is a nice teaching point)
- Tapering is highly individual and must be tested/refined race over race — no fixed formula works for everyone, and even a coach's own best taper "evolves" every cycle. (PRACTICE)
- Specific mileage protocols given: beginner/first-time marathoners get a more aggressive, shorter (2-week) taper — 75% of normal volume at 2 weeks out, 50% in race week, keeping the same number of running days but shorter runs. Advanced/experienced marathoners get a 3-week taper: ~85% volume 3 weeks out, 75-85% at 2 weeks out, 60-75% in race week. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — tapering, but the specific percentage-by-experience-level protocol is a concrete addition)
- Alternative, simpler taper heuristic (used to avoid confusing athletes with percentages): cut every run by 1 mile per week for the final 3 weeks, with a floor of 3 miles per run, keeping the same weekly run-day schedule. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete, easy-to-apply taper heuristic)
- Detailed "quality workout" taper sequence used consistently for years: a hard 10K-effort workout ~10 days out (a Thursday), then an easier week, then a "sanity" workout ~5-6 days out (Tuesday) of 2-4x5 minutes starting at marathon goal pace and dropping to half-marathon pace, with ~5 min recovery — explicitly described as providing mental/physical "massage" rather than fitness gain, since physiological adaptation from a workout takes about 10 days to manifest. (PRACTICE, with an asserted-not-sourced physiological claim about the ~10-day adaptation lag) (POSSIBLE GAP — the specific taper-workout sequence and the "10-day adaptation lag" framing for why final workouts don't build fitness)
- Bill Bowerman quote, cited approvingly: "the hay is in the barn" by the final two weeks — final workouts should build confidence, not fitness. (PRACTICE — quote worth preserving)
- Two stated purposes of tapering: (1) physiological recovery/supercompensation from the hardest training block (typically weeks 8-3 out), and (2) psychological rehearsal — deliberately using smaller races/cycles as "test runs" to learn how an athlete personally handles taper anxiety before a longer-term "command performance" race years down the road. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — framing the taper explicitly as a psychological rehearsal mechanism for future big races, not just physical recovery)
- "Taper madness" management: keep the weekly schedule/routine as similar as possible, limit outside life stressors, warn family/co-workers you'll be edgier, "laugh, laugh, laugh" (watch comedies), and critically — do not start anything new during taper (new diet, new activity, home projects). Anecdote: an athlete once spent 6 hours building a garden bed during a Boston taper and threw his back out. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY — memorable cautionary anecdote)
- A described athlete visualization tool: a hand-written turn-by-turn "trip ticket" for the race course (mirrors Rogue's own weekly long-run "trip tick" maps), carried during the race as a pacing/mental cue and reassurance device. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, transferable visualization/navigation tool)
- Final-week prep priorities, in order: sleep (especially the two nights before race night), hydration/electrolytes, and mental prep (visualization, mantras, course rehearsal); keep eating normal and "clean" — full carb-loading protocols only work if done to an extreme starting 5 days out (attributed to a named practitioner, Jeff Knight, not a formal study) and aren't practical for most; avoid a big pasta dinner the night before due to bloating/water retention. (PRACTICE/SCIENCE-adjacent, not citation-backed) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — fueling/carb-loading, general)
- Getting sick during taper is common (attributed partly to the body "dropping" suppressed symptoms once training load eases) and isn't necessarily race-ending — rest, don't panic, and don't rewrite the whole race plan unless illness is prolonged (more than ~2 weeks); anecdote of an athlete who got sick during taper week, rested, and ran better than the coach's own downgraded prediction. (PRACTICE)
- Immediate post-race recovery: eat and drink right away (carbs + protein, "a little happy sauce"/beer, partly for the carbonation to help settle the stomach and burp out discomfort); keep moving rather than sitting/lying down immediately — walking promotes blood flow and flushes waste products, reportedly improving next-day recovery. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — post-race refueling, general)
- Specific named recovery trick (credited to running-store owner Paul Carrozza): for severe post-marathon quad soreness (especially after downhill-heavy courses like Boston or Pikes Peak), walk backwards up a staircase or gradual ramp to eccentrically load the quads and drive blood flow — described as working "like magic" nearly every time. (PRACTICE, attributed to a named individual, not a formal study) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, actionable, somewhat unusual recovery technique)
- Marathon rebuild protocol: 7-10 days of easy running only (max 30 min/day) or complete rest, explicitly to "detrain" mentally and physically before retraining; then gradual rebuild over subsequent weeks at roughly 50% → 75% → 100% of normal weekly volume, repeating any stage as long as needed before advancing; quality/workouts reintroduced no earlier than ~week 3, in reduced form. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — post-marathon recovery, though the specific staged-percentage protocol is a useful concrete addition)
- Important physiological claim: muscle damage/repair continues well after soreness subsides — soreness going away doesn't mean the muscle is fully repaired, which is the reasoning for gradual mileage/quality reintroduction. (SCIENCE, asserted without citation) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — muscle damage/recovery physiology)
- Half-marathon recovery is much faster than marathon recovery — most athletes are back to full training/workouts about a week (6 days) later, versus weeks for a marathon. (PRACTICE)
- Recommend scheduling a post-race massage and a chiropractor/PT "tune-up" appointment near the end of week 1 or into week 2 (not immediately) — gives the body time to reveal issues that surface after the initial adrenaline/pain fades, even if everything feels fine. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general recovery bodywork)

## Episode #59: Purpose in Practice, Part 1
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Lee and Amanda, two members of the hosts' "podcast training group" — recreational marathoners (not professional coaches, scientists, or elite athletes) chosen to workshop their personal "statement of purpose" live with the coaches, as a follow-on to the podcast's earlier mental-training series.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Core methodology (the episode's whole purpose): a written "statement of purpose" should distinguish two separate questions — "why do I run" (general/philosophical) vs. "why do I train" (specific to a goal) — because the goal-specific answer is what an athlete needs to draw on at mile 21-22 of a race when suffering is acute; the philosophical answer alone ("for my family," "for peace") is described as too diffuse to "take the pressure" in that moment. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this two-question framework and the explicit "mile 22 test" for evaluating a purpose statement is a specific, actionable mental-training methodology, distinct from generic "find your why" advice)
- Diagnostic technique: coaches distinguish "proving something to yourself" from "proving something to others" as different underlying motivational structures and use rapid-fire Socratic-style questioning ("what would you tell yourself at mile 22?") to locate which one is actually operative for a given athlete. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific coaching interrogation technique for uncovering true motivation)
- A quoted test for a purpose statement: "If it's real, it can take the pressure" (coach cites this as adapted from a Terence McKenna line heard on another podcast) — the statement must survive real racing pressure, not just read well on paper. (PRACTICE/quote worth preserving)
- Observation that injuries and past setbacks can be reframed as *sources* of motivation rather than pure negatives — "not in spite of my injuries, but because of them" — the disappointment of a setback becomes fuel for wanting to "end around" it next time. (PRACTICE)
- Diagnostic tool: probing an athlete's confidence level as a percentage for a range of possible goal times (e.g., "can you run 2:59:59? 95% confident. 2:53:59? 70%. 2:49:59? 60%.") to find the edge of genuine self-belief versus wishful thinking, without forcing the athlete to publicly commit to a specific number. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a concrete, reusable technique for calibrating goal-setting confidence)
- Paradox noted and discussed: a visibly confident athlete can still hold "prove it to myself" as a genuine purpose — confidence in ability and the drive to see it proven are not mutually exclusive; distinguished from a purpose built on self-doubt. (PRACTICE)
- Statement of purpose is explicitly framed as a "living document" that should be revisited and refined over time (e.g., after a bad workout, after a race), not written once and left static. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports-psychology goal-setting, though the "living document"/iterative refinement framing adds nuance)

## Episode #60: Purpose in Practice, Part 2
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Greg, another member of the hosts' podcast training group — a recreational marathoner with a personal history of addiction (alcohol, overeating) and significant weight loss (~80 lbs) who found running during recovery; not a professional athlete or coach. Episode also includes host wrap-up/synthesis across all three "purpose" interviews (Lee, Amanda, Greg) from episodes 59-60.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Coaching technique: eliminating negation-based language from purpose statements (e.g., "I run to never go back," "to never be complacent") in favor of affirmative, present-tense statements of identity/control ("I am in control") — the reasoning given is that negatively-framed goals keep the athlete oriented around fear of relapse/failure rather than built from a position of already-achieved strength. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific psycholinguistic reframing technique for goal-setting language that could be a genuinely new addition to sports-psychology content)
- Coach's stated (explicitly non-clinical, "boots on the ground" experience-based) view that most addiction issues are fundamentally about control — losing it (disordered eating, substance abuse) or needing to reassert it — and that running/training can become a healthy vehicle for exercising that control. (PRACTICE, explicitly caveated by the coach as not medical/psychological training) (POSSIBLE GAP — connecting addiction-recovery psychology to running motivation; potentially sensitive territory worth careful framing if used)
- Coaching move: distinguishing between an identity an athlete has *already earned* ("beast," "warrior") versus one they're still trying to prove — pushing the athlete to recognize accomplishment already in hand rather than perpetually chasing validation through more results. (PRACTICE)
- Cross-episode synthesis/takeaways from all three purpose conversations (Lee, Amanda, Greg):
  - The exercise is only valuable if actually done — sitting down and writing is step one, and skepticism is fine, but it must be tried in good faith. (PRACTICE)
  - The statement must be shared with someone (coach, partner, friend) to be tested and refined — vulnerability with another person is treated as an essential, not optional, part of the process ("naked" is the word repeatedly used). (PRACTICE)
  - Test: does it hold up mentally at mile 21-22 of a marathon (or equivalent late-race suffering point)? That's the true validity check, more than whether it reads well. (PRACTICE)
  - Should be read aloud and evaluated for whether it produces a genuine physical/visceral reaction, not just an intellectual "yes, that's true." (PRACTICE)
  - Altruistic motivations (inspiring others) and personal/"selfish" motivations (proving something to oneself) are both legitimate; what matters is honesty about which one is real, not choosing the one that sounds better. Analogy used: airplane oxygen masks — "put your own mask on first" — framed as justification for embracing "selfish" motivation as legitimate, even virtuous. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
  - Recommend using formal Socratic questioning (pointing listeners to look up "Socratic questioning" categories — clarifying thinking, challenging assumptions, examining implications) as a self-directed tool for interrogating one's own purpose statement without a coach present. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — explicit pointer to a structured self-questioning framework as a practical mental-training tool)

## Episode #61: The Artistry of Coach-Athlete Communication
**Guest(s) and credentials:** Kevin Leahy, described as a non-runner and the husband of a Rogue Running athlete; a repeat guest host known for using a Socratic-interview style (previously appeared on episode 33). He interviews hosts Chris and Steve about their own coach-to-athlete written communication (emails).

**Concepts & claims:**
- Chris's documented two-tier coach-communication system: a short, logistics-only "X's and O's" email on Tuesdays before Wednesday workouts (what/where/pace/rest), and a longer Thursday email covering long-run details plus training tips, community/inspirational content (a weekly "today's inspiration" link/story), and event reminders — explicitly designed to build athlete "mastery and wisdom" over time, not just relay logistics. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a documented, intentional two-tier written-communication system as a coaching tool for building long-term athlete understanding, a different angle than physiology/training content)
- Steve's contrasting philosophy: he explicitly "distrusts" email as a medium for conveying the deeper/energetic parts of coaching, preferring to keep group emails terse and purely logistical ("X's and O's" — where/what/pace), while reserving longer, more explanatory writing for individual one-on-one emails to specific athletes when something didn't go as planned. He believes the in-person energy of the workout itself ("the magic") can't be replicated in writing for his athletes. (PRACTICE/DISAGREEMENT — a direct, explicit contrast between two coaches on optimal communication modality, useful as a documented disagreement)
- A small but concrete jargon example: the difference between lowercase "m" (meters) and capital "M" (miles) in one coach's shorthand — illustrates how coach-athlete written communication develops specialized shorthand/jargon over years of working together, which must be explicitly taught to avoid serious misunderstanding (and still isn't universally understood by all athletes in the group). (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — small, concrete illustration of a common coaching-communication pitfall)
- Estimated engagement: one coach estimates roughly 50% of athletes fully read his shorter logistics email but only ~15% read his longer, content-rich email in full (intentionally bulleted/numbered so people can skim to relevant sections); the other coach estimates 50-80% read his logistics-focused emails. Presented candidly as unverified estimates, and both coaches state they don't actually require full readership. (PRACTICE, anecdotal)
- Conceptual framing: written coach communication functions like oral storytelling tradition ("stories around a fire") — it's how a training group's shared history, ethos, and identity get transmitted to new members over time, not just a way to convey logistics. (ANALOGY) (POSSIBLE GAP — framing coach communication explicitly as a culture/community-transmission mechanism, an angle a training-content site is unlikely to already cover)
- Workout-writing practice: workouts are planned months ahead in shorthand as part of a "macro cycle," but the exact paces/rest intervals for a specific session are often finalized in real time, informed by a "micro" read of how the group is doing that week/how the prior session went — described as deliberately deferring precise parameters rather than locking them in far in advance. (PRACTICE) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — overlaps general periodization, though the specific practice of holding workout parameters flexible until the last moment based on real-time group feedback is a nuance worth noting)
- Deliberate design choice for a separate "podcast training group" (geographically dispersed, athletes from 5 countries who've never trained together in person): intentionally uses **no coach email at all** — instead, a weekly ~30-minute bonus podcast segment conveys workout details and tips, paired with a private Facebook group where athletes must post questions publicly (rather than DM the coaches) so everyone benefits from the answer and from each other's shared experiences — explicitly designed to build peer vulnerability/community among people who've never met. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a documented alternative remote-coaching community model, relevant to any site thinking about coaching delivery beyond in-person groups)

## Episode #62: Athlete Ownership + Responsibilities
**Guest(s) and credentials:** No outside guest. Hosts Chris and Steve present a structured framework on athlete self-management and responsibility, for both coached and self-coached runners.

**Concepts & claims:**
- Central framing: the athlete is the only person who has complete information about their own training, life, body, and stress — not even the coach has full visibility — so ultimate ownership of results rests with the athlete regardless of coaching status. (PRACTICE)
- Quote from Bev Kearney (credited as a mentor of one host, former University of Texas coach): "A coach cannot create or destroy. They can only enhance or detract." (PRACTICE/quote worth preserving)
- Recap of a five-part "battle plan" framework from the podcast's earlier mental-training series (originally episode 6): love the run (motivation), have a vision of the runner you want to become, have a statement of purpose, and want to win (goal-setting). (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — general sports-psychology goal-setting; this is a recap, not new content)
- "Fundamentals" framework every athlete should understand well enough to make independent in-the-moment decisions: overall volume, plus four categories of intensity — speed/economy work (faster than 3K pace), VO2max-range work (roughly 3K-10K pace), threshold work (roughly 10K-marathon pace), and race-specific work concentrated late in a training cycle. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE, standard exercise-physiology framework, not attributed to a specific study) (LIKELY EXISTING TOPIC — training-zone/periodization content, though the explicit "four fundamentals" categorization is a tidy summary)
- Anecdote: athletes who don't understand *why* a workout is structured a certain way (e.g., unusually long recovery intervals in a VO2max session) may question or under-execute it; the advice given is that a good coach should always be willing to explain the "why," and "if they're not, get a different coach." (PRACTICE)
- Explicit rule of thumb: cap "one percent" training extras (strength work, diet changes, yoga, foam rolling routines, etc.) at 1-2 new things per training cycle — "a few things done consistently is way more powerful than a lot of things done inconsistently." Anecdote: an athlete new to lifting had degraded workout quality for 4-5 weeks before her body adapted and performance recovered, illustrating that new "extras" take real time to pay off and shouldn't be judged too quickly or stacked together. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — this specific "cap new training extras at 1-2 per cycle" heuristic is a concrete, actionable rule not always spelled out explicitly in general training-load guidance)
- Curated reading list for building mastery across five domains: (1) exercise science — Tim Noakes' *The Lore of Running* (called out as the single best all-around running book, covering history/science/training), Jack Daniels' *Daniels' Running Formula*, Pete Pfitzinger's *Advanced Marathoning*; (2) strength training — Jay Dicharry's books, including *Running Rewired*; (3) dynamic flexibility/mobility (also credited to Dicharry); (4) recovery/soft-tissue tools — explicitly recommends tptherapy.com and the "Trigger Point Therapy" YouTube channel for proper foam-rolling/trigger-point technique, contrasted with "aimlessly crunching" a foam roller with no plan; (5) mental training — Carlos Castaneda's early books, *The Warrior's Craft* by Robert Spencer, *Mind Gym*, and Matt Fitzgerald's *How Bad Do You Want It*. (SCIENCE/PRACTICE — literature recommendations) (POSSIBLE GAP — the tptherapy.com/Trigger Point Therapy recommendation for correct self-massage technique is a specific, actionable practical resource that a general site might not include)
- Concrete injury/pain-triage heuristic: see a medical professional if pain exceeds 3/10 on a pain scale, OR if pain increases (rather than decreases) while running, OR if pain persists more than 3-4 days without improving. Frame as "prehab, not rehab" — catching things early keeps the athlete running through minor treatment rather than losing weeks. Also stresses treating the root cause (e.g., a chronic ankle mobility deficit from old soccer sprains) rather than just symptoms. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — a specific, numeric, actionable pain-triage rule of thumb, more concrete than typical general "listen to your body" advice)
- "It takes a village to run a marathon PR" — assembling a team of resources (coach, multiple medical providers, massage therapist, training partners, family) and learning to manage/synthesize potentially conflicting advice from different providers (e.g., PT vs. chiropractor vs. doctor). (PRACTICE)
- Decision framework for whether to push through or abandon a struggling workout: first assess context (sleep, life stress); then determine whether the workout's core purpose is to hit specific paces (abandon/adjust if the target paces are genuinely unattainable that day) versus simply "getting the work done" for race-simulation purposes (e.g., marathon-pace long runs deliberately meant to simulate the unpredictability of race-day fatigue, in which case push through even off pace). Advice to try "one more" rep/interval before fully pulling the plug, and to recognize that no single day's decision meaningfully changes long-term outcomes — consistency across months/years matters far more than any individual choice. (PRACTICE) (POSSIBLE GAP — an explicit decision framework tying a workout's underlying physiological/psychological *intent* to whether an athlete should persist through it or bail)
- Anecdote illustrating that workout delivery/framing matters independent of content: identical workout given to two different training groups — one told the full prescription up front with no negotiation, the other built up incrementally ("let's start with X and see where it goes") — both groups completed the full session despite the different psychological framing. (PRACTICE/ANALOGY)
- "Bring all your weapons to battle" — ensuring all logistical/gear needs (shoes for different workout types, fueling, recovery tools) are prepared in advance so there are no surprises on race or workout day; illustrated with an image of athletes' practiced Saturday-morning routines at the running store. (ANALOGY)
- Recommendation for self-coached athletes to build an informal accountability/sounding-board peer relationship (even a training partner in a different city) to replicate the function of a coach check-in when no coach is available. (PRACTICE)
