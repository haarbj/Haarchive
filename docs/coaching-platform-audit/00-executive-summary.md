# 00 — Executive Summary

**Status:** pre-launch. The private-coaching platform (`/coaching`, ~19,000 lines) has never been applied to production — 27 migrations sit unapplied there, and production has zero coaching tables. Everything in this audit was verified against staging and, for the browser walkthrough, against real (test-account) data on staging. This is a genuine advantage: every recommendation in this audit can be built as a clean re-seed, not a risky data migration.

**What this audit is:** a full investigation of why the platform feels janky, grounded in direct code inspection, a live browser walkthrough (screenshots, not descriptions), a Postgres RLS test run, and targeted competitive research — reconciled against a product blueprint and its own reconciliation memo, both supplied mid-audit. Ten documents in this directory carry the detail; this one stands alone.

---

## The root cause, in one paragraph

A workout's intensity and volume can be expressed six different, mutually exclusive ways — literal distance, literal duration, a percent of the week's target volume, literal pace, a named pace zone, or a percent of a personal fitness estimate — and that vocabulary is independently duplicated across **six database tables** and **fourteen source files**. Eighteen columns were bolted onto the core `scheduled_workouts` table after it was created, across thirteen separate migrations, because there was never one canonical model to extend. The failure mode is not a crash. It's silent: a surface that forgets one of the six modes doesn't error, it just drops the value. This exact mechanism produced four confirmed bugs fixed during this engagement, and it produces the single most serious defect this audit found (below).

## The five most important findings

1. **Publishing a workout can silently strip the exact data that made it a coaching decision.** The one function that publishes a workout never writes the percent-of-pace or rep/rest-range fields — and there is no bulk-publish action anywhere, so every program-assigned workout must pass through that function, one at a time, to ever become visible to the athlete. This was verified live: an athlete's own database has 8 real published workouts at risk of exactly this.

2. **Athletes have no history — confirmed with real data, not a hypothesis.** A test athlete's home screen read "No upcoming workouts published right now" while 8 real published workouts, two already completed, sat in the database, unreachable, because the app only ever queries dates on or after today. There is no athlete calendar, no past-workout view, and no completion log anywhere in the product.

3. **The coaching interface has no shared foundation.** No page in the entire coaching tree shares a layout, navigation, or error handling — the same access check is copy-pasted into twelve separate files. There are thirteen-plus different ways to create or edit a single workout. Closing a workout-editing panel by clicking outside it silently saves whatever was typed. None of this is a matter of taste; each is a specific, named defect with a file and line number in `01-current-architecture.md`.

4. **The coach's calendar does not work on a phone — confirmed with a screenshot.** At a real mobile width, the week view shows two of seven days and clips the third mid-column, with no indication five more days exist. A self-coached user is routed straight to this screen as their primary surface.

5. **The market has already solved this codebase's hardest open questions, and the answers are specific enough to build from.** Percent-of-reference pace targets, which this audit initially suspected might be an overbuilt idea unique to this codebase, turn out to be the market standard: every serious competitor resolves prescriptions against each athlete's own fitness values. Likewise, every serious structured-workout builder models a workout as a **tree** with repeat containers, not the flat step list with a repetition count this codebase uses — a gap hit directly during this engagement, when "2 sets of 6 × 500m" had to be flattened and its set structure discarded. And on publishing: one competitor spent eight years converging on hidden-by-default work released by an explicit, logged, reversible publish event, which is very close to what this product was reaching for and never quite built. **The underlying ideas here are sound. Six independent copies of the same concept is the actual problem.**

## Recommended direction

Not a rewrite. The pure business-logic layer (`src/lib/private-coaching/`, 19 files, ~2,700 lines) is well-factored and well-tested — 1,833 unit/integration tests and 141 Row-Level-Security tests pass cleanly today, none of them touching the actual defects above, because the problems live almost entirely in the route and server-action layer that calls into that logic, not in the logic itself.

The fix is: **one canonical prescription model**, expressed once as a shared type and enforced everywhere instead of reconstructed per table; **explicit, trustworthy publish and revision states** replacing the current implicit, sometimes-silent saves; and **one real page shell** for the coaching product. `05-target-architecture.md` specifies the model; `04-target-product-experience.md` specifies the six coach-facing and six athlete-facing steps it supports; `08-implementation-roadmap.md` sequences a Stage 0 (fix the data-loss defects) before any new surface is built on top of them, then the blueprint's own four-stage rollout — one coach, one athlete, one real week, proven end-to-end, before anything else.

## Highest-priority changes

In order: fix the publish path so it stops dropping data (Stage 0); split "what the coach prescribed" from "what the athlete actually did," which is the change that makes the workout's status trustworthy for the first time; give athletes a real history view; give the coaching product a real page shell and a mobile-usable calendar; only then move to the planning-speed, Strava-evidence, and billing work the blueprint appropriately defers.

## What was actually inspected, tested, and researched — vs. what remains open

**Inspected directly:** the full coaching route tree, schema (36 migrations), state-machine and transaction behavior, existing test coverage. **Tested directly, this session:** the full unit/integration suite (1,833 tests), a full TypeScript compile, lint on the coaching tree, and — after fixing a stale local database from a prior session — the complete 141-test Row-Level-Security suite against a real local Postgres. **Walked in a real browser, this session:** the coach and athlete paths, at desktop and mobile widths, authenticated as real (test) accounts against staging, with screenshots as evidence, not descriptions from memory.

**Researched directly:** the full planned competitive sweep — TrainingPeaks, Final Surge, V.O2, Nolio, Coachbox, Humango, intervals.icu, plus status checks that found Today's Plan shut down in 2024 and CoachNow and Runna to be different product categories, plus interaction references from Garmin Connect, WHOOP, Strava, Linear, Notion, and the Material, Carbon, and Cloudscape design systems. Every claim is labeled as vendor documentation, firsthand help-center behavior, or third-party reporting, and no product behind a login was used. Residual limits are named in `02`'s method note.

**What the research changed, stated plainly because it matters more than the research itself:** two of this audit's recommendations were reversed by evidence that arrived after they were first written. The publication model moved from bulk-publish-a-week, to a visibility horizon, to hidden-by-default-with-a-logged-publish-event. The review surface moved from a roster compliance signal to a simple needs-review list, once it was clear that every dashboard-style implementation surveyed targets rosters an order of magnitude larger than this one. Both reversals are recorded in place in `05` and `04` rather than quietly applied, along with the reasoning that made the earlier positions wrong.

**The strategic finding worth more than any individual fix:** the research identified a genuinely empty space in this market. No platform surveyed combines a real draft/publish workflow with percentage-relative targets — the products with the best publish model have closed target systems, and the products with the best target systems have no draft state at all. None can answer "what did the athlete actually see, and against what fitness estimate," which is the load-bearing question when adjusting from an athlete's own reports. And none links a prescription to an explanation of *why* that number, which this site is uniquely positioned to do because the content library already exists. This product is not behind the market on the things that matter to it. It is ahead on the concepts and behind on the execution.
